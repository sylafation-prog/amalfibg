Caulking and sealants

The joints are where the water gets in. Not the paint, not the stucco, not the roof on most of the buildings we are called to — the thin line of sealant around a window, along a coping, at the change from one material to another. It is the cheapest element on the building and the first one skipped, and when it goes the repair bill lands somewhere else entirely.

Request a joint survey Painting and coatings

The premise

A building leaks at its joints long before it leaks anywhere else

Walls are good at shedding water. Junctions are not, and a building is mostly junctions once you start counting them.

Everything moves

Concrete, stucco, stone, aluminium, steel and timber all expand and contract at different rates, and they are fastened to one another at exactly the points a sealant has to span. A joint on a south elevation can cycle through several millimetres between a February night and an August afternoon. Nothing rigid survives that for long, which is why these are called working joints and why the material in them has to stretch rather than hold.

Water arrives sideways

In a squall the rain is horizontal and it is being pushed. It finds the head of a window, the back of a coping, the gap behind a railing post, and once it is inside the wall it travels until it finds somewhere to come out. That is usually well below and well away from where it went in, which is why so much water damage is diagnosed in the wrong place.

Sealant ages faster than paint

A good exterior coating here might hold eight years. A urethane joint on a sun-facing elevation can be hard and crazed in five, and a cheap acrylic in two. Running both on the same schedule means the joints spend several years failing while the paint still looks perfectly acceptable from the drive.

The failure is never the sealant's cost

A joint is a few dollars a linear foot. What follows a failed one is stucco repair, interior drywall, a wet cavity, corroded fixings or a window that has to come out. The ratio between the preventive cost and the consequential cost is the widest of anything we do, and it is the reason this has its own page rather than a line on another one.

Nobody inspects them

Joints are the one building element that is simultaneously everywhere, essential and invisible. They are not on anyone's list, no trade owns them, and they are not something an owner notices until a ceiling does. On the houses we keep they are walked and recorded, which is the entire difference.

They are also the easy win

Unlike almost everything else on a building, this is correctable from the outside, in sections, without disruption, and on a schedule you choose. A joint renewed in the right year is a day's work. The same joint four years later is a different conversation.

Joint design

Most sealant failures were designed in before anyone opened a tube

A sealant joint is a small piece of engineering with published rules, and in residential work those rules are followed almost by accident.

A working joint is meant to bond to two faces and nothing else. The sealant stretches between them as the building moves, and the shape it is tooled into decides how much movement it can take before it tears. The reference proportion is two to one — the sealant roughly twice as wide as it is deep — which produces an hourglass section thin in the middle and thick at the bonded edges. That is the shape that stretches.

What sets that depth is backer rod: a foam cylinder pushed into the joint to the right depth before any sealant goes in. It does two jobs. It stops the sealant filling the whole cavity, which would be a waste and would set the wrong proportion, and it acts as a bond breaker so the sealant cannot grip the back of the joint.

That second job is the one that matters. When sealant adheres to all three surfaces — both sides and the back — it cannot stretch. The building moves, the sealant has nowhere to go, and it tears, usually straight down the middle. Three-sided adhesion is probably the single most common cause of joint failure in residential construction, and it is entirely the result of gunning sealant into a joint without a rod in it because the rod took an extra hour.

Then there is movement capability. Sealants are classified under ASTM C920 by how far they will stretch and compress — roughly twelve, twenty-five, thirty-five or fifty per cent of joint width, with some silicones rated far higher. A joint that moves more than the sealant in it is rated for will fail on schedule no matter how well it was installed. On a wide expansion joint or a long run of coping, the class is not a detail.

And adhesion is not a given. A great many substrates — porous stucco, some stones, certain metals and coated surfaces — need a sealant primer, and the manufacturer's own literature says so. It is skipped more often than it is used. Where there is any doubt, a field adhesion test to ASTM C1193 takes a few minutes: cure a short length, cut and pull it back, and see whether it releases from the substrate or tears in itself. The answer decides the specification.

What a correct joint has

  • Sound, clean, dry substrate with the old sealant cut out rather than covered
  • A width-to-depth proportion of about two to one, not a cavity filled to the brim
  • Backer rod of the right diameter, set to a consistent depth
  • Bond to two faces only, never to the back of the joint
  • A sealant primer where the substrate or the manufacturer calls for one
  • A movement class matched to how far the joint actually moves
  • A tooled concave face, struck in one pass while the sealant is still wet
  • A non-staining formulation anywhere it meets stone

Chemistry

Four families, and only two of them belong outside

The right sealant is decided by what it has to stick to, how far it must stretch, whether it will be painted and whether it touches stone. Price is the least useful way to choose.

Silicone

The best weathering and the highest movement capability of anything in common use, with excellent adhesion to glass and metal and a service life that can reach twenty years in this climate. It is not paintable, which rules it out wherever the joint has to disappear into a painted elevation, and it attracts airborne dirt along the bead. Its other trap is staining: ordinary silicone can bleed plasticiser into porous stone and leave a permanent dark halo either side of the joint.

Polyurethane

Paintable, tough and abrasion-resistant, which makes it the usual answer for horizontal and trafficked joints — terraces, drives, deck perimeters. Its weakness is ultraviolet. At this latitude a urethane bead on a sun-facing vertical elevation hardens, crazes and loses movement capability considerably faster than the same product would further north, so it is specified where it is protected or where it is painted.

Silyl-terminated hybrids

Often sold as MS polymer or STPe. Paintable like urethane, ultraviolet-stable like silicone, low odour, non-staining on stone, and adheres to most substrates with little or no primer. For the majority of facade and perimeter joints on the buildings we work on, this is the sensible default, and it is what we specify unless something about the joint argues otherwise.

Acrylic latex

Inexpensive, paintable, easy to tool and entirely appropriate for interior trim, where the joint barely moves and never gets wet. Outside it is a false economy: very low movement capability, poor weathering, and a two to three year life before it splits and starts admitting water behind a surface that still looks painted.

Non-staining matters more here

A great deal of what these buildings are faced in is porous and calcareous — marble, travertine, limestone, coral keystone, cast stone. Sealants are tested for stone staining under ASTM C1248, and the test result is published. On any joint touching stone we use a formulation that has passed it, because the alternative is a permanent oily shadow either side of every bead, and no amount of cleaning takes it out.

Compatibility with what is there

New sealant will not reliably bond to cured old sealant, to silicone residue, or to a release agent nobody mentioned. It also has to be compatible with the coating going over it, and with the glazing and gaskets it touches. The old material is cut out and the substrate taken back — which is most of the labour on the job, and the reason a competitive price is almost always a price for skipping it.

The inventory

Where the joints are on a building nobody has surveyed

The first deliverable is not sealant. It is a list, because on most properties no one has ever written down how many joints there are or when any of them was last done.

Vertical and above

  • Window and door perimeters, both the frame-to-wall joint and the glazing bead
  • Control and expansion joints in stucco and masonry
  • Copings, caps and parapet joints — the most consequential and least visited
  • Junctions between unlike materials: stucco to stone, stone to metal, timber to masonry
  • Through-wall flashing terminations and counterflashing
  • Penetrations: vents, hose bibs, light fittings, conduit, camera and speaker mounts
  • Balcony and terrace door thresholds
  • Railing and guard post bases, where water enters and corrodes the embedded fixing

Horizontal and at grade

  • Terrace and deck perimeter joints at the wall
  • Expansion joints in pool decks, drives and large paved areas
  • Coping-to-tile and coping-to-deck joints at the pool
  • Thresholds and sill pans at every exterior opening
  • Dock and seawall cap joints where they meet hardscape
  • Planter and water-feature perimeters
  • Summer kitchen and outdoor counter junctions
  • Movement joints in stone paving, which are usually grouted solid and should not be

Diagnosis

How a joint failed tells you what to do next

There are four ways a sealant joint lets go, and they have four different causes. Replacing like with like without reading which one happened reproduces the failure on the same schedule.

Adhesive failure

The bead has released cleanly from one face and is still intact in itself. You can often lift it with a fingernail and find a clean substrate behind. This is a preparation problem: a dirty, damp or dusty surface, a missing primer, or sealant applied over the residue of the last one. The joint was never going to hold, and the sealant is blameless.

Cohesive failure

The bead is still firmly attached at both edges and has split down its own middle. This is a movement problem: the joint moves further than the sealant's class allows, or the joint is too narrow for the movement it has to take, or the bead was gunned so deep it could not stretch. The fix is a wider joint, a higher class, or both — not more of the same.

Three-sided adhesion

Looks like cohesive failure and is usually mistaken for it. The sealant bonded to the back of the joint as well as the sides, so it had no ability to stretch at all and tore at the first real movement. Cut a section out and you will find sealant adhering to the substrate behind, with no backer rod anywhere. Extremely common, entirely preventable.

Substrate failure

The sealant held and the building did not. The bead comes away with a layer of stucco, render or stone face still attached. This means the substrate was friable or already delaminating when the joint was made, and it points at a repair that has to happen before any sealant goes back. On older lime render it can also mean the previous sealant was stronger than the wall.

Hardening and crazing

No separation yet, but the bead has gone hard, lost its elasticity and developed a fine network of surface cracks. This is ultraviolet degradation and it is the end of service life rather than a defect. It is also the moment to replace, because the joint is no longer accommodating movement and the separation is weeks or months away.

Staining either side

A dark oily halo in the stone running parallel to the joint. Plasticiser has migrated out of a non-compliant sealant into porous masonry. It is effectively permanent, poulticing rarely recovers it fully, and the only real answer is to not let it happen — which is a specification decision made before anyone bought material.

Buildings

Condominiums, milestone inspections and the facade

Florida's structural inspection regime has put a great deal of deferred facade work in front of boards at once, and joint sealant is on almost every list that comes back.

A milestone inspection looks at the building envelope and the structure behind it, and sealant is where those two meet. Open joints at copings, parapets, windows and balcony slab edges are the route by which water reaches embedded reinforcement, and chloride-driven corrosion of that reinforcement is precisely what the inspection regime exists to catch. A report that lists spalling almost always lists sealant alongside it, because one caused the other.

The work is also, usefully, among the least disruptive items on a board's list. It does not require shoring, it does not displace residents, it can be phased by elevation across budget years, and it produces a documented, photographed, warrantable record of what was done and when. For a board assembling a structural integrity reserve study, a sealant programme with a written cycle is far easier to defend than an unscheduled large repair.

We write the joint survey as a quantified document: linear feet by joint type and elevation, the condition of each run, which failure mode is present, what each run needs, and what it will cost. That is a thing a board can read, compare and approve. It is also a thing an engineer can check, which matters when the work is being done in response to a report rather than ahead of one.

Where the building is above three storeys there is a licensing point worth knowing. The state preemption that removed local licensing from these trades specifically allows a local government to keep licensing rooftop painting, coating and cleaning above three storeys, and the access itself — swing stage, boom, rigging — brings its own requirements. We establish both before quoting rather than after.

What a board receives

  • A quantified joint survey: linear feet by type, elevation and condition
  • Each run classified by failure mode, with photographs
  • The specification: substrate preparation, backer rod, primer, product and class
  • Phasing options by elevation, costed, so the work can span budget years
  • Adhesion test results where the substrate warranted testing
  • A completion record with batch numbers and product data sheets
  • The manufacturer's warranty and what it does and does not cover
  • A renewal schedule by elevation for the reserve study

The standard

How the work runs

Most of the cost of doing this correctly is in the first three steps, which is exactly why a cheaper quote is cheaper.

  1. Survey and quantifyEvery joint walked, classified by type and location, measured in linear feet, photographed and assessed for how it failed. Substrates identified. Movement estimated from joint width and material. Existing sealant identified where it matters, because what is there decides what will bond to it.
  2. Test where it mattersOn questionable substrates, a field adhesion test to ASTM C1193: a short length of the proposed sealant installed, cured, then cut and pulled back by hand. Adhesive release means a primer or a different product. It costs an afternoon and it is the difference between a ten-year joint and a two-year one.
  3. SpecifyProduct, movement class, primer, backer rod type and diameter, joint dimensions, and the non-staining requirement anywhere the joint meets stone. Written down, priced against the survey, and handed over as the document the work is held to.
  4. RemoveThe old sealant cut out entirely, not skinned over. Both faces taken back to sound, clean substrate. Residue, release agents and old primer removed. This is the majority of the labour and the step that is quietly omitted when a price looks good.
  5. PrepareJoints cleaned and dried. Any friable or delaminated substrate repaired and allowed to cure before sealant goes near it. Masking set where a crisp line matters. Primer applied where specified, within its working window.
  6. Rod and toolBacker rod of the correct diameter installed to a consistent depth, unpunctured. Sealant gunned in a single continuous pass with no voids, then tooled concave in one stroke while wet. Tooling is what forces the sealant against both faces and creates the bond; a bead left untooled is decorative.
  7. Cure and verifySealants cure on their own schedule and most are affected by humidity, which here is a help as often as a hindrance. Joints are left alone through cure, then walked again: continuity, adhesion, tooling, and the stone either side checked for any sign of migration.
  8. RecordLinear feet by elevation, products and batch numbers, photographs before and after, the adhesion test results, and the date the next renewal falls due by elevation. Filed, and yours.

On a cycle

Joints have their own clock, and it is not the painting clock

The most useful thing we can tell an owner about sealant is when it is going to need doing, by elevation, before anything has gone wrong.

Service life here depends on chemistry, exposure and movement, and the spread is wide: a quality silicone on a protected elevation can run close to twenty years, while a urethane bead facing west may be finished in five. An acrylic outside is finished almost immediately. Averaging those into a single number for the building is how joints get missed.

On houses under the estate calendar the joints are walked and photographed on the monthly exterior condition walk. Hardening, crazing and the first adhesive separations show long before water does, and a run that is turning gets renewed in its own year rather than as part of a general panic. Because we hold the product and batch record, the replacement matches.

That turns the whole subject from an emergency into a line item. It also protects the coating programme: paint applied over joints that are about to fail buys a year and then has to be cut and made good, which is the most annoying way to spend money on a house.

On a building rather than a house, the same logic runs through the reserve study. A renewal schedule by elevation lets a board fund sealant as planned maintenance instead of finding it inside a structural repair, where it is both more expensive and considerably less voluntary.

Under the estate programme

  • Joints walked and photographed monthly from fixed positions
  • Hardening, crazing and early separation flagged before water appears
  • Runs renewed by elevation in their own year rather than all at once
  • Product, class and batch record held so replacements match
  • Coating and sealant cycles coordinated rather than colliding
  • Pool coping, threshold and terrace joints on their own shorter cycle
  • Renewal windows forecast by elevation and budgeted ahead

The line

Where caulking ends

Caulking is a named exempt job scope under Florida's contracting statute and requires no trade licence. Some of what sits next to it on a building does.

Ours

  • All exterior and interior joint sealant: removal, preparation, priming, rodding and installation
  • Window and door perimeter sealing, and glazing bead renewal
  • Expansion and control joint renewal in stucco, masonry and hardscape
  • Coping, cap, parapet and through-wall termination joints
  • Non-staining systems against marble, travertine, limestone and keystone
  • Threshold, sill and penetration sealing
  • Pool coping and deck perimeter joints
  • Quantified joint surveys, adhesion testing and specifications
  • Associated stucco patching and repainting of the joint line

Not ours, and why

  • Window and door replacement — permitted work, and impact units in the high-velocity zone doubly so
  • Roofing and roof membrane terminations, including anything that keeps water out of the roof assembly
  • Through-wall flashing replacement, which is construction rather than sealing
  • Structural repair of spalled concrete and corroded reinforcement — engineer first, licensed contractor after
  • Glazing replacement and structural glazing
  • Rooftop work above three storeys where the municipality still licenses it
  • Anything requiring a permit beyond the exempt scopes

Where a job needs any of the second column, the licensed trade contracts directly with you, as on every project we run. Florida's contracting statute draws no distinction between performing licensed work yourself and having it performed by others under your contract, which is why it does not enter ours.

Questions

What owners ask

How often does caulking need replacing in Florida?

It depends on the product and the exposure, and the honest range is wide. A quality silicone on a sheltered elevation can run fifteen to twenty years here. A polyurethane bead on a west-facing wall in full sun may be hard and crazed at five. An acrylic latex used outside is often finished in two or three. That spread is why we forecast by elevation rather than quoting a single figure for a building, and why the first thing we produce is a survey rather than a price.

Do you need a licence to caulk in Florida?

No. Caulking is named explicitly in Fla. Stat. 489.117(4)(a) as a job scope that does not correspond to a state contractor category, and the statute also prevents a local government from requiring a licence to obtain a permit for it. A business tax receipt and current insurance still apply, and work above three storeys can still be locally licensed in some municipalities. The licensing questions that matter on this kind of job attach to the work next to it, which is set out above.

My caulking looks fine but we still have a leak. Is it the joints?

Possibly, and a visual check will not settle it. Sealant fails from the back as often as the front: a bead can look continuous and well tooled while having released from one face entirely behind the visible surface. It can also have gone hard, which admits water through movement cracks too fine to see from the ground. Water also travels — the entry point is routinely several feet above and to one side of where it appears inside. A joint survey, with sections cut where the evidence points, is how that question gets answered.

Can you just caulk over the old caulking?

It is what a competitive price usually assumes, and it does not hold. New sealant does not reliably bond to cured sealant, and it certainly does not bond to silicone residue. More importantly, covering the old bead leaves whatever caused it to fail exactly where it was, and buries the backer rod question entirely. The removal is most of the labour on this work. A quote that is substantially cheaper than the others is almost always a quote that has left it out.

Why does the caulking around my marble have dark marks beside it?

Plasticiser migration. An ordinary silicone has bled oils into the stone either side of the bead, and because marble, travertine, limestone and keystone are porous and calcareous, the oil has gone in rather than sitting on top. It is effectively permanent; poulticing sometimes lightens it and rarely clears it. Sealants are tested for this under ASTM C1248 and the compliant ones are published as non-staining. It is entirely a specification failure, decided before anyone bought the material.

What is backer rod and does it really matter?

It is a foam cylinder set into the joint before the sealant, and it is the most important component in a joint that nobody sees. It controls the depth so the bead is roughly twice as wide as it is deep, which is the proportion that stretches, and it stops the sealant bonding to the back of the joint. Sealant stuck to all three surfaces cannot move at all, so it tears down the middle at the first real thermal cycle. Omitting the rod saves an hour and costs the joint.

Our condo had a milestone inspection and sealant is on the report. What now?

A quantified survey comes first: linear feet by elevation and joint type, the condition and failure mode of each run, and the specification each one needs. That document is what lets a board compare bids on the same basis and lets the engineer confirm the remedy matches the finding. The work phases well by elevation across budget years, it does not displace residents, and it produces a warrantable record. We are happy to work alongside the engineer of record rather than around them.

Will the new caulking be paintable?

If the joint needs to disappear into a painted elevation, yes — we specify a paintable hybrid or urethane and the coating goes over it after cure. Silicone is not paintable and we do not use it where paint has to cover it, whatever its other merits. Where a joint is against glass, metal or anodised aluminium and will remain exposed, silicone is usually the right answer and the colour is selected rather than painted.
