Mechanical · Hotels

Chilled water or VRF for a South Florida hotel

Most of what is written on this question is a manufacturer's brochure with the logo removed. An owner facing a plant replacement deserves the arithmetic, which says neither system wins on its own and sets out what decides it.

Published 30 October 2026 · reviewed against primary sources

The crossover

Where does the crossover between chilled water and VRF actually sit?

Below roughly 150 tons of cooling, VRF nearly always wins; above roughly 450 tons, a chilled-water plant nearly always wins; and between those marks, which covers most hotels of 80 to 200 keys, either answer is defensible and the decision turns on the building rather than the technology.

The small end is easy: a sixty-key property has no business running a cooling tower, a water-treatment programme and a machine room it must staff and ventilate. The large end is also easy. A full-service hotel of 250 keys with a ballroom, a kitchen line and a laundry carries large, lumpy loads that a central plant absorbs and a field of outdoor units does not. Refrigerant piping has physical limits — most manufacturers cap the longest run at around 165 metres and the height between outdoor and indoor units at somewhere between 50 and 110 metres depending on brand and arrangement — so a tall tower becomes several independent VRF systems.

The middle is where the honest answer is "it depends", and the dependencies are these: whether the building already has chilled-water piping and fan coils in the walls, how much roof there is, whether the property has an engineer on staff, what the brand standard requires in the guest room, and how long the owner intends to hold the asset. Those five facts settle it far more often than an efficiency rating does.

First cost and life cost

What does each system cost to install and to run over twenty years?

On published 2026 ranges a complete VRF installation costs roughly a fifth to a third less than a complete water-cooled chilled-water system, and over twenty years that gap is only partly recovered by the plant's lower running cost — so the money alone rarely decides it.

A worked example

What does the twenty-year arithmetic look like for a real-sized hotel?

For a hypothetical 220-key oceanfront hotel of 200,000 square feet and 500 tons, VRF comes out roughly twelve per cent cheaper over twenty years on mid-range inputs — a margin that sits inside the uncertainty of those inputs, which is the finding that matters.

This is an illustration, not a quotation, and every input is stated so it can be replaced with a bid, a metered ton-hour history and the local tariff. The property is ten storeys with a restaurant, a ballroom and a laundry, built new or gutted to the slab so both systems start from nothing. Design load is 500 tons at 400 square feet per ton, and the plant is assumed to deliver 3,500 equivalent full-load hours a year — an assumption for a building cooling around the clock, not a measurement — or 1.75 million ton-hours. Electricity is a blended eleven cents per kilowatt-hour; Florida Power & Light's October 2026 general-service demand tariff bills $12.70 per kilowatt of demand plus roughly six cents per kilowatt-hour in energy and fuel, and a hotel's blended rate lands near that.

Energy intensity is 0.85 kW per ton, annual average, for a well-designed variable-speed plant including tower fans, pumps and fan-coil fans, and 1.0 kW per ton for VRF including indoor fans — a deliberate derating of rated IEER figures for roof ambient above 90°F most of the year and long piping. The two are close on purpose: in a climate with almost no mild weather, VRF loses the part-load advantage behind its laboratory ratings, and a water-cooled plant gains less from its tower than in a dry climate because Miami's design wet-bulb is high.

Three things in that table deserve a plain reading. The operating lines are almost identical: the plant's energy advantage of about $29,000 a year is cancelled by its water bill, which most comparisons leave out. The entire difference is first cost, the number that varies most between bids — VRF at $36 a square foot against chilled water at $40 flips the result. And the horizon is doing quiet work: at twenty years the plant still holds piping, fan coils and chillers with life in them, and at twenty-five the columns converge.

One case changes the arithmetic entirely. A hotel that already has sound chilled-water piping and fan coils is choosing between replacing a plant and demolishing a distribution network while it trades, and there the plant replacement is usually the only sensible bid.

This coast

Why does South Florida's climate change the answer?

Because the climate removes VRF's best argument, heat recovery, and sharpens its worst weakness, humidity at part load, while adding two hazards — salt and wind — that fall hardest on equipment left out on a roof.

Heat recovery has little to recover

Heat-recovery VRF earns its premium where a building heats one face while cooling another. A hotel on this coast heats on a handful of January mornings, and strip heat in a fan coil covers that for a few dollars a room. The one genuine use of recovered heat here is reheat for dehumidification in the outdoor-air unit, which either system can provide.

Humidity at part load

A VRF indoor unit at low load runs a warm coil and a high sensible heat ratio: it cools the air without drying it. A guest room at night in August, set at 72°F with the bathroom fan pulling in humid corridor air, is exactly that condition, and relative humidity above 65 per cent for days on end is how mould complaints begin. A fan coil fed with 44°F water keeps its coil below dew point whenever the valve is open. ASHRAE 62.1 sets the outdoor-air rate and requires indoor humidity held at or below 65 per cent; neither system meets that alone, and both depend on a dedicated outdoor-air unit delivering air at a dew point near 50°F. A chilled-water DOAS with a deep coil and reheat is a mature assembly; VRF-connected outdoor-air units work, but their capacity is limited and their humidity control usually needs a third-party controller.

Salt and wind on the roof

Chloride eats condenser fins from the inside, and equipment on a beachfront roof fails years earlier than inland. VRF puts every one of its thirty condensers in that air; a chilled-water plant keeps its chillers indoors and leaves only a fibreglass or stainless tower outside. The Florida Building Code's 8th Edition adopted ASCE 7-22 wind loads, and in the high-velocity hurricane zone of Miami-Dade and Broward every roof-mounted unit needs engineered anchorage and product approval: thirty VRF units are thirty anchorage details and thirty exposures to debris. VRF answers with standby power. A plant wants a generator sized for a chiller's inrush or it does not run; VRF can be sectioned so a small generator carries the lobby, the kitchen and a block of rooms.

Operations

Who can service each system, and what happens when a VRF line is discontinued?

A chilled-water plant can be serviced by any competent chiller mechanic with parts from any supplier, while VRF can be serviced only with its manufacturer's tools and parts, and when the manufacturer retires the line the outdoor units, indoor units and controls are orphaned together.

Chilled water is an open market: factory and independent service on this coast, commodity pumps, towers and fan coils, and controls that speak standard protocols. VRF is closed by design: each manufacturer's outdoor units, indoor units, branch selectors and controller work only with one another, and third-party controls reach the system through a gateway that exposes a few points. That is not a reason to avoid VRF; it is a reason to buy from a manufacturer with a deep local distributor and to write the service agreement before the system is ordered.

Discontinuation is the risk that is hardest to price, and it is happening now. Every R-410A VRF line is being withdrawn in favour of R-32 or R-454B equipment, and a new-refrigerant outdoor unit is not, as a rule, a drop-in replacement for an old-refrigerant indoor unit. An owner whose twelve-year-old outdoor unit fails in 2031 may find the compatible replacement no longer exists and the honest repair is a new system. A chiller plant makes the same transition in one machine in one room.

Refrigerant

How do the refrigerant rules and the A2L transition change both options?

Under the EPA's Technology Transitions rule issued under the AIM Act, new comfort-cooling chillers have been limited to refrigerants below 700 GWP since 1 January 2025, and VRF systems manufactured from 1 January 2026 face the same limit, with R-410A VRF stock installable only until 1 January 2027 — so a system bought today is on the new refrigerants either way, and for VRF that means a mildly flammable one.

For the chiller the change is nearly invisible: the replacements for R-134a — R-513A, R-1233zd(E), R-514A and, in some lines, R-1234ze — are non-flammable or sit in a machine room already built to ASHRAE 15.

For VRF the change reaches into every guest room. R-32 and R-454B are A2L refrigerants, classed as mildly flammable, and ASHRAE 15-2022 — which the Florida Building Code Mechanical now references for A2L systems through its 2024 supplement to the 8th Edition — limits how much refrigerant may be released into a given room volume. For a small guest room on a long piping run that limit can be below the charge in the piping alone, and the fixes are refrigerant sensors and shut-off valves in the ceiling, relocated branch selectors or redesigned piping: each an added cost, point of failure and maintenance item, and together the reason a VRF bid on the new refrigerant is not the bid of three years ago.

One warning on timing. Discounted pre-2026 R-410A VRF stock is a system whose refrigerant the AIM Act cuts to 30 per cent of baseline production in 2029, with a service life running well past that date; judge the discount against the future price of its refrigerant.

Guests

Which system gives the better guest experience?

Neither, by much: both give every room its own thermostat, and the differences a guest can feel are recovery after a room has sat empty, humidity on a still night, and the noise of what is on the roof.

Where VRF is better

  • Fine modulation holds a setpoint within a fraction of a degree
  • No water in the ceiling: a leak is refrigerant, not a flooded room below
  • Heat recovery can heat one exposure while cooling another

Where chilled water is better

  • A cold coil dries the air at any load, so a room left at setback recovers without the clammy hour
  • One tower on the roof, not thirty fans, above the top-floor suites and the neighbours
  • A room failure is a valve or a fan fixed from stock, not a circuit down for a wing

The conclusion

So which should a South Florida hotel owner choose?

Choose VRF for the small or mid-sized property with no existing chilled-water piping, a short hold and no engineer on staff; choose a chilled-water plant for the large or tall hotel, the beachfront, the long hold, the building that already has pipes in the walls, and any owner whose first fear is a humidity complaint.

  1. VRF winsUnder about 150 tons, or a select-service hotel of up to roughly 120 keys on three or four floors. New build or gut renovation. An owner who will sell within ten years. Sites a mile or more from the water. Properties that must run partly on a small generator.
  2. Chilled water winsOver about 450 tons, or a full-service hotel of 200 keys and up with a ballroom, kitchen line and laundry. Any tower whose height strains refrigerant piping limits. Any hotel with sound chilled-water distribution in place. Oceanfront roofs. Owners who hold for twenty years and want an open service market.
  3. Either is defensibleBetween 150 and 450 tons with no existing piping. Decide on two bids with the twenty-year table filled in from each, and on the five building facts at the top of the page. Water-cooled VRF — outdoor units indoors on a condenser-water loop — is sometimes the right hybrid on the beach. Whichever is chosen, the outdoor-air unit decides whether the rooms are dry; design it first.

Questions

What hotel owners ask about chilled water and VRF

Three questions arrive before any other; the short answers are below.

Is VRF cheaper than a chiller plant for a hotel?

To install, usually yes: published 2026 ranges put a complete VRF system at $24 to $38 per square foot against $32 to $58 for water-cooled chilled water. Over twenty years the gap narrows, because the plant uses less electricity and lasts longer, though its water bill cancels much of the energy saving. If the hotel already has chilled-water piping and fan coils, replacing the plant is almost always cheaper than converting.

Does VRF cause humidity problems in Florida hotel rooms?

It can, at part load. A VRF indoor unit at low load runs a warm coil and removes little moisture, and a guest room at night in summer is a low-load, high-humidity space; the usual result is relative humidity above 65 per cent and, in time, a mould complaint. The remedy in either system is a dedicated outdoor-air unit delivering dry air to every room, and chilled-water fan coils are more forgiving when it falls short.

Can a hotel still install R-410A VRF equipment?

Only until 1 January 2027, and only equipment manufactured or imported before 1 January 2026, under the EPA's Technology Transitions rule. New VRF is built on R-32 or R-454B, both A2L refrigerants, which brings ASHRAE 15-2022 charge limits, sensors and shut-off valves into guest-room design. New comfort-cooling chillers have been limited to refrigerants below 700 GWP since 1 January 2025, and the transition changes a chiller plant very little.
