Building here

What governs a project on this coast

Six things decide a project here before anybody draws anything: the flood datum, the wind code, the permit clock, the town’s working season, the seawall, and the order in which you hire. Most owners meet them late, one at a time, and pay for each discovery. These pages set out all six plainly, with the statute where there is one.

The sequence

The order of things

Not a rule — an order of information. Each step produces what the next one needs, and skipping one means the next is done on assumption.

  1. Survey before anything is designedBoundary, topographic, trees where there is canopy, and an elevation certificate in a flood zone. Until those exist nobody knows the buildable envelope, how the ground falls or how high the floor has to sit.
  2. Read the land's rules yourselfZoning district, setbacks, coverage, floor area ratio, height from the required datum, overlays, anything historic, and on the water the waterward setback. A reading exercise, and it belongs to the owner.
  3. Test the soil earlyBorings and a geotechnical report. The foundation follows the soil, and the soil can rewrite the structure. Discovering it after schematic design is an expensive way to learn it.
  4. Design with the constraints in handA first scheme that fits is worth more than a beautiful one that has to be dismantled at the second zoning meeting.
  5. Bring the builder in during designAt roughly the halfway mark, while the details are still lines. This is the single step most often skipped and the one that saves the most.
  6. Engineer the structure and the systemsIn the high-velocity hurricane zone, structural engineering is not a refinement. Nothing clears plan review without it, and every opening carries a documented product approval.
  7. Build the calendar before committing to a dateStatutory review periods are fixed; the local layer is not, and the seasonal working restrictions in several towns here can move a start by months.
  8. Name an owner for every threadConsultants, submittals, long-lead items and the property itself all need someone holding them. Establish at the outset which your architect's fee covers and which your builder's does; the ones nobody names are the ones that go missing.

The step owners most often reverse is the first one. Design is the part of the process people have heard of, so it is where they begin, and the survey arrives afterwards to tell everybody what was not possible. Who to hire first works through each appointment in turn, including the statutory exemption that means Florida does not require an architect for a house at all.

Local conditions

Six things that are true here and not elsewhere

An owner who has built in another state has most of the experience and almost none of these.

The floor is set by a datum, not by taste

On most of this coast the finished floor elevation is dictated by a flood map, a municipal freeboard requirement and the crown of the road, and those three can disagree. The house rises to satisfy the highest of them, and everything else — the entry sequence, the driveway slope, the pool deck, the relationship to the garden — follows from a number nobody chose.

Every opening is a tested assembly

In Miami-Dade and Broward a window is not selected from a catalogue and approved on appearance. It carries a Notice of Acceptance or a Florida product approval, tested to impact protocols, and the frame, the anchorage and the substrate are part of the approval. Changing a window late in a project is not a finish decision; it is a structural one.

The town decides when you may work

Several of the towns on this coast suspend or sharply restrict construction for the season. It is not a noise ordinance in the ordinary sense — it closes the site. A project that ignores this does not run slowly, it stops, and the stop lands in the months when an owner most wants progress.

The seawall is the biggest number nobody models

Buyers inspect the house and look at the dock. Very few have the wall assessed, and of everything on a waterfront parcel it is the item most capable of producing a seven-figure surprise — particularly now that municipalities have begun setting minimum elevations with deadlines that redevelopment accelerates.

Per-square-foot figures mislead here more than anywhere

The same drawing costs materially different sums in Palm Beach, Fort Lauderdale and Boca Raton, and a long thin house on a difficult lot behaves nothing like a compact one on a dry inland parcel. The honest unit is the project, read against published bands by tier.

A licence can be checked in about a minute

Every certified and registered contractor in Florida appears in the Department of Business and Professional Regulation's public register, with the licence class and its status. An owner about to spend seven figures should look, every time, and should ask for the number rather than wait to be offered it.

The season

The restriction that is in no one’s budget

Several towns on this coast do not merely limit construction noise. They close the site for the season, and the closure falls in the months an owner most wants to see progress.

The pattern is consistent along the barrier island and in the smaller towns behind it. A season is defined, usually running from late autumn to spring. Within it, construction is permitted on weekdays only, or on sharply reduced hours, and in some towns the loudest categories of work are prohibited outright rather than merely restricted — pile driving, dredging, dry sandblasting, jackhammering, and in at least one town the mixing and pumping of concrete.

The consequences are structural rather than cosmetic. A concrete frame cannot be poured in a town that prohibits concrete pumping during the season, which means the structure either goes up before the season opens or waits until it closes. A piling foundation on a soft lot has the same problem. The sequence of the whole job therefore has to be designed around a calendar that most architects outside these towns have never had to read, and that no statutory permit clock accounts for.

There are further layers. Some towns prohibit work entirely across the end of December. Some define planting, pruning and mowing as regulated activity, so that even landscape maintenance is inside the ordinance. Some set no decibel limit at all, which sounds permissive and is the opposite: with no measurable threshold, the question is whether the work is audible and objected to. And on the oceanfront, sea turtle lighting ordinances switch off beachfront illumination from dusk to dawn for roughly eight months of the year, which affects construction lighting and the finished house equally.

We map this per town before a schedule is written, because it is the single most common reason a realistic South Florida programme is longer than the one an owner was shown. It is also, usefully, knowable in advance. The ordinances are published; almost nobody reads them until the stop-work notice.

What to establish per town

  • Whether a construction season is defined, and its dates
  • Permitted days and hours within the season
  • Which categories of work are prohibited outright
  • Whether concrete pumping or pile driving is among them
  • Any complete shutdown around the holidays
  • Whether landscape work falls inside the ordinance
  • Whether a decibel limit exists, or only an audibility test
  • Beachfront lighting dates, on an oceanfront parcel
  • Whether an architectural review board sits, and how often

Questions

What owners ask

What should I know before building a house in South Florida?

Five things govern a project here before design begins. The finished floor elevation is set by the flood datum rather than by preference. In Miami-Dade and Broward every opening and assembly is a tested product carrying documented approval, which adds roughly eight to twelve per cent. Permit review runs on statutory clocks measured in business days, and the local layer on top of them is not fixed. Several towns on this coast restrict or suspend construction for the season, which can move a start date by months. And on the water, the seawall is routinely the largest unmodelled cost on the parcel. The order in which you hire the professionals determines how many of these you find out about early and cheaply rather than late and expensively.

Do I need an architect to build a house in Florida?

No. Section 481.229(1)(b) of the Florida Statutes exempts one-family and two-family residence buildings, townhouses and appurtenant domestic outbuildings from the requirement for an architect's seal, regardless of cost. What the building official requires is a document set they will accept — in practice sealed structural engineering, product approvals for every opening, energy calculations and, in a flood zone, flood design documentation. The exemption removes a legal requirement, not the technical work, and on a significant house hiring an architect is usually still right — for design and coordination rather than for permission.

How long does it take to get a building permit in Florida?

Section 553.792 sets the clocks. Thirty business days for a residential permit on a structure under 7,500 square feet, sixty for one at or above that, and sixty for most non-residential work under 25,000 square feet. The jurisdiction has five business days to tell you what is missing, or the application is deemed complete. Miss the deadline and the permit fee is reduced ten per cent per business day, rising to twenty after a resubmittal. None of which covers zoning review, the architectural board in towns that have one, utility approvals or the seasonal restrictions — which is why the real calendar is longer than the statute.

How much does it cost to build a custom home in South Florida?

Published 2026 figures put high-end production custom work on the mainland in the region of $450 to $650 per square foot, bespoke custom at roughly $650 to $900, direct waterfront at about $900 to $1,300 and oceanfront or overlay-constrained sites from $1,300 upward. Palm Beach County commonly runs ten to twenty-five per cent above comparable Miami-Dade waterfront, and the high-velocity hurricane zone adds about eight to twelve per cent on top. Those are market bands rather than our quotations, and the per-square-foot unit is the least reliable way to read them.

What is the high-velocity hurricane zone?

A specific part of the Florida Building Code applying only to Miami-Dade and Broward counties, created after Hurricane Andrew in 1992. Risk Category II design wind speeds are 175 mph in Miami-Dade and 170 mph in Broward. Every opening and assembly must carry a Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance or a Florida product approval, tested to the impact protocols. It raises cost by roughly eight to twelve per cent, and the record since the unified code took effect in March 2002 is that no home built to it has been destroyed in a subsequent storm.

Yes, at no charge.
