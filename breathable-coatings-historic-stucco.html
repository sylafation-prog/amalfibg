Historic · Coatings

The wall has to breathe, and the paint decides whether it can

A 1920s wall and a 2005 wall are not the same object with different dates on them. One was built to let water pass through it and leave; the other was built to keep water out. Put the coating meant for the second on the first and the wall does not stop getting wet. It simply stops being able to dry, and everything that follows is the consequence of that one decision.

The mechanism

Why old walls were built to get wet

Nothing about a lime-stuccoed wall was intended to be waterproof. The system was designed to absorb, buffer and release.

A Mediterranean Revival wall in Palm Beach or Coral Gables is typically hollow clay tile or poured concrete, rendered in a lime-rich stucco and finished with limewash or a mineral coating. That assembly has no cavity, no membrane and no drainage plane. It has never had one and was never supposed to. Water that arrives in wind-driven rain is taken into the render, held there, and given back to the air when the weather changes. The render is sacrificial by design: soft, open and replaceable, protecting the structure behind it by being the thing that wears.

Modern construction took the opposite approach. A wall of block with modern cement stucco, a weather-resistive barrier and an acrylic topcoat is designed to shed water at the outer face and keep the assembly dry. Both strategies work. They are simply incompatible, and the failure mode when they are mixed is entirely predictable.

The property that governs all of this is water vapour permeance, measured under ASTM E96 and expressed in perms. It appears on every reputable coating data sheet, and almost nobody reading a painting quote has ever been shown it. The working rule is the one conservators have used for a century: the coating must be at least as permeable as the material under it, and preferably more. Each successive layer outward should be more open than the one beneath, never less.

When that rule is broken the wall still takes water in — through hairline cracks, open joints, window perimeters, rising damp, or simply because stucco is porous and humidity here is relentless. What it can no longer do is give that water back. The moisture accumulates behind the film, and the first thing to fail is the bond between the film and the wall.

What it looks like

The failures are diagnostic

Each of these has a different cause, and the difference tells you what was done to the wall before you owned it.

Sheet peeling

Large areas coming away in a continuous membrane, often with a layer of powdery render attached to the back. This is moisture pressure behind a film that will not pass vapour. The paint did not fail; the wall pushed it off.

Blistering while dry

Bubbles appearing in settled weather, with no visible water source. Vapour accumulating at the back of the coating and lifting it. Puncture one and the render behind is usually damp.

The widening ring

A previous patch, sound in the middle, failing in a ring around its edge and growing outward each season. The moisture that used to leave through the failed area is now concentrating at the perimeter of the repair.

Efflorescence

White crystalline bloom pushing through or under the paint. Dissolved salts carried to the surface by moisture moving out of the wall, left behind as the water evaporates. It indicates water movement, and brushing it off addresses nothing.

Spalling at a hard patch

A cement patch sitting proud and intact while the original render crumbles in a halo around it. The patch is harder and less permeable than the wall, so stress and moisture both concentrate at its edge.

Rust staining in a straight line

Not a coating problem at all. Chloride has reached embedded steel — a lintel, reinforcement, a casement frame — and the corrosion is splitting the masonry from inside. No paint addresses it.

Lime

Limewash, and why it is still specified

The oldest coating on any of these buildings is also, on the right wall, still the best one.

Limewash is slaked lime and water, sometimes with lime-fast pigment. It does not dry so much as cure: the calcium hydroxide takes carbon dioxide out of the air and reverts to calcium carbonate — chemically, back into limestone. It does not sit on the wall as a film. It becomes part of the top fraction of a millimetre of the render.

That gives it properties nothing synthetic reproduces. It is effectively as vapour-open as the wall itself, so moisture passes through it without resistance. It is strongly alkaline as it cures, which suppresses the algae and mildew that otherwise streak a shaded elevation here within a season. Hairline cracking tends to self-heal as free lime migrates and recarbonates. And the surface has a depth and a slight variation that no roller-applied modern paint imitates convincingly, because it is a mineral surface rather than a plastic one.

The trade-offs are real and should be stated plainly rather than discovered. Limewash weathers — it erodes gently and gradually, and on an exposed elevation it wants attention on a cycle of years rather than a decade. The palette is limited to pigments that survive high alkalinity, which is why historic colour schemes look the way they do. It is applied in several thin coats and it shows the hand that applied it, which is the aesthetic for some owners and a defect for others. And it is not a coating for modern cement stucco, which is too dense and too smooth for it to key into properly.

On an original lime render that has never been sealed, limewash is often both the historically correct answer and the cheapest one over thirty years. The reason it is rarely quoted is that most painters have never applied it.

Silicate

Mineral paint, which bonds rather than coats

The nineteenth-century German invention that behaves like limewash and lasts like masonry.

Potassium silicate paint — mineral paint, sometimes sold as silicate or sol-silicate — works by a reaction rather than by adhesion. The silicate binder penetrates a mineral substrate and chemically fuses with it in a process called silicification. The result is not a layer resting on the wall; it is a mineral surface continuous with the wall beneath.

That gives it the permeability of limewash with far greater durability. The pigments are mineral oxides rather than organic colourants, so they do not break down under ultraviolet — at twenty-six degrees north with little cloud cover, that difference shows within a few years on a south elevation. It will not support mould growth, it does not chalk the way acrylic does, and service lives measured in decades are normal in European practice where these coatings have been in continuous use since the 1890s.

The constraint is the substrate. Pure silicate needs a mineral surface to react with: lime render, cement render, concrete, brick, natural stone. It will not bond to an existing organic film, which means a wall previously coated in acrylic or elastomeric is not a candidate until that film comes off. Dispersion or sol-silicate formulations tolerate a small organic fraction and widen the range somewhat, but they do not rescue a sealed wall.

Where the original coating is long gone and the render is sound, a mineral system is usually the recommendation: it respects how the wall works, it holds colour, and it does not need redoing on the cycle limewash does.

Modern systems

Acrylic and elastomeric, and where they belong

Both are good products. Neither is a general-purpose answer, and one of them is the most common cause of serious stucco damage on old houses in Florida.

A quality exterior acrylic forms a continuous film over the surface. Its permeance varies considerably by product and by film thickness, and the data sheet will tell you — the range runs from reasonably open to effectively a vapour retarder. On modern cement stucco over block, designed to shed water at the face, that is exactly right. On an original lime render it is a question that has to be answered from the data sheet rather than assumed, and a great many acrylics are too closed for the job.

Elastomeric is the serious one. It is applied at several times the film thickness of conventional paint specifically so that it can stretch across moving cracks, and that thickness is the point of it. On a modern wall with hairline movement cracking and a dry assembly behind, it solves a real problem well. On a lime-rendered wall it is close to the worst thing that can be done, because it combines very low permeance with a film tough enough that when it finally lets go it takes the face of the render with it rather than flaking off harmlessly.

The pattern on these houses is wearily familiar. An elevation is sealed, perhaps with the best intentions after a leak. For two or three years it looks excellent. Then a blister appears, usually low on the wall or near a window head. The blister is cut out and patched. A ring of failure opens around the patch over the following season, because the moisture that was trying to leave at that point is now trying to leave at its edges. Within five years a substantial area of original render has gone, and the replacement is modern cement stucco, which is harder than the original and sets up the next failure at its own perimeter.

Silicone resin emulsion systems sit between the two: water-repellent at the surface, still substantially vapour-open, and tolerant of a wider range of substrates. On a wall that has to shed driven rain but must also dry inward and outward, they are frequently the practical compromise.

Diagnosis

How to tell what is on your wall

Before anyone specifies anything, the question is what was done to this building by people who are no longer available to ask.

The water test

Flick clean water at the wall in several places. On an open mineral surface it darkens and soaks in within seconds. If it beads and sits, something film-forming or water-repellent is on there. Test high and low, and on each elevation — they are frequently not the same.

Film thickness at an edge

Find a broken edge, a chip or a window reveal and look at the section with a lens. A conventional paint reads as a thin skin. Elastomeric is unmistakable: a thick rubbery layer you can often lift and stretch between your fingers.

Count the layers

At any chip, count the colours in section. Six or seven layers on a 1925 house is normal and tells you the wall has been painted roughly once a decade. It also tells you how much there is to remove if the answer turns out to be removal.

Where it is failing

Map it before diagnosing it. Failure concentrated low on the wall suggests rising damp or splashback. Failure under window heads suggests water entering above. Failure on one elevation only usually means exposure. Failure everywhere means the system.

Look at the sheen

Original limewash and mineral coatings are flat and slightly granular, and they look different wet than dry. A uniform plastic sheen across a 1920s elevation, particularly a smooth one, is a modern film.

Check the data sheet

If anyone can tell you what was last applied, find the product and read its permeance. This one piece of paper settles more arguments than any amount of inspection, and it is worth asking the previous owner's contractor for.

The hard part

Taking a sealed wall back is the expensive step

This is the part that gets left out of the optimistic version of the conversation.

Once a film-forming coating is on lime render, going back to a breathable system means removing it, and there is no pleasant way to do that. Abrasive methods — sandblasting, needle guns, aggressive grinding — take the historic surface off along with the paint, and on a designated property they are usually prohibited outright. High-pressure water drives more moisture into a wall already struggling to dry and erodes the render as surely as sand does.

What is left is chemical stripping, patiently and in sections: an appropriate poultice or gel stripper, adequate dwell time, gentle removal, thorough neutralising and rinsing, and a long drying period before anything new goes on. It is slow, it is labour-intensive, and the runoff has to be contained and handled properly rather than washed into the planting and the Intracoastal.

On a house built before 1978 the stripping is also, almost by definition, a lead-safe job. Removing coatings disturbs every layer beneath, which is precisely the activity the federal Renovation, Repair and Painting rule governs, and the minor-repair exemption of twenty square feet of exterior surface is irrelevant at this scale. Containment, prohibited methods, verified cleaning and record-keeping all apply.

The honest arithmetic is that sealing a lime wall costs very little and costs a great deal later. Which is the whole argument for establishing what the wall is before a product is chosen, rather than after a blister appears.

Approvals

If the house is designated, the coating is a decision somebody else shares

A change of coating type is a change of appearance, and that is the trigger a preservation ordinance is written around.

On a landmarked property, repainting in the same colour and the same material is routine maintenance and generally sits outside review. Changing from limewash to acrylic, or from a mineral finish to elastomeric, changes the texture, the sheen and the way the elevation reads in sunlight — and in several ordinances that is a material alteration rather than maintenance.

It is a short conversation with staff and a bad surprise if it is skipped. Most municipalities will tell you over the telephone whether what you are proposing is administrative, chair-level or a full board item, and the answer usually turns on whether the work is in kind. We have written separately on how that process runs in the Town of Palm Beach, including which items staff can approve without a hearing.

There is also a practical reason to ask early that has nothing to do with compliance. Preservation staff have seen every coating failure in the municipality, repeatedly, on buildings very like yours. They are frequently the best free technical advice available on what has worked on that street and what has not.

Questions

What owners ask

Can I just put breathable paint over the elastomeric that is already there?

No. Permeability is governed by the least permeable layer in the system, so an open coating over a closed one performs exactly like the closed one. The moisture still cannot leave, and you have added a layer that will come off with the elastomeric when it eventually releases. If the wall needs to breathe, the film has to come off first.

Is limewash practical in the Florida climate?

Yes, with realistic expectations. High humidity actually suits the carbonation that cures it, and the alkalinity suppresses the biological growth that is the main nuisance on shaded elevations here. What the climate does shorten is the recoating cycle on exposed elevations, particularly those taking salt and full afternoon sun. Treated as a maintenance finish on a cycle rather than a ten-year paint job, it works well. Treated as a modern coating, it disappoints.

What is the difference between mineral paint and limewash?

Both are mineral systems and both are highly vapour-open, but they cure differently and perform differently. Limewash is slaked lime that carbonates back into limestone and remains relatively soft, erodes gently and wants recoating on a cycle of years. Silicate mineral paint reacts chemically with the substrate and produces a far more durable surface with a wider colour range and a service life measured in decades. Limewash is the historically authentic finish; silicate is usually the practical one where the original is long gone.

How do I know if my stucco is lime or cement?

Age is the first indicator: houses here from before roughly the 1930s are generally lime-rich, and cement-based stucco became the norm afterwards. Beyond that, lime render is noticeably softer — it can usually be scratched with a coin or a key — and it tends to be irregular in thickness with a slightly undulating surface. Cement stucco is hard, flat and uniform. Many older houses are now both, having been patched in cement over decades, which is why the condition is mapped elevation by elevation rather than assumed for the building.

Does a breathable coating mean the wall will leak?

No, and this is the most common misunderstanding. Vapour permeability and liquid water resistance are separate properties, and a well-formulated mineral or silicone-resin system is water-repellent to liquid rain while remaining open to vapour. The wall sheds the storm and still dries afterwards. What a breathable system will not do is compensate for a genuine defect: a failed flashing, an open joint or a blocked scupper is a building fault, and no coating is the repair for it.

My painter has never heard of any of this. Is it exotic?

It is standard practice in conservation and routine in most of Europe, and it is unfamiliar to a great deal of the residential painting trade in Florida simply because almost all of the housing stock here is modern and the modern answer is correct for it. That is not a criticism of the trade. It does mean that on a pre-war house the question of what the wall is has to be raised by somebody, and if nobody raises it the default product goes on.

Will a preservation board object to limewash?

On a building that was originally limewashed, it is usually the opposite — a return to the original finish is generally welcomed and frequently the treatment a board would prefer. What draws scrutiny is a change away from the original material, or a colour that cannot be substantiated. Where there is any designation on the property the municipality is the right place to confirm before ordering, and the answer is typically quick.
