Estates · Grounds oversight

Oversight of the crew you already have, against a written standard

Almost every estate on this coast already has a landscape crew, and almost none of them has a written standard for the crew to be held to. That is the gap. Not another maintenance contract — a specification, a calendar, a record of what was found, and someone on the owner’s side of the fence reading it. It is also, incidentally, the cheapest way an owner will ever discover what their existing vendor is and is not certified to do.

The angle

Nobody in this market sells oversight, so nobody is held to anything

The landscape trade sells labour by the visit. What it does not sell — and what an estate actually needs — is a written standard and an independent reading of whether the labour met it.

The structure on most estates is the same. A crew arrives weekly. They mow, they blow, they trim what is in the way, and they leave. Once a quarter somebody sprays something. Twice a year somebody fertilises. The owner knows the crew is good or bad in the way one knows the weather is good or bad — by looking out of the window — and when a hedge thins out, a palm declines or a bed fills with the same weed three seasons running, nobody can say what was applied to it, when, by whom, or whether the person applying it was certified to.

Oversight is the missing half of that arrangement. It starts with a written specification: mowing height by turf species and by season, blade sharpness, clipping disposal, edge lines, pruning windows by plant and the cuts that are not to be made, palm practice and the fronds that are not to be removed, bed depth and mulch type, irrigation heads checked for aim at every visit, debris removal, and the areas the crew is not to enter. Then it becomes a calendar, and then a record: what was found, what was done, what was not done and what needs a decision.

None of that requires a licence or a certificate of any kind. Mowing, pruning, edging, debris removal and the inspection of a crew’s work against a written standard sit outside Florida’s pest control regime and outside Chapter 489 entirely. That is worth saying plainly, because the useful part of this service is the part that is unregulated, and the regulated part — what may be sprayed, on what, by whom, with which label and with which equipment — is where the existing vendor is almost always exposed.

Which is the second thing oversight does, and the reason the certification limits are published in full below. An owner holding a written standard can ask their crew for the certificate number behind every application, check what that certificate authorises, and find out whether the quarterly spray on the lawn was lawful. On a surprising number of estates it was not — not through bad faith, but because the regime is narrower than its name suggests and almost nobody reads it.

Requires no licence or certificate

  • Mowing, by height and by species
  • Edging and line definition
  • Pruning and palm work within a written window
  • Debris and clipping removal
  • Mulch depth and bed definition
  • Checking irrigation head aim at every visit
  • Photographing and grading condition against a standard
  • Inspecting another vendor’s work and reporting on it
  • Holding the calendar and the record
  • Asking a vendor for the certificate number behind an application

The certificate

The limited certification does far less than its name suggests

The credential the landscape trade points to is the Limited Certification for Commercial Landscape Maintenance Personnel, section 482.156 of the Florida Statutes, applied for on FDACS form DACS 13604. Here is its actual reach, in both directions.

What 482.156 authorises

  • Herbicides for weeds in plant beds, driveways, sidewalks and patios
  • Integrated pest management on ornamental plants — and on ornamentals only
  • Only pesticides bearing no signal word, or the signal word “CAUTION”
  • Portable handheld applicators and backpack sprayers only
  • No power application equipment of any kind
  • FDACS guidance puts the equipment limit at a 3-gallon compressed-air sprayer or a 5-gallon backpack
  • Held by a person, for landscape maintenance personnel, not by a business

What 482.156(4) expressly excludes

  • ANY pesticide application to turf — no exceptions, no products, no circumstances
  • Operating a pest control business
  • Letting uncertified personnel apply pesticide under the certified person’s supervision
  • Products bearing the signal word “WARNING” or “DANGER”
  • Pesticide application to anything that is not an ornamental plant
  • Power equipment, including any tow-behind or truck-mounted sprayer
  • Weed-and-feed mixtures, which FDACS states are outside the certification

Read those two columns together and the practical conclusion is blunt. The certification covers weeds in the beds and on the hardscape, and gentle products on the ornamentals, applied out of a hand pump. It does not cover the lawn — and the lawn is where almost all of the chemistry on a South Florida estate actually goes. A crew holding this certificate and spraying turf is outside its certification, and the fact that the practice is widespread does not make it lawful. The exclusion at subsection (4) is not an interpretation; it is the text.

The mechanics

Examination, insurance, one year, four hours

The administrative shape of the certificate matters for one reason: a certificate that lapses is not a certificate, and the renewal clock is short and unforgiving in a way most crews do not plan for.

How it is obtainedBy examination administered by the department, on application form DACS 13604

The last row is the one worth building a programme around. Subsection (5) imposes a statutory duty to record which pests were treated, in which areas, by which method and with which material, and to produce it to the department on request. That is the same record a written estate standard needs, for the same reasons — so the documentation is not an overhead invented by a service company but a legal obligation a well-run programme discharges properly. An owner asking a vendor for their 482.156(5) records is asking for something the vendor is already required to hold.

Turf

Any pesticide on turf is a business licence, not a certificate

This is the single most consequential thing on the page, and it is the point at which most estate landscape arrangements turn out to be built on a misunderstanding.

Because section 482.156(4) excludes any pesticide application to turf from the limited certification, turf work has to find its authority somewhere else. It finds it in section 482.071, which requires a pest control business licence. That is a different kind of thing altogether: it is held by a business rather than by an individual, it requires liability cover of $250,000 per occurrence and $500,000 aggregate, or a $500,000 combined single limit, and it requires a certified operator in charge under section 482.111, holding certification in the relevant category — for a lawn, the Lawn and Ornamental category.

So the lawn chemistry on an estate is not a certificate somebody on the crew carries. It is a licensed business with an insured balance sheet and a certified operator standing behind it. There is no version of the limited certification, no supervision arrangement and no stacking of credentials that gets a landscape maintenance certificate holder onto turf — subsection (4) also expressly forbids uncertified personnel applying under the certified person’s supervision, which closes the obvious workaround.

So the structure for an estate programme is straightforward. Your licensed lawn-and-ornamental vendor sprays the turf, under their own business licence, with their own certified operator and their own insurance, and the oversight role is to hold them to the written standard: the right product for the pest actually present, the application recorded under 482.156(5) or its business-licence equivalent, the licence and certification numbers on file, the insurance certificate current, and the result inspected afterwards rather than assumed.

That arrangement also protects the owner. The party carrying the statutory liability for a turf application is the party licensed to make it, and only so long as they are the party making it. An owner who lets an unlicensed hand spray the lawn to save a line item has moved the exposure from an insured licensee onto themselves, and finds out at the worst moment: a drift complaint, a dead specimen tree, a neighbour’s loss, an FDACS enquiry.

For turf pesticide work, ask for

  • The pest control business licence number under 482.071
  • The name of the certified operator in charge under 482.111
  • Certification in the Lawn and Ornamental category specifically
  • Proof of liability cover: $250,000 / $500,000, or $500,000 combined single limit
  • The record of the pest treated, the area, the method and the material
  • The product label, and the signal word on it
  • Who physically made the application, and under whose licence
  • A current certificate of insurance before anyone arrives

Fertiliser

Fertiliser is a separate certification, on a separate clock

Owners and crews alike tend to assume fertiliser rides along with the pesticide credential. It does not. It has its own statute, its own prerequisite, its own fee and a term four times as long.

Section 482.1562 of the Florida Statutes creates a limited certification for urban landscape commercial fertilizer application, and it has been required of anyone applying commercial fertiliser to an urban landscape since 1 January 2014. Its prerequisite is not an examination in the first instance but a training certificate under section 403.9338 — the Green Industries Best Management Practices programme. The fee is $25 to $75. The term is four years, and renewal requires 4 classroom continuing education hours, of which at least 2 must be on fertiliser best management practices.

Subsection (7) sets out what it does not authorise, and the list closely mirrors the pesticide certificate: it does not authorise pesticide application to turf or to ornamentals, it does not authorise pesticide-fertiliser mixtures, and it does not permit uncertified workers to apply under the certified person’s supervision. Taken with the FDACS position that weed-and-feed products fall outside the landscape maintenance certification, the result is that the common combined product — the one that fertilises and kills weeds in a single pass over the lawn — is authorised by neither certificate.

There is one exemption and it is narrower than it first appears. Subsection (9) exempts yard workers who apply fertiliser only to individual residential properties using equipment supplied by the property owner or resident. The equipment condition is the whole exemption: a worker who arrives with his own spreader on the truck is outside it, however residential the property and however individual the application. On an estate, where the crew brings its own equipment as a matter of course, the exemption is of no practical help.

Fertiliser is the application an owner is least likely to question and most likely to be charged for: two visits a year, a bag rate, a line on an invoice. Asking for the 482.1562 certificate number, its expiry date and the GI-BMP certificate behind it costs nothing and takes one email, and it is the clearest test of whether the crew working the grounds is run as a business or run out of a truck.

For fertiliser application, ask for

  • The 482.1562 limited certification number, and its expiry date
  • The GI-BMP training certificate under s.403.9338 behind it
  • Confirmation that the certificate is within its four-year term
  • The continuing education record, including the 2 hours on fertiliser BMPs
  • The product, the analysis and the rate actually applied
  • Confirmation that no pesticide-fertiliser mixture was used
  • Whose equipment was used, if the 482.1562(9) exemption is being relied on
  • The date, in writing, against the county’s own application rules

The rain rule

Palm Beach County has no calendar blackout

Owners who have read about Florida fertiliser bans are usually thinking of a summer blackout — a date range in which nothing may be applied. Palm Beach County does not work that way. Its rule is triggered by the weather, which makes it harder to plan and far easier to break by accident.

The instrumentThe Florida Friendly Fertilizer Use Ordinance, adopted 30 October 2012

The rules differ by county and by municipality, and several municipalities are stricter than the county they sit in. This page publishes Palm Beach County’s ordinance because it has been read; it does not publish a Broward or municipal rule, because those have not been, and a fertiliser rule stated from memory or by analogy is worse than no rule at all. Each applicable county and municipal ordinance is read directly before a programme is written for an address, and the governing dates and triggers are put on the calendar from the text rather than from a summary.

One calendar

The watch that closes the house is the watch that stops the fertiliser

This is the connection worth taking away from the page, and it is the reason the grounds and the house belong on one calendar rather than two.

A Tropical Storm Watch is issued when tropical-storm-force winds are possible within about 48 hours. On a waterfront estate that notice starts a sequence that has nothing to do with the garden: panels and hardware confirmed against the labelled schedule, furniture and loose grounds items struck and stored, the pool level taken down to the skimmer throat, the dock secured, the generator fuelled and exercised, every opening closed and photographed with the time against it. That is the subject of the storm closing sequence, and it is run against the National Hurricane Center’s own lead times.

The same notice does something else entirely. In Palm Beach County, a Tropical Storm Watch places the property inside the Prohibited Application Period: fertiliser may not be applied while it stands. So one meteorological event triggers two obligations on the same property at the same moment — an operational one to close the house and a regulatory one to stop the grounds programme — and they are almost always held by different people who do not speak to each other.

The failure mode is ordinary and expensive. A crew arrives on its scheduled Tuesday with a spreader on the truck and a watch is in force; or the watch is lifted, two inches of rain are still forecast, and the application goes on anyway and washes into the Intracoastal from inside the 10-foot setback. Both are scheduling failures between two vendors, and both are visible in advance to anybody reading one calendar instead of two.

Which is the whole argument for oversight rather than another contract. The grounds standard, the irrigation programme and the storm sequence are not three services; they are three readings of the same week. A head left aimed at the glass, a fertiliser application made under a watch, and a storm close started late are all the same failure — nobody holding the property as one thing. The oversight role is to hold it as one thing, in writing, and to be the party who notices.

Held on one calendar

  • National Weather Service watches and warnings, read against the application rules
  • Fertiliser windows, by applicable county and municipal ordinance
  • Pesticide applications, with the certificate or licence behind each one
  • Pruning and palm windows by species and season
  • Irrigation head aim checked at every grounds visit
  • Watering days set to the ordinance for the address
  • Storm close triggers and the labelled opening schedule
  • Certificate and insurance expiry dates for every vendor on the property

Who does what

The division of work on the grounds

Set out plainly. The standard, the inspection and the record are our own work and require no certificate; the chemistry belongs to the licensed and certified vendor who applies it, contracted in your name.

Our own people, on the written standard

  • Writing the grounds standard for the property, plant by plant and area by area
  • Mowing, edging, pruning within the written window, and debris removal
  • Mulch depth, bed definition and the dry margin against the building
  • Checking irrigation head aim and reporting overspray on glass, stone and stucco
  • Inspecting and grading another vendor’s work against the standard
  • Photographing condition, visit by visit, and holding the record
  • Collecting and holding certificate, licence and insurance numbers for every vendor
  • Reading the applicable county and municipal ordinance before the calendar is written
  • Flagging a scheduled application that falls inside a Prohibited Application Period

Held by your licensed vendor, in your name

  • Turf pesticide application — a pest control business licence under 482.071
  • With a certified operator in charge under 482.111, Lawn and Ornamental category
  • Insured at $250,000 / $500,000, or $500,000 combined single limit
  • Bed and ornamental pesticide work — the 482.156 limited certification holder
  • Commercial fertiliser application — the 482.1562 certificate holder, GI-BMP trained
  • Each application recorded by the applicator, as 482.156(5) requires
  • Each licence, certificate and insurance number on file and verifiable
  • Each vendor’s own price, passed through without a mark-up

There is a reason worth knowing for keeping the vendor contracts in your own name. Section 489.105(3) defines a contractor as a person who for compensation undertakes work “himself or herself or by others”, and the pest control statutes work the same way: the party licensed to make an application is the party who must make it, and the party who holds the statutory record of it. Contracted in your name, the licence, the insurance, the liability and the record all sit with the party that actually holds them — and each of those is checkable in a minute. Oversight sits on the owner’s side of that arrangement, which is exactly what makes it useful: the party reading the work is not the party being paid to perform it.

Questions

What owners ask

Do I need a licence to mow, prune and maintain grounds in Florida?

No. Mowing, pruning, edging, debris removal, mulching and the inspection of a crew’s work against a written standard require no Florida licence or certificate. The regulated activity is the application of pesticides and commercial fertiliser, governed by Chapter 482 of the Florida Statutes rather than by the construction licensing chapter.

What does the FDACS limited certification for landscape maintenance actually allow?

Under section 482.156, it allows herbicides for weeds in plant beds, driveways, sidewalks and patios, and integrated pest management on ornamental plants only, using only products bearing no signal word or the signal word “CAUTION” — never “WARNING” or “DANGER”. Application must be by portable handheld applicator or backpack sprayer; no power equipment is permitted, and FDACS guidance puts the limit at a 3-gallon compressed-air sprayer or a 5-gallon backpack.

Can a landscape crew spray my lawn under that certification?

No. Section 482.156(4) expressly excludes any pesticide application to turf from the limited certification, and it also excludes operating a pest control business and allowing uncertified personnel to apply under the certified person’s supervision, which closes the obvious workaround. Turf pesticide work requires a pest control business licence under 482.071 — with liability cover of $250,000 per occurrence and $500,000 aggregate, or a $500,000 combined single limit — and a certified operator in charge under 482.111 in the Lawn and Ornamental category. That is a licensed business, not a certificate somebody on the crew carries.

Who does what under a grounds oversight arrangement?

The written standard, the mowing, edging, pruning within the agreed window, debris removal, mulch and bed definition, the irrigation head aim checks, the inspection and grading of every vendor’s work and the photographic record are ours. The chemistry belongs to the licensed and certified applicator: turf pesticide work to your pest control business licensee under 482.071, bed and ornamental work to the 482.156 certificate holder, commercial fertiliser to the 482.1562 certificate holder. Those vendors are contracted in your name, and their licence, certificate and insurance numbers are held on file and checked at renewal.

Is fertiliser covered by the same certification as pesticide?

No — it is a separate limited certification under section 482.1562, required of anyone applying commercial fertiliser to an urban landscape since 1 January 2014. Its prerequisite is the Green Industries Best Management Practices training certificate under section 403.9338. The fee is $25 to $75 and the term is four years, with 4 classroom continuing education hours to renew, at least 2 of them on fertiliser best management practices.

Does the yard worker fertiliser exemption help my crew?

Almost certainly not. Section 482.1562(9) exempts yard workers who apply fertiliser only to individual residential properties using equipment supplied by the property owner or resident. The equipment condition is the entire exemption. A crew that arrives with its own spreader is outside it, however residential the property, which on an estate means the exemption is of no practical use.

Does Palm Beach County have a summer fertiliser blackout?

No. Palm Beach County’s Florida Friendly Fertilizer Use Ordinance, adopted 30 October 2012, uses a weather-triggered Prohibited Application Period rather than a calendar ban: no fertilising while any part of the county is under a National Weather Service Flood Watch or Warning, Tropical Storm Watch or Warning, or Hurricane Watch or Warning, or when 2 or more inches of rain are expected within one day. There are also setbacks — 10 feet from a water body, reduced to 3 feet with a deflector shield — and requirements for spreader deflectors, spill recovery and keeping product out of storm drains.

What are the rules in Broward County?

This page does not state one, deliberately. The Palm Beach ordinance above has been read; the Broward county and municipal ordinances have not, and several municipalities on this coast are stricter than the counties they sit in. Fertiliser and pesticide rules vary by county and by municipality, and each applicable ordinance is read directly before a programme is written for an address rather than inferred from a neighbouring county.
