Stone and terrace care

A waterfront house here is largely made of stone that dissolves in acid, sits in salt, and is washed every evening by irrigation carrying dissolved minerals. Most of what owners are told is a stain is not a stain, most of what is sold as a cure is a sealer that cannot help, and a great deal of permanent damage is done by people cleaning conscientiously with the wrong bottle.

The material

Almost everything underfoot here is calcium carbonate

The single most useful division in this subject is not between stones by name. It is between the ones that dissolve in acid and the ones that do not.

Marble

Metamorphosed limestone, calcareous, and the most acid-sensitive surface in most houses. A squeeze of lime, a splash of wine, a toilet cleaner or a tumbled glass of soda leaves a dull mark within minutes that is not dirt and will not wash off. Takes a true polish, which also means it shows every loss of that polish.

Travertine

A freshwater limestone full of natural voids, usually supplied filled, often honed. Calcareous and acid-sensitive like marble, with the added habit of losing its fill over time — the small pits that open up on a terrace are the original voids reappearing, not damage, and they are refilled rather than resurfaced.

Coral keystone and coquina

The Florida stone. Quarried fossil reef, visibly full of shell and coral, extremely porous, and the signature material of the Mediterranean Revival houses on this coast. It absorbs readily, it holds salt, and it loses its cured outer skin permanently to pressure or abrasive cleaning. Handled with more care than anything else on the property.

Limestone and shellstone

Softer than marble, calcareous, and used widely for terraces, pool surrounds and cladding here precisely because it stays cool and does not glare. Takes a honed finish rather than a polish. Absorbent enough that oil from a dropped olive will sit in it until it is drawn back out.

Granite and quartzite

Siliceous rather than calcareous, which means acid does not etch them. They can still stain, still take mineral deposits and still lose polish to abrasion, but the panic over a spilled drink does not apply. Different problem set, different products, and the two should never be cleaned with the same bottle on autopilot.

Cuban tile and terrazzo

Not stone but treated here with it, and common on the older houses. Cement-based, coloured through, and intolerant of acid and of harsh alkaline strippers alike. Terrazzo polishes beautifully and historic Cuban cement tile is effectively irreplaceable, so both are approached as conservation rather than refinishing.

Diagnosis

Etch, stain and scratch are three different problems

They look similar at a glance and they have nothing in common. Nearly every wasted afternoon and every ruined floor starts with treating one as another.

An etch is a chemical burn. Acid has reacted with the calcium carbonate and removed a microscopically thin layer of the stone, taking the polish with it. It is a dull patch, often in the exact shape of whatever was spilled, and it sits level with or very slightly below the surface. Nothing has been added to the stone, so no cleaner on earth will touch it. It is corrected mechanically, by re-polishing the area or the field.

A stain is the opposite. Something has been absorbed into the stone and is still in there. It is usually darker than the surrounding surface and often has a soft edge where it wicked outward. The polish is intact. Cleaning the surface does nothing because the material is below it; it has to be drawn back out, which is what a poultice does, and the chemistry of the poultice depends entirely on what kind of substance it is.

A scratch is mechanical loss — sand underfoot, a dragged chair, a dropped tool. It catches a fingernail. Light scratching is removed by re-honing and re-polishing; deep scoring may need the field taken down further to make it disappear, which is why grit is dragged in at the door and why a mat at every exterior opening is worth more than any product.

The simplest field test: wet the mark. A stain usually darkens along with the stone and remains visible as a contrast when it dries. An etch frequently disappears while wet, because the water is temporarily filling the reflective loss, and reappears exactly as before within a minute. That one observation decides whether the answer is a chemical or a diamond.

Owners are rarely told any of this, which is why so many arrive having scrubbed an etch for a season with progressively stronger cleaners — several of them acidic, each one making the mark slightly larger.

The question we answer first

  • Is this etch, stain, scratch, mineral deposit or efflorescence
  • Is the stone calcareous or siliceous
  • What finish does it currently hold — polished, honed, brushed, tumbled
  • Is there a coating or topical sealer on it already
  • Where is the moisture coming from, if the problem keeps returning
  • Is the damage in the surface or in the body of the stone
  • Is the stone original to a historic building, which changes everything

The climate

What is actually attacking it

Several of these are things the household is doing every day with the best intentions.

Acid, in ordinary forms

Not industrial chemicals. Citrus, wine, vinegar, coffee, soft drinks, many bathroom and tile cleaners, and almost anything sold to remove hard-water spots. Pool chemistry is the serious one: muriatic acid used to adjust water or to clean tile will etch a marble or travertine coping on contact, and it is routinely used by people who have no idea the coping is calcareous.

Irrigation overspray

The quietly destructive one. Well and reclaimed water here carries a heavy mineral load, and a head that throws onto a terrace, a cladding panel or a stone sill deposits that mineral every cycle, every day. It builds into a hard white scale that etches as it is removed if the wrong product is used. Correcting the spray pattern is the actual repair; the cleaning is just the symptom.

Salt

Chloride is drawn into porous stone and crystallises as it dries. When that happens at the surface it is efflorescence, which looks alarming and is easily removed. When it happens just below the surface it is subflorescence, and the growing crystals exert enough pressure to push the face off the stone in flakes. Keystone, coquina and soft limestone are the vulnerable ones, and fresh-water rinsing is the whole defence.

Iron

Rust staining appears from three sources: metal furniture left standing on wet stone, ferrous fixings and anchors set into it, and iron naturally present within the stone itself. The first two are tractable. The third usually is not — once oxidation has started inside the body of the stone, the stain is in the material and poulticing rarely clears it fully. Honesty about which of the three you have is worth more than any product.

Organic and biological

Leaf litter, bougainvillea, mulch tannins and the drip line under planting all leave organic staining, and shaded damp terraces grow algae and mildew that read as dirt until the stone stays dark after cleaning. Both respond to the right chemistry and both return on a schedule set by the landscape, which is why the answer is usually a programme rather than a one-off.

Abrasion

Sand. It arrives on every foot from the beach and it is harder than calcite. On a polished floor it dulls traffic lanes within a season; on a terrace it slowly matts the surface. Nothing exotic is needed here — mats at the openings, regular dust-mopping and keeping grit off the stone does more for a marble floor than any treatment applied to it.

Mechanical

Honing and polishing is grinding, done carefully

Restoring a stone surface is abrasion under control. There is no chemical that re-polishes marble, and anything sold as one is doing something else.

The surface is worked through a progression of diamond abrasives, each grit removing the scratch pattern left by the one before it. A badly etched or scratched floor may start coarse and climb through several steps; a lightly worn one may need only the final two. The sequence is not optional and it cannot be shortened — skipping a grit leaves scratches the finer pads cannot reach, and they show as a haze under raking light exactly the way a Level 4 drywall joint does.

Where it stops decides the finish. Taken to a medium grit and left, the surface is honed: flat, matte, soft, and the correct finish for most terraces, pool surrounds and heavily used floors. Taken through the fine grits it becomes polished, and on marble a final powder polish brings up the reflective surface that makes the stone look wet. Honed hides wear; polished shows it. That is a decision about how the room is used, not about quality.

Levelling is a separate and much larger operation. Where tiles are set unevenly — lippage, a ridge you catch a toe on — the whole floor has to be ground flat as one plane before any finish is put on it. It is dusty, slow and expensive, and it is the one job that genuinely justifies a long conversation before anyone starts.

Then there is crystallisation, sometimes called vitrification, which is a chemical treatment that reacts with the stone surface to produce a hard shine quickly. It is common in commercial maintenance and it is not what we use on good stone. It alters the surface chemistry, it reduces vapour permeability on a material that often needs to breathe, and on historic or soft stone it is difficult to reverse. On a 1920s keystone terrace it is simply the wrong idea.

All of it produces slurry and dust, so containment, floor protection on the routes in and out, and water management are planned before the first machine comes through the door. On an occupied house that planning is most of what makes the job tolerable.

What the work involves

  • Test area first, in an inconspicuous place, assessed dry
  • Diamond abrasive progression matched to the damage, not to a standard menu
  • Lippage and levelling addressed as a separate decision, priced separately
  • Honed or polished finish chosen for how the space is actually used
  • Powder polish on marble where a reflective finish is wanted
  • Slurry contained, floors protected, routes in and out covered
  • No crystallisation or vitrifying treatments on soft or historic stone
  • Penetrating sealer applied only once the surface is finished and dry

Chemical

Drawing a stain back out

A poultice is an absorbent paste that reverses the direction the stain travelled. It works slowly, it often takes more than one application, and the chemistry has to match the substance.

The method is the same whatever the stain. An absorbent medium is mixed with the appropriate chemical into a paste, spread over the area, covered so it dries slowly from the outside in, and left for hours or days. As the poultice dries it draws the dissolved stain up out of the pores with the solvent. It is then removed, the area rinsed, and the result assessed dry. Two or three cycles is normal rather than a sign something went wrong.

What changes is the active ingredient, and it changes completely by stain class. Organic staining — leaves, tannin, coffee, wine — responds to an oxidising agent. Oil and grease want a solvent or a degreaser. Biological growth wants a different oxidiser again. Ink and paint want solvents matched to the medium. Rust wants a dedicated iron remover, and several of those are acidic, which on calcareous stone means the etch has to be anticipated and corrected afterwards.

Efflorescence is the exception that needs no chemistry at all. The white bloom is salt that has come out of the stone and crystallised on top of it; it brushes and vacuums off dry. What it is telling you is that water is moving through the material and arriving at that face, and if the water is not addressed the bloom simply returns. Treating efflorescence as a cleaning problem is a way of never fixing it.

The honest part of this conversation is that some stains do not come out. Deep iron oxidation within the body of the stone, old oil in a porous limestone, and plasticiser migration from the wrong sealant beside a joint are all frequently permanent. We will say so after a test patch rather than selling a sequence of attempts, and where it matters the options are replacement of the unit or living with it.

Stain classes and what they want

  • Organic — leaf, tannin, coffee, wine: an oxidising poultice
  • Oil and grease — cooking, suncream, machinery: solvent or degreaser
  • Biological — algae, mildew, moss: an appropriate oxidiser, not chlorine bleach on stone
  • Metal — rust from furniture or fixings: a dedicated iron remover, with etch correction after
  • Ink, paint and marker: solvent matched to the medium, tested first
  • Efflorescence: dry removal, and then find the water
  • Mineral scale from irrigation: controlled removal, and then move the head
  • Plasticiser from a staining sealant: usually permanent, and a specification failure

Sealing

What a sealer does, and the two things it does not

Sealing is useful, routinely oversold, and widely misunderstood in a way that costs owners real money.

A penetrating or impregnating sealer soaks into the pore structure and lines it, so liquids bead and sit on the surface instead of being drawn in. It does not form a film, it does not change the appearance much, and critically it leaves the stone vapour-open so moisture in the substrate can still get out. On a terrace, a pool surround, a kitchen counter or any absorbent floor, it is worth having and worth renewing.

What it does not do, first: it does not stop etching. Acid etch is a reaction with the stone surface itself, and a penetrating sealer sits below that surface rather than over it. A sealed marble counter will etch from lemon juice exactly as an unsealed one does. Anyone who tells you sealing solves etching is selling something.

What it does not do, second: it does not make stone stainproof. It buys time. A sealed stone gives you minutes or hours to wipe up a spill instead of seconds. Left overnight, red wine on sealed limestone will still stain. The sealer is a grace period, not a barrier, and describing it that way to a household is more useful than any instruction sheet.

The failure to avoid is a topical sealer — a film-forming coating that sits on top. Outdoors it is the same mistake as putting an impermeable paint on lime stucco: moisture coming up through the slab or out of the masonry cannot leave, so it collects under the film, and the film clouds, blisters and peels in sheets. It also makes a wet terrace slippery. On exterior stone we do not use them, and where one is already there the conversation starts with how it comes off.

A simple test tells you where you stand: flick water onto the stone in a few places. Beads and sits means the sealer is working. Darkens and soaks in within seconds means it has gone and it is time to reapply.

How we handle sealing

  • Penetrating and impregnating sealers only on exterior stone, never topical films
  • Applied after any honing, polishing or stain work, on dry stone
  • Different products for calcareous and siliceous stone
  • Water-bead test read and recorded at each programme visit
  • Renewal driven by the test result rather than by a calendar guess
  • Honest expectations set with the household: time to wipe, not immunity
  • Existing topical coatings identified before anything is applied over them

Outside

A terrace is not a floor that happens to be outdoors

Exterior stone has a different set of requirements, and one of them is that nobody falls over on it.

Slip resistance

Wet stone around a pool is where this stops being an aesthetic question. Surfaces are measured for dynamic coefficient of friction under ANSI A326.3, and a polished finish on an exterior walking surface is a poor idea regardless of how it looks dry. Honed, brushed, flamed and tumbled finishes exist for this reason. Where an owner wants more shine on a terrace, the conversation includes what that does when it rains.

Heat

Dark stone on a south-facing pool deck in July is not usable barefoot, and no amount of maintenance changes that. It is worth saying out loud during any discussion of refinishing or replacement, because the lighter limestones and shellstones used here were chosen for exactly this reason.

Movement joints

Exterior paving moves, and it needs joints that let it. These are frequently grouted solid during a well-meant repair, after which the field has nowhere to go and the stone cracks or tents. Movement joints are sealed, not grouted, with the correct sealant and backer rod, and on stone that sealant has to be a non-staining formulation.

Drainage and the thing underneath

Standing water, persistent dark patches and recurring efflorescence in the same spot are rarely a stone problem. They are a drainage or setting-bed problem showing through the stone. Cleaning the surface again each year treats the symptom; we would rather find out where the water is sitting and say so, even when the answer is not ours to fix.

Pool chemistry

The most common cause of damaged coping on this coast. Acid washing, muriatic acid used for tile scale, and splash-out from chemically unbalanced water all attack calcareous stone. Coordinating with whoever handles the water costs nothing and prevents the single most expensive avoidable form of stone damage we see.

Historic stone

Original keystone, coquina and early cast stone are conservation subjects, not refinishing subjects. The hardened outer skin that formed as they cured is the protective layer, it cannot be put back once abraded away, and that rules out pressure and most mechanical cleaning. The approach is the one set out under historic and landmark work: gentlest effective means, test patch first, assessed dry.

On a cycle

Stone is maintained or it is restored, and one is far cheaper

Almost every large stone bill we are asked to quote is the accumulated cost of several years in which nothing was done, by people who were cleaning it regularly.

The damage curve on stone is slow and then sudden. Grit dulls a traffic lane gradually. Scale builds on a sill a little each irrigation cycle. Salt accumulates in a keystone wall through a season. Sealer wears off unevenly over a couple of years. None of it announces itself, and then the floor is visibly two colours and the terrace is scaled and the coping has a white bloom, all at once, and the quote is for refinishing the field rather than maintaining it.

Under the estate calendar the stone is looked at on every visit and the water-bead test is read and recorded rather than guessed. Spills are addressed while they are still on the surface. Irrigation overspray is corrected at the head rather than cleaned off the stone forever. Elevations and terraces are rinsed of salt after an onshore blow. Light traffic lanes are polished back in a morning instead of becoming a project.

Where a house is on the programme we also hold the record: which stone is where, what finish it holds, what sealer was used and when, which products have been tested on it and which have not. That record is what prevents the next person with a bottle from undoing several years of care in an afternoon.

The other half of it is the household. A short written note of what may and may not be used on each surface, given to whoever cleans the house, prevents more damage than anything we apply. It is the cheapest item in this entire subject and it is almost never provided.

Under the estate programme

  • Stone condition walked and photographed on every visit
  • Water-bead test read and recorded; sealer renewed on the result
  • Salt rinsing of stone elevations and terraces after an onshore blow
  • Irrigation overspray onto stone corrected at the head
  • Traffic lanes polished back before they read as a change of colour
  • Travertine fill made good as voids open
  • Movement joints checked and renewed with non-staining sealant
  • A written surface-by-surface care note for the household and its cleaners

The line

Where stone care ends

Decorative stone, tile, marble, granite and terrazzo installation is a named exempt job scope under Florida's contracting statute, and maintenance of it was never contracting at all. Some adjacent work is licensed.

Ours

  • Cleaning, honing, polishing and refinishing of marble, travertine, limestone, keystone, granite and terrazzo
  • Etch correction and scratch removal
  • Poulticing and stain removal across all stain classes
  • Efflorescence and mineral-scale removal, and finding the source
  • Penetrating sealer application, testing and renewal
  • Travertine fill and minor chip and edge repair
  • Grout renewal, and movement joints sealed rather than grouted
  • Decorative stone, tile, marble, granite and terrazzo installation
  • Cuban cement tile and historic terrazzo conservation care

Not ours, and why

  • Structural repair of a failed setting bed, slab or substrate
  • Waterproofing membranes below tile or stone on an occupied space
  • Drainage correction and regrading, which is construction
  • Stone cladding anchorage and facade panel refixing — engineering before trade
  • Pool shell, deck structure and coping replacement requiring a permit
  • Pool water chemistry and acid washing — coordinated, not performed
  • Anything requiring a permit beyond the exempt scopes

Where a job needs any of the second column, the licensed trade contracts directly with you. The most useful thing we do in those cases is identify the real cause early, because a terrace that keeps blooming white does not need cleaning again next year; it needs somebody to find the water.

Questions

What owners ask

There are dull marks on my marble that will not clean off. What are they?

Almost certainly etch rather than stain. Something acidic — citrus, wine, a soft drink, a bathroom cleaner — has reacted with the calcium carbonate and removed the polish in that spot. Nothing has been added to the stone, so no cleaner will help, and several of the products sold for the job are themselves acidic and make the patch larger. The test: wet it. An etch usually vanishes while wet and returns as it dries. It is corrected mechanically, by re-polishing the area or the field.

Will sealing my stone stop it staining and etching?

It helps with one and does nothing for the other. A penetrating sealer lines the pores so liquids bead rather than soak in, which buys you minutes or hours to wipe a spill instead of seconds. It is worth having and worth renewing. It will not stop etching at all, because etching is a chemical reaction with the surface of the stone and the sealer sits below that surface. A sealed marble counter etches from lemon juice exactly as an unsealed one does.

What is the white powder that keeps appearing on my keystone wall?

Efflorescence — salts carried through the masonry by moisture and left behind at the surface as the water evaporates. It brushes off dry and needs no chemistry. What matters is that it is a signal, not a problem in itself: it tells you water is moving through that wall and arriving at that face. If the water is not dealt with it returns indefinitely. The dangerous relative is subflorescence, where the crystals form just below the surface and push the face off in flakes, and on porous keystone that damage is permanent.

Can you pressure wash a stone terrace?

Not at the pressures used on a driveway, and on historic stone not at all. Keystone, coquina, soft limestone and travertine all have a cured outer surface that pressure removes along with the dirt, leaving a rougher, more porous face that soils faster than before. Owners often notice a terrace looks worse eighteen months after a hard wash and conclude it needs washing more often. The washing is the cause. Soft washing at controlled pressure with the right chemistry and adequate dwell time does the job without that trade.

Why does my travertine keep developing small holes?

Those are the stone's natural voids reopening, not damage. Travertine forms with holes in it and is supplied with them filled, and the fill is softer than the stone and comes out over time with traffic, cleaning and weather. It is normal and it is maintenance: the voids are refilled in matching material as part of a visit, rather than being a reason to resurface anything.

Do you need a licence for stone restoration in Florida?

No. Decorative stone, tile, marble, granite and terrazzo installation is named in Fla. Stat. 489.117(4)(a) as a job scope exempt from contractor licensing, and maintenance of existing stone — cleaning, honing, polishing, poulticing and sealing — was never within the contracting statute to begin with. A business tax receipt and current insurance apply. Work on the structure under the stone, drainage, waterproofing and anything requiring a permit are a different matter and are set out above.

Our pool coping has gone white and rough along one edge. What happened?

Most often pool chemistry. Muriatic acid used to adjust water or to remove scale from the waterline tile will attack a calcareous coping on contact, and splash-out from chemically unbalanced water does the same more slowly. Salt crystallisation can produce a similar look on porous stone. We would want to see it, test a small area, and talk to whoever handles the water — because refinishing the coping without changing what caused it buys a season.

Should my terrace be polished or honed?

Honed, in almost every case. Wet polished stone outdoors is a slip problem, and exterior walking surfaces are assessed for dynamic coefficient of friction precisely because of it. Honed, brushed and tumbled finishes exist for exterior use and they also hide wear far better, which matters on a surface that gets sand tracked across it daily. Where an owner wants more depth of colour outside, an enhancing penetrating sealer gives some of that without the shine.
