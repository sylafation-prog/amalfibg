Renovating a condominium unit, from the owner’s side

Almost everything written about condominium renovation in Florida is written for boards. This is written for the person who owns the unit and wants a new kitchen, a new floor or a wall moved, and who has just discovered that the slab under the floor is not theirs. The sequence that works here is not the sequence a mainland house follows, and the document that governs it is not the statute everyone quotes.

Talk through your unit The board’s side of compliance

Approval

Approval comes from the declaration, not the statute

This is the single most useful thing to understand before you ask anyone for permission, because it tells you which document to read and whom to ask.

Owners arrive expecting Chapter 718 of the Florida Statutes to set out when a board may approve or refuse interior work. It does not. Section 718.113 deals with the maintenance, alteration and improvement of common elements and association property. It imposes no general requirement that a board approve work inside the boundaries of a unit. What it does do, at subsection (3), is bar any alteration or addition that impairs the safety or structural integrity of the common elements — and that single sentence is where most of the real friction lives, because a surprising amount of what sits inside a unit is not inside the unit at all.

The document that actually controls your project is the declaration of condominium, together with the articles, the bylaws and whatever rules the board has adopted under them. The declaration defines the physical boundary of your unit — usually the undecorated surfaces of the perimeter walls, floor and ceiling, which means the finish is yours and the structure behind it is not. It also sets out the approval procedure, the insurance and indemnity the association can require, the hours work may be carried out, the lift that may be used, and the deposit that will be held. Two towers a hundred metres apart can have entirely different answers, because they have entirely different declarations.

So the first task is documentary, not practical. Obtain the declaration and every recorded amendment, the current rules, and the alteration packet if the building has one — ask the managing agent for it by name, because it is usually more specific than the declaration about what the board will want to see. Read the unit boundary definition first and the approval clause second.

The consequence is that there is no universal answer to “do I need board approval to replace my floor?” In many buildings it is yes for any flooring change above ground level, no for paint, and yes with engineering for anything touching a wall. In a small low-rise with a thin declaration nobody may need to be asked for a cabinet swap. An owner who has read the document is in a materially stronger position than one relying on what a neighbour did in 2019.

Documents to obtain before you plan anything

  • The declaration of condominium and all recorded amendments
  • Current rules and regulations, including work hours
  • The association’s alteration or modification application
  • The unit boundary definition — where your property stops
  • Insurance and indemnity requirements for vendors
  • Lift use, protection and deposit rules
  • Any flooring or sound policy the board has adopted
  • Whether the declaration requires a licensed contractor for all vendors

Boundaries

What is yours, and what belongs to the building

The boundary is not where owners assume it is. Six items decide most condominium renovation arguments before they start.

The slab

The structural floor slab is a common element in effectively every tower. You may take finishes off it and put new ones onto it. You may not core it, chase it, grind it to an uncontrolled depth or cut it to run a drain. A proposal that moves a shower or a floor waste almost always means cutting the slab, and that is where a straightforward bathroom becomes an engineering submission.

Risers and stacks

Vertical plumbing stacks, waste risers, vent stacks and the chases they occupy serve more than one unit and are therefore common element. The branch inside your own wall is generally yours. Moving a fixture any distance lengthens that branch and changes the fall, which is why buildings resist relocating toilets and why a licensed plumber is the only person who can say whether it works.

Windows and doors

In many declarations the glazing and the entry door are common element, even though you look through one and lock the other. Replacement is then an association decision rather than an owner decision, and in a high-velocity hurricane zone it carries product approval and permit obligations as well. Check the declaration before you price windows.

Partitions and drywall

Non-structural partitions inside the unit boundary are usually yours to remove, and this is the part of a condominium renovation that actually opens up a plan. The qualification is that nothing in a tower should be assumed non-structural on sight: shear walls, columns encased in drywall and post-tensioned elements all look like ordinary construction until an engineer says otherwise.

Air conditioning

The air handler inside the unit is normally the owner’s equipment and responsibility. The condenser location, the refrigerant routing through the building and any condensate connection are frequently common element or association-controlled. Mechanical work is reserved to a licensed mechanical or air-conditioning contractor in any event, so this belongs to a licensee from the start.

Hurricane protection

Section 718.113(5)(c) of the Florida Statutes is useful and not widely known: where a board has adopted specifications for hurricane shutters, impact glazing, code-compliant windows or doors or other hurricane protection, it may not refuse an owner’s installation that conforms to them. Conformity is the condition, so ask for the adopted specification in writing and meet it exactly.

Sound

The sound requirement, and why it stops most floor jobs

Hard flooring above ground level is the most commonly refused item in a condominium renovation, and it is refused on paper rather than on site.

  1. Find out what the building requires, in writingMost towers that permit hard flooring above the ground floor require a rated assembly, and the figures asked for in this market are an STC of 50 and an IIC of 50 — sound transmission class for airborne noise, impact insulation class for footfall. Some declarations set a higher number. Get the figure from the association before anything is specified: an underlayment that meets 50 and one that meets 55 are not the same product or the same price.
  2. Obtain the laboratory reports, not the marketing sheetThe association and the building department will want test data generated to the recognised laboratory methods — ASTM E90 for airborne sound transmission and ASTM E492 for impact insulation. A brochure claiming a number is not a test report. Check that the tested assembly resembles yours, with the same slab thickness, underlayment and finish, and keep the report in the submission file.
  3. Prepare a flooring limits planA simple drawing showing which rooms receive hard flooring and which stay carpeted or soft-finished. Many declarations restrict hard flooring in bedrooms, require a soft border, or exclude it from certain areas entirely. Pompano Beach’s interior renovation checklist asks for this plan as a submission item, so it serves the association and the building department at once.
  4. Get the association letterA letter from the association confirming it has approved the work and the flooring assembly. Several building departments here will not accept a condominium interior application without one, and it is the item most likely to hold up a permit, because it depends on a board meeting rather than a counter.
  5. Expect an in-progress inspectionThe Pompano Beach checklist provides for a sound barrier inspection while the work is open — after the underlayment is laid and before the finish goes down. Where it applies the sequence is not negotiable and the tile cannot go in over the weekend. Build it into the programme as a hold point.
  6. Do not cite a code section you have not readWe are not going to quote a code section number for the sound requirement, because it varies by jurisdiction and edition and we have not verified it for yours. Ask your building department which provision it applies, and the association which figure the declaration sets. Two questions asked before the floor is bought replace a great deal of argument later.

One honest observation about sound, from the other side of it. The complaints that follow a condominium floor replacement are almost never about the rating on the underlayment. They are about perimeter detail — a skirting board or a threshold making hard contact between the new finish and the structure, which bridges the isolation and carries footfall straight into the unit below. A correctly rated assembly installed with a rigid perimeter will fail in use while passing on paper. The detail at the wall is worth more attention than the specification sheet.

Asbestos

Asbestos in a tower is a regulated step, not a precaution

The exemption owners have heard about does not apply to a condominium building. This is the item most likely to stop a job that has already started.

Demolition and renovation of regulated facilities are governed federally by the asbestos NESHAP, 40 CFR Part 61 Subpart M. The exemption people remember — that residential buildings of four or fewer dwelling units are outside it — does not reach a condominium building. A tower is a regulated facility. That means the renovation of a unit inside it sits within a regulatory framework that a comparable house does not, regardless of how small the scope is.

The thresholds that trigger the formal obligations are 160 square feet of regulated asbestos-containing material on surfaces, or 260 linear feet on pipes. Below them the material still has to be handled and disposed of correctly; above them the process becomes formal. The route is a survey by a Florida-licensed asbestos consultant before anything is disturbed, and written notice to the regulator ten working days before work begins. Ten working days is a fortnight of real time, and it is a fortnight nobody has budgeted when the discovery is made on a Tuesday with the tiler booked for Thursday.

In Broward County, Code section 27-180 adds a county layer: an online statement of responsibilities before demolition or renovation, with a review fee of $100. It is a short filing and a hard stop if it has not been made. The two items that most often produce a positive result in an older South Florida tower are textured “popcorn” ceilings and the black mastic under old flooring, neither of which looks like a hazardous material to anyone standing in the room.

For single-family work the paperwork position is genuinely unresolved and we will not pretend otherwise. Homes of four or fewer units are federally exempt, but Broward appears to require the online statement for them at no fee, while a Fort Lauderdale interior demolition checklist states single-family work is exempt from it. Those two positions conflict, so confirm it with the county for your address rather than taking the answer from a website, including this one.

Questions to put to the county, in writing

  • Does this building fall within the asbestos NESHAP as a regulated facility
  • Which consultant certifications the county will accept for the survey
  • Whether the scope exceeds 160 square feet or 260 linear feet of regulated material
  • Whether a ten-working-day notification is required for this scope
  • Whether the Broward online statement of responsibilities is required, and the fee
  • For single-family work, whether the statement is required at all
  • What documentation the permit application must contain
  • What happens to the schedule if a positive survey comes back

The permit

Which licence can hold the permit, and above which storey

The licence class question is the one that decides whether your project is a counter transaction or a general contractor’s job, and it turns on the height of the building.

Florida’s licence classes are defined by what the licensee may touch and how large a building it may be touched in. Section 489.105(3)(b) describes the building contractor, and the Construction Industry Licensing Board’s declaratory statement DS 2014-021 reads it in the way that matters to a condominium owner: a building contractor may remodel a building of any size, but only where structural members are not touched. A residential contractor is out of scope above three storeys altogether. So the shape of the rule is this — above three storeys, work that touches a structural member needs a certified general contractor, and no lesser class will do.

This is why the same kitchen costs differently on the second floor of a low-rise and the twenty-second floor of a tower. Not because the cabinets differ, but because the moment the scope touches a structural member in a tall building the only lawful prime is a certified general contractor, and that is a different market with different overheads. It is also why owners are told a wall “cannot” come out. Frequently it can, with engineering and the right licensee on the permit; what cannot happen is a cheaper licensee holding it.

The exempt job scopes in section 489.117(4)(a) of the Florida Statutes have their own permit provision, and it is worth stating precisely because it is routinely overstated. A local government may not require a state or local licence as a condition of obtaining a permit for one of those listed job scopes. That removes a licensure barrier to applying. It does not remove the permit requirement, and it does not allow a permit to issue for a scope containing licensed work with an unlicensed applicant on the front page. Broward’s administrative provisions still frame applicant qualification around Chapter 489 at section 105.3.0.1.1, so the honest line is that the exempt-scope route is confirmed with the individual building department before anybody relies on it. Our page on the permit calendar covers the statutory review clocks and what House Bill 803 changed this year.

Where the scope needs a building permit for structural or wall changes, the permit is held by a licensed contractor or by the owner as a genuine owner-builder, and the conditions on that second route are heavier than owners are usually told. They are set out at the end of this page.

How the licence class question resolves

  • Finishes only, no structure, any height — exempt job scopes are available
  • Structural member touched, three storeys or under — building or residential class
  • Structural member touched, above three storeys — certified general contractor
  • Electrical, mechanical, plumbing, roofing, gas — a licensee in that trade, always
  • Window or door replacement — check the declaration first, it may not be yours
  • Any of it — verify the licence number with the state before signing
  • Exempt-scope permit applications — confirm with your building department

Two contracts

The two-contract structure, and when it is real

There is a lawful way to arrange a finish-only renovation alongside licensed trade work. It depends on four conditions, and without them it is decoration.

The structure is straightforward to describe. The owner signs one contract for the finish scope — the job scopes that section 489.117(4)(a) of the Florida Statutes places outside state contractor licensure — and separate, direct contracts with the licensed plumber and the licensed electrician. Each licensee pulls its own permit in its own name, and each answers to the owner for its own work. Nothing licensed appears in the finish contract, and nothing licensed appears in the finish price.

Four conditions make that real rather than cosmetic. First, the finish contract and its price contain no licensed task, no markup on the trades and no warranty of their work — if a plumbing line appears in that number, at any margin, the arrangement has collapsed. Second, the finish trade does not supervise the licensed trades, direct their schedule or pay them. Third, where the job needs a building permit for structural or wall changes, a licensed contractor or a genuine owner-builder holds it. Fourth, the split has to reflect what actually happens on site, because a regulator looks at substance rather than at paperwork.

Now the part nobody selling a kitchen volunteers. Without a licensed prime contractor over the whole job, that structure limits the work in practice to like-for-like kitchens and bathrooms — fixtures and appliances staying where they are, no walls moving, no slab cut, no new circuits beyond what the electrician contracts for directly. The moment a plan opens up, a shower relocates or a bathroom gains a second basin, the arrangement that works is a licensed prime holding the whole of it, and above three storeys with structure involved that means a certified general contractor.

It is worth knowing how an owner is protected either way. Section 489.128(1)(a) of the Florida Statutes provides that where no state licence is required for the scope of the work covered by a contract, the contractor is not unlicensed for the purpose of that section, and the test is applied as at the contract’s effective date. A clean finish-scope contract is therefore an enforceable contract with lien rights attached. One that quietly mixes licensed and exempt work is neither enforceable nor clear, and the party who usually discovers that is the owner.

The four conditions, in order of how often they fail

  • No licensed task, no markup on the trades, no warranty of their work
  • No supervision, no schedule direction, no payment of the licensees
  • A licensed contractor or genuine owner-builder on any structural permit
  • The split reflects what actually happens on site
  • And the consequence: like-for-like only, until a licensed prime is engaged

The work

The finish work inside a unit, done by our own people

Paint, flooring, tile and stone, cabinetry, plaster and caulking — and two things an owner should settle with the association before anybody starts.

Section 489.117(4)(a) of the Florida Statutes places a defined set of job scopes outside state contractor licensure: painting, flooring, cabinetry, interior remodeling where the project includes no task requiring a state licence, decorative stone, tile, marble, granite and terrazzo, plastering, stuccoing, caulking, pressure washing, driveway installation, handyman services, and canvas awning and ornamental iron installation. That list is our work inside a condominium unit, performed by our own people and to a specification we will write down before we start.

The qualifier on interior remodeling decides how a job is set up, so it is worth repeating. The entry is exempt when the scope of the project does not include a task for which a state licence is required, and the statute tests the project rather than the invoice line. A kitchen with one new appliance circuit in it is not an exempt project with a licensed line item; it is a project that requires a licence. Section 489.113(3) separately reserves electrical, mechanical, plumbing, roofing, sheet metal, pool and air-conditioning work to a licensee in that trade, which is why the owner contracts those directly, as set out above.

The first thing to settle with the association costs us work, and we would rather lose it honestly than find it in week six. Many declarations require that every vendor working in the building be a “licensed and insured contractor.” That is a private contractual requirement, and a statutory exemption does not override it. Read the clause carefully and ask the association in writing how it treats finish-trade vendors before engaging anybody. Some boards accept a finish trade carrying the right insurance and some do not, and it is a question with a one-line answer if you ask it early.

The second is the owner-builder route, which will be suggested to you at some point by somebody. Section 489.103(7) of the Florida Statutes lets an owner permit work on their own property, and the conditions are heavier than owners are told. The owner must personally appear and sign. The owner must supervise all work directly on site, and the statute says that responsibility may not be delegated to anyone other than a licensed person. Unlicensed workers must be the owner’s own employees, with tax withholding and workers’ compensation. Sale or lease within one year of completion raises a presumption that the permit was obtained in violation. And the statutory disclosure the owner signs warns, in its own words, that unlicensed people use these permits to conceal their involvement in a project. The legislature put that sentence on the form because the pattern is well established, and an owner is entitled to read it before signing.

Questions

What owners ask

Do I need board approval to renovate inside my condo unit?

It depends on your declaration, not on the statute. Section 718.113 of the Florida Statutes deals with common elements and imposes no general requirement that a board approve work inside a unit; what it does do, at subsection (3), is prohibit any alteration that impairs the safety or structural integrity of the common elements. The approval procedure comes from the declaration of condominium, the bylaws and the rules adopted under them. Read the unit boundary definition first and the approval clause second. In most towers flooring above ground level, anything touching a wall and anything touching plumbing all need approval, and paint does not.

Is the floor slab part of my unit?

Almost never. The structural slab is a common element in effectively every tower, and section 718.113(3) bars alterations that impair the safety or structural integrity of common elements. You may take finishes off the slab and put new ones onto it. Coring it, chasing it or cutting it to run a drain is a different matter, and it is why relocating a shower or a floor waste turns a straightforward bathroom into an engineering submission. The same applies to vertical plumbing stacks and waste risers, which serve more than one unit and are therefore common element.

Can my board refuse my hurricane windows or shutters?

Not if they conform to the specifications the board has adopted. Section 718.113(5)(c) of the Florida Statutes provides that a board may not refuse an owner’s installation of hurricane shutters, impact glass, code-compliant windows or doors or other hurricane protection conforming to the specifications the board has adopted. Conformity is the condition, so ask for the adopted specification in writing and meet it exactly. Note separately that in many declarations the glazing and the entry door are themselves common element, which makes replacement an association decision in the first place.

What sound rating does my condo need for hard flooring?

Ask the association, because the figure comes from the declaration and the board’s adopted policy rather than from one universal rule. The figures asked for in this market are an STC of 50 and an IIC of 50, supported by laboratory reports generated to ASTM E90 for airborne sound and ASTM E492 for impact insulation. A brochure claiming a number is not a test report. Several jurisdictions also want a flooring limits plan, a letter from the association confirming approval, and an in-progress sound barrier inspection after the underlayment is down. We do not quote a code section number for the sound requirement because we have not verified it for your jurisdiction — ask your building department which provision it applies.

Does an asbestos survey apply to a condo renovation?

Yes, and the exemption owners have heard about does not help. Condominium buildings are regulated facilities under the asbestos NESHAP at 40 CFR Part 61 Subpart M, and the exclusion for residential buildings of four or fewer dwelling units does not reach them. The formal thresholds are 160 square feet of regulated asbestos-containing material on surfaces or 260 linear feet on pipes, with a survey by a Florida-licensed asbestos consultant and written notice ten working days before work starts. Broward County Code section 27-180 adds an online statement of responsibilities and a $100 review fee. In older towers the two common positives are textured popcorn ceilings and the black mastic under old flooring.

Who has to hold the permit for a condo renovation?

It depends on the scope and on how tall the building is. For a finish-only scope within the job scopes listed in section 489.117(4)(a), a local government may not require a state or local licence as a condition of obtaining a permit — though that removes a licensure barrier to applying, not the permit requirement itself, and Broward’s administrative provisions still frame applicant qualification around Chapter 489, so confirm the route with your own building department. Where structural members are touched, section 489.105(3)(b) and declaratory statement DS 2014-021 mean a building contractor may remodel only where structure is untouched, a residential contractor is out of scope above three storeys, and above three storeys structural work needs a certified general contractor.

My declaration requires a licensed and insured contractor. Does the statutory exemption override that?

No, and it is worth being clear about even though it costs us work. A statutory licensure exemption tells you what the state requires. Your declaration is a private contractual instrument between you and the association, and it can require more. If it says every vendor in the building must be a licensed and insured contractor, read the clause carefully and ask the association in writing how it treats finish-trade vendors before engaging anyone. Some boards accept a finish trade carrying the right insurance and some do not, and it is a question with a one-line answer if you ask it early.

What does Amalfi take on inside a condominium unit?

Painting and decorative finishes, flooring, tile, decorative stone, marble, granite and terrazzo, cabinetry and finish installation, plaster and stucco repair, caulking and sealant renewal, and a like-for-like interior refinish — the job scopes section 489.117(4)(a) of the Florida Statutes places outside state contractor licensure, performed by our own people. We do not take on electrical, mechanical, plumbing, roofing, sheet metal, pool or air-conditioning work, which section 489.113(3) reserves to a licensee in that trade and which the owner contracts directly.
