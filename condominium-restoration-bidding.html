Condominiums · Tendering

Three prices that can actually be compared

Most restoration bids a board receives cannot be compared with each other, and the board usually cannot tell. The numbers differ because the assumptions differ — access, substrate repair, sealant depth, how much of the building is included at all — and the cheapest bid is frequently the one that assumed the least. This page is about how to stop that happening before the invitations go out.

Request a facade survey The whole repair programme

The rule

When you must bid, and when you need not

Section 718.3026 is short, widely cited and routinely misread in two directions at once.

Where an association’s payments for materials, equipment or services, on behalf of any condominium it operates, exceed 5 per cent of its total annual budget including reserves, it must obtain competitive bids. Contracts for services, and contracts for materials or equipment that will not be fully performed within a year, must be in writing. A facade restoration clears that threshold on nearly any association, so the requirement applies.

The first misreading is that the board must take the lowest bid. The statute says expressly that the association is not required to accept the lowest bid. Say so in the minutes when you do not, and say why. A board that has documented its reasoning is in a far stronger position with owners than one that took the lowest number because it felt it had to.

The second misreading runs the other way. Contracts for the services of attorneys, accountants, architects, community association managers, timeshare management firms, engineers and landscape architects are exempt from the bidding requirement. So the engineer who inspects your building and writes the report need not be competitively bid, while the contractor who performs the repairs that report recommends must be.

That asymmetry has a consequence boards should sit with. The engineering appointment determines the scope that every price is then measured against, and it is the appointment least likely to have been tested. It does not follow that it should be bid — professional services are properly selected on judgement and fit. It does follow that a board should know which of its two major appointments it actually competed.

The remaining exceptions: associations of ten or fewer units may opt out by a two-thirds vote of owners, including by proxy specifically stating the exception; contracts with association employees are exempt; the section does not limit the association’s ability to obtain products and services in an emergency; and it does not apply where the business is the only source of supply within the county. The emergency exception is genuine and is also the one most often stretched — a deadline the board has known about for eighteen months is not an emergency, and characterising it as one is the kind of decision that reads badly later.

Unit pricing

Ask for rates, not just a number

A lump sum tells a board what the work costs if the assumptions hold. Unit rates tell it what happens when they do not — which, on a thirty-year-old building, they will not.

ItemUnitWhy the rate matters
Sealant removal and replacement, perimeter jointsLinear foot, by joint width bandJoint width governs backer rod size and sealant volume. A rate quoted without width bands will be repriced the moment wider joints appear.
Expansion and control joint renewalLinear foot, by width bandDifferent detail, different materials, different labour from a window perimeter. Should never share a rate with one.
Stucco patch and substrate repairSquare foot, by depth bandThis is the single largest source of overrun on a facade contract. Depth bands — skim, partial, full to lath, lath replacement — are what make an increase in quantity a calculation instead of a negotiation.
Crack routing and fillingLinear foot, by width bandHairline against structural. Quantities on an old building are always discovered rather than surveyed exactly.
Coating applicationSquare foot, by coats and systemLets you compare a two-coat elastomeric against a three-coat acrylic rather than comparing two words.
Balcony edge and drip repairLinear foot, or eachA repeated element. A rate makes a lump sum verifiable by multiplication.
Railing post base repair and resetEachCounts change once the substrate is opened. A rate prevents each discovery becoming a change order conversation.
Access — swing stage, mast climber, lift, pipe scaffoldPer drop, per week, or per elevationPriced separately or buried. Buried is where bids diverge most and is almost never visible in a comparison.
Daily or weekly rate for directed extra workDay or week, by crew sizeThe rate that decides what every unforeseen item costs. Agree it before the contract, never during.

The purpose of unit rates is not to build the price up from them. It is to make the lump sum auditable and to fix in advance what a change costs. Nearly every dispute on a restoration contract is about quantities that turned out larger than surveyed, and the dispute is survivable where the rate was agreed and unpleasant where it was not.

Ask for the rates in the bid, not after award. A contractor who has priced the work properly has them already and will provide them. A contractor who will not provide them is telling you that the lump sum rests on assumptions they would rather not disclose, and that is useful information obtained cheaply.

Two further requests worth making in the invitation. First, a quantity schedule: have each bidder state the quantities they have priced against for the main items. Where three bidders state three different square footages of substrate repair against the same building, you have learned why the prices differ and you have learned it before signing. Second, the mobilisation and access assumptions in writing — which elevations, what method, how many drops, whether unit owners must clear balconies and for how long.

There is a reason to insist on the quantity schedule beyond comparability, and it concerns the 5 per cent rule. Where the association’s payments exceed 5 per cent of its total annual budget including reserves, competitive bids are required — and a board that has obtained three prices against three different sets of assumed quantities has arguably not obtained competitive bids at all, whatever the paperwork shows. It has obtained three estimates of three different jobs. The quantity schedule is what makes the competition real rather than formal.

A last request, which costs nothing and tells you a great deal: ask each bidder to state, in writing, what they would need to see before they would convert their lump sum into a fixed price with no substrate allowance. The answers separate contractors who have surveyed the building from contractors who have priced a type of building.

What the numbers look like

Orientation on cost, with its limits stated

Boards ask what this should cost before they ask anything else. There is now some public data and it is worth reading carefully, including for what it does not tell you.

In July 2026 OPPAGA published the first statewide milestone inspection data. Across 2024 and 2025 it recorded 903 repair permit applications, with values ranging from under $1,000 to $30 million, and reported average values of $337,229 and $496,236. Phase one inspections completed came to 8,736 and phase two inspections to 1,575.

Two things to take from that and one trap to avoid. The trap is the averages: a range running from under a thousand dollars to thirty million mixes a single balcony repair with full structural reconstruction, and an average across that distribution tells you almost nothing about any particular building. Do not take it to owners as a budget.

The first thing worth taking is the gap between 1,575 phase two inspections and 903 repair permit applications. Phase two is only reached where substantial structural deterioration was found, and that finding is what the repair obligation attaches to. Some of the gap has innocent explanations — unpermitted minor work, timing across year ends, incomplete reporting — but it runs the same way in both years and the honest reading is that a significant number of associations hold a finding and have not permitted a repair.

The second is that the data’s own limitations are substantial and the report states them. Only 71 per cent of jurisdictions reported for 2024 and 64 per cent for 2025, of 389; several municipalities in Broward, Miami-Dade and Palm Beach did not report for 2025. The form does not restrict entries to condominium and cooperative buildings, so other building types are in the figures. Some officials filed against the wrong year. Neither “unsafe” nor “uninhabitable” is defined in statute, and the 54 buildings so designated were classified by officials applying their own definitions.

Which leaves the only reliable answer to what it should cost: a quantified scope, priced by three contractors against the same quantities, with unit rates stated. That is not a dodge. It is the reason the quantification step is worth paying for.

Source: OPPAGA, Milestone Inspection Reporting Data 2024 and 2025, Report 26-04, July 2026. We cite it because it is the best public data that exists and because its limits are as informative as its figures — a board being shown a confident cost benchmark drawn from it should ask which 64 per cent of the state it came from.

Comparing

The inclusions that differ, and almost never surface

Work down this list against each bid before any of them is discussed as a number. Most of it is absent from most bids, and absence is a price difference.

  1. Access method and extentSwing stage, mast climber, boom lift, pipe scaffold or rope access — and which elevations each covers. The cheapest access method is often the one that cannot reach a return wall or a courtyard elevation, and the omission appears as a change order in month four. Ask which elevations the quoted access reaches and what is excluded.
  2. Substrate repair allowanceAlmost every bid contains an allowance for patching and almost none of them state it. Ask for the square footage assumed, the depth assumed, and what happens when it is exceeded. Where three bids assume 2,000, 6,000 and 14,000 square feet of repair, the prices are not comparable and the lowest may be the most expensive by completion.
  3. Sealant specification and joint preparationASTM C920 class and grade, whether backer rod is included, whether joints are being cut out to a proper dimension or merely topped over. Topping over an undersized joint is faster, cheaper and will fail again inside three years, and it is invisible in a line item reading “replace perimeter sealant”.
  4. Coating system, coats and film thicknessManufacturer, product, number of coats, wet and dry film thickness, and whether the primer is a dedicated primer or the first coat thinned. Two coats at 8 mils dry against three coats at 20 mils is a different building in ten years and a modest difference in bid.
  5. Warranty, and who stands behind itContractor workmanship warranty and manufacturer material warranty are different things with different durations and different triggers. A manufacturer system warranty generally requires certified application, specified film thicknesses and manufacturer inspection — so ask whether the bid includes the inspections the warranty depends on, because a warranty voided by undocumented application is not a warranty.
  6. Unit owner impact and working hoursBalcony clearing, how long owners lose use, window restrictions, noise hours, parking and lay-down areas, protection of landscaping and pool decks. Not strictly cost until it is: a contractor who has not planned it will plan it on site, at your owners' expense in goodwill and sometimes at yours in money.
  7. Protection, containment and clean-downPressure washing runoff, coating overspray, containment where surfaces are being prepared, and final clean-down of glass, railings and decks. Omitted routinely, and remediating overspray on a hundred balcony glass panels is a real number.
  8. Insurance, licence and the disclosure answerCertificates of insurance naming the association, workers' compensation, and for every permitted portion the licence it will be pulled under. Plus the written conflict-of-interest answer: does the bidder hold any direct or indirect interest in the firm that inspected your building, or is any relative of theirs an interested party? Required of them under HB 913 in any event, and the absence of an answer is itself an answer.

The invitation

What to send out, and in what order

The sequence matters more than the document. Most of the value is created before any contractor is contacted.

  1. Quantify first, invite secondSurvey and quantify the work — by elevation, by joint type, by substrate condition and depth, with counts of repeated elements and photographs keyed to location. Until that exists there is nothing for bidders to price against except their own judgement, and their own judgement will differ. This is the step that creates comparability and it is the step most often skipped.
  2. Write the specification, name the productsSealant to a stated ASTM C920 class and grade. Coating system by manufacturer and product, coats and film thickness. Surface preparation standard and substrate moisture limits. Where the board does not specify, each bidder specifies, and the cheapest specification wins a competition the board did not intend to run.
  3. Set a common bid formLump sum, plus the unit rate schedule, plus a stated quantity schedule, plus the inclusions checklist above answered item by item. Identical for every bidder. This single document does more to produce comparable prices than anything else in the process.
  4. Invite a sensible number, and vet them firstThree to five bidders who have each done this work on a building of this height and this envelope in this climate. Ask for two association references from the past three years and actually telephone them. More bidders produces more paper, not better information.
  5. Hold one site walk for everyone, togetherOne walk, all bidders present, questions answered in front of all of them, and every question and answer circulated in writing afterwards. Separate walks produce separate understandings and an argument about what was said. It also takes less of the manager's time.
  6. Compare on the schedule, not the cover pagePut the bids side by side on the inclusions checklist and the quantity schedule before anyone looks at the totals. The conversation that follows is about scope, which is a conversation a board can have well. The conversation that starts from totals is about price, which is a conversation it usually cannot.
  7. Document the award, especially a non-lowest oneThe statute does not require the lowest bid. Minute what was compared, which inclusions differed, and why the selected bid was selected. Owners will ask, and in eighteen months a different board will ask. A recorded reason is worth a great deal and costs a paragraph.

Where we fit

Which side of this we are on

Plainly, because it bears on how to read anything on this page.

We do two separable things. We survey and quantify facade, sealant and coating work and write the specification — and we perform waterproofing, caulking, coating, exterior painting, stone and architectural metalwork. A board can engage us for the first and tender the second competitively, including against us, and some do. We think that is a reasonable arrangement and we are willing to say so on a page intended to help boards tender well.

What we do not do: milestone inspections, reserve studies, structural repair, roofing, window and door replacement, or any permitted trade, each of which is contracted by the association directly with a contractor licensed in that trade. On a condominium above three storeys structural work requires a certified general contractor, and the association should contract that party directly.

And we hold no interest in any inspecting or engineering firm, so we never bid on findings we produced. That is a matter of what we are, not a policy we adopted.

Two practical consequences for a board reading our survey. The quantities in it are the quantities we would price against ourselves, so they are not inflated to create scope — a survey that overstates substrate repair makes our own bid less competitive, not more. And the specification names products and film thicknesses, which means a rival bidder can price it exactly and the board can see whose number is lower on identical work.

If a board would rather have the quantification done by a party with no interest in the work at all, that is a reasonable preference and a quantity surveyor or the engineer under an expanded appointment can do it. We would rather say so than pretend the question does not arise.

Questions

What owners ask

Do we have to competitively bid a facade restoration?

In almost all cases yes. Under Fla. Stat. 718.3026, where an association's payments for materials, equipment or services exceed 5 per cent of its total annual budget including reserves, competitive bids must be obtained, and contracts for services must be in writing. A restoration clears that threshold on nearly any association. Exceptions: associations of ten or fewer units may opt out by a two-thirds owner vote including by proxy specifically stating the exception; contracts with association employees; genuine emergencies; and where the business is the only source of supply within the county.

Must we accept the lowest bid?

No. Section 718.3026 states expressly that the association is not required to accept the lowest bid. What a board should do is document the comparison and the reasoning — which inclusions differed between bids, what quantities each assumed, and why the selected bid was selected. Owners will ask, and a future board will ask. A recorded reason costs a paragraph in the minutes and is worth a great deal later.

Does the engineer have to be competitively bid?

No. The statute exempts contracts for the services of attorneys, accountants, architects, community association managers, timeshare management firms, engineers and landscape architects from the competitive bidding requirement. So the inspecting engineer may be single-sourced while the repair contractor must be bid. Since the engineering appointment defines the scope everything else is priced against, boards should at least be conscious that it is the appointment least likely to have been tested.

What unit rates should we ask for?

At minimum: sealant removal and replacement per linear foot by joint width band; expansion and control joint renewal per linear foot by width band; stucco patch and substrate repair per square foot by depth band; crack routing and filling per linear foot by width band; coating application per square foot by system and number of coats; balcony edge and drip repair; railing post base repair and reset, each; access by drop, week or elevation; and a daily or weekly rate for directed extra work by crew size. The point is not to build the price up from rates but to make the lump sum auditable and to fix what a change costs before the contract rather than during it.

What does a condominium restoration cost?

There is no reliable general answer and boards should be wary of anyone who offers one. OPPAGA's July 2026 report recorded 903 milestone repair permit applications across 2024 and 2025 ranging from under $1,000 to $30 million, with reported averages of $337,229 and $496,236 — but an average across a range that wide mixes a single balcony repair with full structural reconstruction and should not be taken to owners as a budget. Only 71 per cent of jurisdictions reported for 2024 and 64 per cent for 2025, and the data includes building types other than condominiums. A quantified scope priced by three contractors against the same quantities is the only figure worth relying on.

Why do three bids for the same building differ so much?

Usually because they are not bids for the same work. The commonest divergences are the substrate repair allowance, which almost every bid contains and almost none states; the access method and which elevations it actually reaches; whether sealant joints are being cut out to a proper dimension or topped over; and the coating system, number of coats and film thickness. A bid that assumed 2,000 square feet of substrate repair and a bid that assumed 14,000 are different propositions with different prices, and nothing on either cover page reveals it. A stated quantity schedule and a common inclusions checklist make the difference visible before award rather than after.

What should we ask about warranties?

Separate the contractor's workmanship warranty from the manufacturer's material or system warranty; they have different durations, different triggers and different people standing behind them. A manufacturer system warranty generally requires certified application, specified film thicknesses and manufacturer inspection during the work. So the question is whether the bid includes those inspections and the documentation they generate, because a warranty voided by undocumented application is not a warranty. Ask for a specimen warranty document rather than a sentence in a bid.

Should the firm that quantifies the scope also bid the work?

It is a reasonable question to put to anyone, including us. Our view is that a board can sensibly engage one party to survey, quantify and specify, and then tender the work competitively — including to that party, with the comparison and the award documented. What matters is that the board knows which arrangement it has and has tested the price against a scope it controls. Separately and as a matter of statute following HB 913, any bidder must disclose in writing a direct or indirect interest in, or a relative's interest in, the firm that performed the inspection or reserve study, and an undisclosed relationship can render the contract voidable.
