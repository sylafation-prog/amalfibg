Hurricane preparation for rooftop mechanical plant

Tie-downs, the pre-season programme, and what insurers expect. The season opens on 1 June and the National Hurricane Center puts the climatological peak at 10 September, which makes April the month for this work.

Published 30 October 2026 · reviewed against primary sources

How is rooftop HVAC equipment actually secured to a building?

Through a continuous load path: the unit is positively fastened to a curb, rail or dunnage frame, that support is fastened to the roof structure, and the structure carries the load. Weight is not an attachment method; nor is friction.

In practice, a prefabricated curb matched to the unit's footprint, fastened into structural members rather than deck alone, with the unit bolted where the manufacturer nominates. For anchors set to the membrane rather than through it, FM Approvals publishes FM 4481, Anchors for Roof Mounted Equipment, issued 22 July 2022.

Outside the High-Velocity Hurricane Zone, Florida Building Code Section 1510.10 requires roof-mounted mechanical units to sit on curbs raised at least 8 inches above the roof. Inside the HVHZ, Section 1522.2 is blunter: all rooftop equipment and supports shall be secured to the structure per the loading requirements of Chapter 16, and wood sleepers are not permitted.

The loads come from ASCE 7, which the 8th Edition (2023) Code adopted at ASCE 7-22 and the 9th Edition carries forward from 31 December 2026. Rooftop equipment is treated at Section 29.4.1, yielding a lateral force and a simultaneous uplift. ASCE 7-16 removed the old 60-foot height cut-off, so uplift now applies at any building height.

What does the High-Velocity Hurricane Zone mean for rooftop equipment?

HVHZ means Miami-Dade and Broward counties, and nowhere else. Inside those two the design wind speeds are higher, the approved-product route is narrower, and prohibitions apply that do not exist elsewhere in the state.

The jurisdiction is defined at Section 202. It does not reach Palm Beach County to the north or Monroe County to the south, which surprises anyone assuming the Keys must be the strictest place in Florida to build.

Section 1620.2 sets the 3-second gust speeds used in structural calculations: in Miami-Dade, 175 mph for Risk Category II and 186 mph for Categories III and IV; in Broward, 170 and 180 mph. Section 1620.1 sends the designer to Chapters 26 through 31 of ASCE 7; Section 1620.6 covers rooftop equipment.

Three provisions catch out an unreassessed roof. Table 1522.3 sets minimum support heights by equipment width, from 14 inches up to 48 inches at 61 inches and wider. Section 1522.3.4 requires conduit and service lines raised at least 8 inches. Section 1522.3.5 forbids condensate draining onto the roofing system. Equipment swapped out without a permit breaches all three.

Does a unit set down in 1998 and never reassessed still comply?

Almost certainly not against today's numbers, and in most cases it does not have to be: existing equipment is not retroactively required to meet the current code. Whether the attachment will hold is another question.

The code reaches old plant at replacement, at relocation, when the roof beneath it is replaced, and on any permitted work. Section 553.844(5), Florida Statutes, settles the roofing side: where 25 percent or more of a roof permitted under the 2007 code or later is repaired, only the repaired portion must meet current code.

So the legal exposure is usually small; the physical exposure is not. The commonest finding is not an old unit but an undocumented changeout — a newer unit on an older curb built for a different footprint, shimmed and sealed, with no calculation and no permit record.

What is a Notice of Acceptance, and why does an engineer's letter matter in a claim?

A Notice of Acceptance is a Miami-Dade County product approval for a product as tested. An engineer's letter is the site-specific document saying this unit, on this curb, on this building, resists the loads the code requires. The first is about the product; the second is about your roof.

Statewide approval runs under Section 553.842, Florida Statutes. Subsection (5)(a) sets four routes — a certification mark, a test report from an approved laboratory, a product evaluation report, or a report from a Florida-licensed engineer or architect — with the detail in Rule 61G20-3. Subsection (8)(a) names the Miami-Dade County Product Control Division as an approved evaluation entity, which is why NOAs and FL numbers travel side by side here.

HVHZ approvals are tested to the Test Protocols for High-Velocity Hurricane Zones: TAS 202 for uniform static air pressure, with TAS 201 and TAS 203 for missile impact and cyclic loading where the envelope is involved. Rooftop equipment is generally approved for structural load rather than impact; Section 1626 carries exemptions, and an impact rating is the building official's call.

The insurance consequence is the point. After a displaced unit, the insurer's engineer asks what it was rated to resist and how it was attached. A product approval answers the first; only a site-specific letter answers the second.

The walk

What does the pre-season inspection actually cover?

Nine items, in this order, each photographed and dated as it is found.

  1. Curbs and flashingCurb top level and square to the unit, counterflashing lapped and terminated rather than caulked, no sealant doing structural work. Look hardest at a reflashed area.
  2. Fasteners and corrosionEvery anchor accounted for against the manufacturer's pattern, no sheared shanks under a cap. Plated steel on a beachfront roof is a consumable, not a fixing.
  3. The unit's own envelopeAccess panels latched, hinges sound, hail guards and coil grilles secure. A panel that leaves the unit at 120 mph is a projectile with your serial number on it.
  4. Condensate and drainagePans sloped, lines flushed, traps charged, terminations off the membrane. Drains and overflows cleared the same visit — a blocked drain turns a wind event into a water event.
  5. Louvres, dampers and hoodsDampers stroking and closing fully, actuators exercised, hoods and bird screens fastened. A damper that will not close is an open hole when the pressure differential arrives.
  6. Refrigerant lines and supportsSupported at the manufacturer's intervals, insulation unbroken, runs clear of the membrane. A whipping line work-hardens a braze joint until it opens, presenting later as a slow loss of charge.
  7. Disconnects, conduit and sealsDisconnects labelled and operating, enclosures rated and closed, conduit raised, penetrations sealed. Confirm which units sit on standby power.
  8. Penetrations and the roof itselfPitch pockets, pipe boots, exhaust curbs, antenna bases, lightning protection, fall-arrest anchors, and the membrane laps around each. Record the roof's age.
  9. Loose itemsFilters, offcuts, timber, ladders, duct sections, pavers, walkway pads, abandoned equipment. Anything unfastened is ammunition.

Underwriting and claims

What do insurers actually expect before a hurricane?

A record. Not an endorsement and not a certificate — a dated service history showing the equipment was maintained and the attachments inspected before the storm, producible within days of a loss.

Florida law gives you less here than people assume. The statutory windstorm-mitigation discount machinery — Section 627.711, Florida Statutes, and the uniform mitigation verification inspection form issued under it — is written for personal lines residential property. On commercial property there is no equivalent entitlement, so credits, deductibles and wind sublimits respond to evidence rather than to a form.

The photographs matter more than people expect. Date-stamped images from the same fixed positions each season, covering every unit and every attachment, are the cheapest evidence in the building — they establish pre-loss condition without an expert.

The documentation pack

  • Equipment schedule — make, model, serial, age, tonnage, refrigerant
  • Dated service records naming the technician and the tasks
  • Pre-season inspection report with photographs, dated before 1 June
  • Engineer's letter on attachment and wind resistance
  • Permit records, NOA or FL numbers for every replacement
  • Roof age, condition report and warranty
  • Refrigerant leak repairs and verification tests
  • Generator and life-safety test logs

What separates storm damage from deferred maintenance in a claim?

Standard commercial property forms cover direct physical loss from windstorm but exclude wear and tear, rust, corrosion, decay and deterioration. So the argument after a hurricane is rarely whether the storm happened. It is which part of the damage the storm caused, decided on evidence of condition before the wind arrived.

Three patterns account for most disputes. A coil with eight years of chloride pitting is damaged further by debris, and the insurer apportions. A curb whose anchors had already corroded fails below the design load, and the insurer puts the failure in the fixing. Interior water enters, and coverage turns on whether wind first opened the envelope — the windstorm and hail peril in the basic form excludes interior damage from rain unless wind or hail first damages the roof or walls.

A dated April inspection changes that: the deferred-maintenance narrative has to be argued against a document rather than asserted into a vacuum. Two things then decide the number. Depreciation, since a unit at the end of its life settles on actual cash value terms unless replacement cost coverage says otherwise. And code upgrade, since a replacement must be anchored to current loads and, in the HVHZ, raised to the clearances in Table 1522.3 — ordinance or law coverage decides who pays that.

When do you shut rooftop equipment down, and when do you let it run?

Run the plant while the building is occupied and the roof is safe to reach, then shut it down in a deliberate, recorded sequence at the local disconnect — rather than leaving the utility to do it for you, under load, with nothing written down.

Seventy-two hours out. Fuel, filters and batteries confirmed; the generator exercised under load and the transfer switch operated. NFPA 110, the standard for emergency and standby power systems, builds its regime on weekly inspection and monthly load exercising, thirty minutes minimum for a diesel set.

Forty-eight to twenty-four hours out. Strip the roof: anything unfastened goes inside, including what lives up there permanently without being secured. Condensate lines flushed and drains cleared again. Dampers driven closed where the controls allow, hoods secured, positions recorded. This is the last reasonable point to send anyone onto a roof.

The shutdown. De-energise at the unit disconnect, not at the thermostat or the building management system, so nothing restarts remotely or on a returning utility feed. Photograph each disconnect open with its label legible. Do not tie fan blades: a restrained fan can shear a shaft if the unit is energised before the tie is found.

What stays running. Smoke control, stair pressurisation and generator-room ventilation serve life safety, and taking any of them out of service belongs with the fire official and the engineer of record. Where a building is to remain occupied, the cooling plant is part of that case and runs through. A compressor short-cycling behind a generator with a weak voltage profile is a common cause of post-storm failure — confirm time-delay restart.

How do you restart rooftop equipment after a hurricane?

Document first, inspect second, dry and test third, energise last. A unit that has been flooded or salt-sprayed is not switched on to see whether it works. That converts a repairable claim into a replacement, and puts someone in front of equipment whose insulation may no longer be insulation.

Photograph before anything is touched. Wide shots that place the damage in the building, then each unit, then the attachment detail, then the debris where it landed. Debris moved before it is photographed is debris whose origin can be argued.

Then the clock, which runs under Sections 627.70132 and 627.70131, Florida Statutes, set out below. A governor's declaration of a state of emergency does not by itself toll those deadlines — only an order from the Office of Insurance Regulation does.

Electrical before mechanical. NEMA CS 70006-2026, Evaluating Water-Damaged Electrical Equipment, formerly NEMA GD 1, lists water-damaged motors, moulded-case circuit breakers, switches and fuses for replacement rather than reconditioning, and notes that salt water residue is both corrosive and conductive. Insulation resistance on windings is measured, not judged by eye.

Then the mechanical side. Coils and cabinets rinsed with potable water to carry the chloride off before it dries in place; drain pans cleared, bearings, belts and blower wheels inspected.

A refrigerant circuit is a repair, not a top-up. Under EPA's Section 608 rules at 40 CFR Part 82, Subpart F, appliances holding 50 pounds or more of regulated refrigerant must have leaks repaired once the annual leak rate passes the trigger — 10 percent for comfort cooling, 20 for commercial refrigeration, 30 for industrial process — within thirty days, with verification tests.

Energise last, with a written note of who inspected what and when, and then photograph the unit running.

Questions

Questions property managers ask about storm season

When should hurricane preparation for rooftop HVAC start?

February for engineering and capital decisions, March for ordering, April for the structural walk, May for the mechanical walk, documentation closed before 1 June. The constraint is the supply chain, not the weather.

Does rooftop mechanical equipment need a Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance?

Inside the High-Velocity Hurricane Zone — Miami-Dade and Broward counties only — rooftop equipment is expected to carry an approval demonstrating resistance to the HVHZ design loads: a Notice of Acceptance from the Miami-Dade County Product Control Division, or a statewide Florida Product Approval under Section 553.842, Florida Statutes.

Should rooftop air conditioning be shut down before a hurricane?

Yes, once the building is no longer occupied and the roof is no longer safe to reach — and at the unit disconnect rather than the thermostat, so it cannot restart remotely or when the utility returns. Life-safety systems stay in service.

Why can a flooded or salt-soaked rooftop unit not simply be switched back on?

Because water and salt change the equipment rather than merely wetting it. NEMA CS 70006-2026, formerly NEMA GD 1, lists water-damaged motors, moulded-case circuit breakers, switches and fuses for replacement rather than reconditioning.

How long do I have to report hurricane damage to rooftop equipment in Florida?

Under Section 627.70132, Florida Statutes, notice must be given within one year after the date of loss and a supplemental claim within eighteen months; for a hurricane the date of loss is the date of landfall. Section 627.70131 then gives the insurer seven days to acknowledge, thirty to inspect and sixty to pay or deny.
