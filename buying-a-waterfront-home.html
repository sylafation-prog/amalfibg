Home · Estates · Buying a waterfront home

Estates · New owners

You have bought the house. Now someone has to run it.

An inspection report tells you the condition of a house on one morning, so that you can decide whether to close on it. It is not a manual and was never meant to be. What an owner needs afterwards is a different document entirely: what is in the house, where it is, who made it, and when it was last touched. On the water that gap is wider than it looks.

Two different documents

What does a home inspection report actually tell you?

It records what a licensed inspector could see on one visit, so that a buyer can decide whether to proceed. It does not tell you what the house needs, on what interval, or where any of it is.

Those are two jobs, and only one of them is finished at closing. The report is a decision document with a short life; once the sale completes it stops being useful, because it answered a question that is no longer being asked. What should replace it is an operating record, and almost nobody creates one. So the first year of ownership is spent rediscovering the house by accident, generally on the evening something stops working.

None of this is a criticism of inspectors. A Florida home inspection is a licensed activity with a defined scope, and a good inspector does that scope well. It simply ends where ownership begins.

The difference

What does a waterfront house need that an inland house does not?

Three things change on the water. The air is corrosive, there is a structure holding the land up, and there is equipment at the house that does not exist a mile inland.

The air

Salt aerosol carries well past the waterline and works on everything exposed to it: condenser coils, fasteners, hinges, rails, light fittings, gate hardware, anything plated. The difference between 316 and 304 stainless in a single fixing matters here in a way it does not in Orlando. What salt does to a coil is the clearest version of the problem, because the damage is measurable and the remedy is cheap and routine.

The water’s edge

Seawall and cap, the tiebacks behind them, the soil they retain, dock piles and decking, the boat lift with its cables and motors, shore power pedestals and the protection on them. These are frequently the most expensive structures on the property and the least visible, and most of them sit outside the scope of a standard home inspection.

What is only here

A generator and its transfer switch. A residential lift, often with its pit below the flood line. Impact glazing whose hardware needs adjusting to keep doing its job. Pool equipment running hard twelve months a year. Irrigation drawing from a well with its own iron and salt to deal with. Each is a service interval somebody has to own.

The handover

Why does the knowledge about a house disappear at closing?

Because it was never written down. It lived with the seller, and with whichever trades they happened to call.

The seller knew which breaker fed the dock. They knew the north air handler drips if the pan is not cleared, that the lift was rebuilt three years ago and roughly by whom, that the irrigation on the south lawn has never worked properly. None of that is in the report, none of it conveys with the deed, and nobody thinks to ask for it while the lawyers are busy.

The service history does exist, but it is scattered across five or six separate companies, none of whom knows the buyer’s name. Some of them will not come back for a new owner at all. The practical window for recovering any of it is the first month, while the trades still remember the address and the permit record is easy to pull.

The first month

What should a new owner do in the first thirty days?

Find the shutoffs, collect whatever the seller left behind, clear the services that lapsed during the sale, have the water’s edge looked at, and set the calendar before the first season rather than during it.

  1. Find and label every shutoffMain water and each zone, gas at the meter and at each appliance, the pool and irrigation feeds, the main panel and every sub-panel, the generator transfer switch. Photograph each one in place. Nobody should be learning where the main is while standing in two inches of water.
  2. Collect what the seller leftManuals, warranty papers, permits and final inspections, pool and lift service tickets, alarm and automation account details. What was not handed over at closing can usually still be recovered in the first month from the permit record or the original installer, and rarely after that.
  3. Clear what lapsed during the saleFilters, coil cleaning, generator service and load test, backflow testing, pool equipment, the lift’s annual. Maintenance almost always stops once a house goes under contract, because nobody wants to pay for the next owner’s service.
  4. Have the water’s edge examinedSeawall and cap, settlement or voids in the ground behind it, pile condition, lift cables and motors, shore power. Marine and seawall work is its own trade with its own licensing, and it is worth a specialist rather than a generalist.
  5. Rekey and reset accessLocks, keypad codes, gate and garage remotes, alarm and camera accounts, network credentials. Every trade, every showing and every stager had access to this house in the months before you did.
  6. Set the calendarWhat happens monthly, what happens quarterly, what has to be finished before June, and who performs each item. Written down and agreed in advance, rather than assembled in a hurry when a storm is named.

The record

What is an opening record, and how is it different from an inspection?

It is an inventory of what is in the house and the schedule the house should run on. It is not an opinion about whether the house is sound, and it does not replace an inspection.

The distinction matters, and not only legally. An inspection answers should I buy this. An opening record answers how is this run. The second question is the one an owner lives with for the next decade, and it is the one nobody is handed an answer to.

What it contains

  • Every system photographed where it stands, with make, model and serial
  • Filter sizes, belt sizes, lamp and cartridge types, so nobody is measuring in an attic at seven in the evening
  • Shutoff, panel and meter locations, labelled and photographed
  • Whatever service dates can be established, from tickets, stickers, permit records and the installing trades
  • Warranty terms and expiry dates wherever the paperwork survives
  • The manufacturer’s maintenance interval for each item
  • The trades who installed or last serviced each system, with contact details

What it is not

  • It carries no professional opinion about the condition of the house, its structure or its systems
  • It is not a home inspection. In Florida that is a licensed activity under Chapter 468, with a defined scope and a licensed inspector behind it
  • It is not a substitute for one, and a buyer should have a proper inspection before closing regardless
  • It is not a valuation, a structural report, or an insurance survey

What makes it worth building is that it compounds. Every visit afterwards adds to it, so by the second year there is an actual history rather than a memory, and the question when was that last done has an answer with a date and a photograph attached. When the house eventually sells again, the next owner inherits the thing this one never got.

Questions

Quick answers

Four questions buyers ask on the water.

Do I still need a home inspection when buying a waterfront property?

Yes, and it should be performed by a Florida-licensed home inspector. It is also worth commissioning the seawall, dock and boat lift separately from marine specialists, because those structures usually fall outside the scope of a standard home inspection and they are among the most expensive things on the property.

What gets missed most often on a waterfront purchase?

The water’s edge. Attention goes to the house, while the seawall, the ground retained behind it, the piles and the lift receive a glance. A seawall failure is a six-figure problem that announces itself slowly, and the early signs are settlement and voids behind the cap rather than anything visible on the wall itself.

How quickly does salt air cause damage?

Corrosion on exposed fasteners and condenser coils becomes visible within a season or two once rinsing stops. Routine fresh-water rinsing of exposed equipment and specifying the correct stainless grade at installation both do more than any coating applied afterwards.

Does a seller have to hand over service records and manuals?

No. Anything not written into the contract is a courtesy. Ask for manuals, permits, warranty paperwork and the names of the trades who serviced the house before closing, while there is still something to trade for them.
