Historic & landmark houses

A house built in 1925 on the ocean is not a modern house that happens to be old. Its stucco is lime and it is meant to breathe. Its walls are hollow tile. Its windows are steel. Its roof tile was fired before the Depression and cannot be bought again. Most of the damage we are asked to look at on houses like these was not done by the weather. It was done by somebody maintaining them the way a new house is maintained.

The work itself

What historic house care actually is

The national standards recognise four separate treatments of a historic building. They are not interchangeable, and knowing which one a house needs is most of the job. Ours is the first of them, and the first one is the one nobody sells.

  1. Preservation

    Keeping what is there. Repair rather than replacement, retention of original material and finishes, and a continuous cycle of cleaning, monitoring and small intervention so that the fabric never reaches the point of needing to be rebuilt. It is almost entirely maintenance, it is unglamorous, and it is the cheapest thing an owner of a historic house will ever buy.

    This is what we do

  2. Rehabilitation

    Adapting a historic building to a contemporary use while retaining the features that make it historic — a kitchen brought into this century, a service wing converted, air conditioning threaded through a house that never had any. Permitted work, drawn by an architect and carried out by licensed trades.

  3. Restoration

    Returning a building to its appearance at a particular moment in its history, which usually means removing later additions and recreating what was lost. Specialist work, slow, and reviewed closely by the authority with jurisdiction.

  4. Reconstruction

    Rebuilding something that no longer exists, from documentary evidence. Rare, and almost never the right answer for a private house.

The discipline that sits underneath preservation is called cyclical maintenance: a written schedule of recurring tasks against each element of the building, on monthly, quarterly, annual and multi-year cycles, carried out by the same people, recorded every time. It is the model the National Park Service has recommended to stewards of historic buildings for decades, and it happens to be exactly how we run every estate on our calendar — the difference on a historic house is not the system, it is the method and the materials used within it.

There is a second reason to keep the record, beyond the condition of the house. When an owner eventually does need to go before a landmarks board, apply for a tax abatement, argue an insurance claim, or sell, the single most useful document they can hold is a dated photographic history of the building's fabric and everything that has been done to it. Almost nobody has one. We build it from the first visit, and it belongs to the owner whether or not we continue.

We are not architects, preservation consultants or tax advisers, and we do not pretend to be. We are the people who keep the building between the times those professionals are needed, and who know enough not to quietly destroy it in the process.

On a historic house we record

  • Every elevation photographed from fixed positions, each visit
  • Stucco, render and masonry condition, mapped and dated
  • Mortar, patch and coating materials already present
  • Roof tile type, count of slipped or broken units, ridge condition
  • Window and door type, glazing, ironmongery, operation
  • Rust staining and crack lines, traced and measured over time
  • Timber elements, species where identifiable, coating age
  • Floors and interior finishes, with the products used on them
  • Every anchor, bracket and fixing driven into historic fabric
  • What was cleaned, with what, at what pressure, on what date

What the house is made of

Four kinds of old house, and what each is built from

South Florida's historic housing stock is narrow and well defined. Nearly every landmarked or period house between Palm Beach and Naples belongs to one of four families, and each one fails in its own way.

Period and typeWhereBuilt from
Mediterranean Revival, 1918–1935 Palm Beach, Boca Raton, Coral Gables, Naples Hollow clay tile or poured concrete walls under soft lime-based stucco; barrel and mission clay roof tile, much of it imported or salvaged; cast stone and coral keystone surrounds; pecky cypress ceilings and beams; wrought iron; steel casement windows; Cuban cement tile and terrazzo floors.
Frame vernacular, 1890–1930 Old Fort Lauderdale, Delray, Coconut Grove, the Keys Dade County pine framing, siding and floors — old-growth heart pine so dense it blunts tools and resists termites; metal roofs; wood shutters and louvres; deep porches with tongue-and-groove decking and ceilings; brick or tabby piers.

Why it fails here

Salt gets inside the building, not just on it

The same house in Charleston or Santa Barbara ages slowly. Two hundred yards from the Atlantic it is in a chemical environment, and the deterioration runs from the inside out.

What happens, in order

  1. Chloride-laden air arrives off the water, every day, whether it rains or not.
  2. It passes through the porous lime stucco and reaches the steel inside the wall.
  3. The steel corrodes. Rust occupies several times the volume of the metal it came from.
  4. The masonry has nowhere to give, so it splits — from the inside out.
  5. The crack and the rust bloom finally show at the face, years after it began.
  • 1Lime stucco — soft, permeable, and the sacrificial layer by design
  • 2Hollow clay tile or poured concrete body
  • 3Embedded steel lintel, reinforcement or casement frame
  • 4Straight cracks running from the ends of the steel to the face
  • 5Rust bloom in the stucco, following the line of the bar
I

Rust jacking in the walls

The defining failure of 1920s Florida concrete. Airborne chloride works through stucco and concrete to the steel inside — lintels, reinforcement, casement frames, balcony rails. The steel corrodes, and rust occupies several times the volume of the metal it came from, so it splits the masonry outward from within. By the time you see the crack, the damage is years old. The early sign is a rust-coloured bloom in the stucco following a straight line, and that line is the steel.

II

Salt crystallising under the surface

Salt carried into porous stucco, coral keystone and cast stone by spray and rain crystallises as it dries. The crystals grow in the pores just under the face of the material and push the skin off in flakes. Rinsing the surface with fresh water removes the salt before it goes in; sealing it in does the opposite.

III

Water that cannot get back out

A lime-rendered wall is designed to take water in and give it back. Put a modern impermeable coating over it and the water still gets in — around windows, through hairline cracks, up from the slab — but it can no longer leave, so it migrates until it finds the weakest point and takes the face of the wall with it when it goes.

IV

Ultraviolet and the ocean side

Coatings, sealants and timber on the east and south elevations of an oceanfront house age at several times the rate of the sheltered sides. Treating a building as one surface with one repaint cycle wastes money on three elevations and under-protects the fourth.

V

Roof tile outliving its underlayment

A good clay barrel tile may last a century or more. The felt beneath it will not — that is the part doing the waterproofing, and it is a consumable. Original tile is almost always worth lifting, cataloguing and relaying over new underlayment rather than replacing, and on a designated property relaying salvaged tile is usually the only treatment that will be approved anyway.

VI

Steel windows seizing and swelling

Original steel casements corrode first in the glazing rebate, where old putty has shrunk and lets water stand. The expanding frame then cracks the stucco and the stone surround around it. Caught early it is a matter of cleaning back, priming, reglazing and adjusting. Left, it becomes a masonry repair and a replacement-window application.

The uncomfortable part

Most of the damage was done by the maintenance

Every item below was carried out by a competent contractor who believed he was helping, and every one of them is irreversible.

What happens, in order

  1. Left alone, lime takes water in and gives it back. The wall stays sound.
  2. Sealed, the water still gets in — around the head, up from the slab — but it cannot leave. It collects, and takes the face off.
  3. Patched in hard cement, the patch survives and the softer original spalls away in a widening ring around it.

Pressure washing is the first one. Four thousand pounds of pressure will clean a driveway and will also erode the face off lime stucco, open the surface so it holds more dirt than before, drive water deep into a wall that then has nowhere to send it, and strip a hundred years of patina in an afternoon. On old timber it blasts out the soft growth between the grain and leaves a corrugated surface that never takes a finish properly again. The governing principle in every preservation standard written is the gentlest effective means, and on historic fabric that is usually a low-pressure application, a dwell time, a soft brush and a great deal of clean water. The rig matters less than the restraint.

The second is Portland cement. Historic lime stucco and lime mortar are deliberately softer and more permeable than the masonry behind them — they are the sacrificial layer, designed to crack and move and let moisture pass so that the structure does not have to. Patch or repoint them with modern cement and you install something harder and tighter than its surroundings. The patch survives. The original material around it spalls away, and it keeps doing so, in a widening ring, for as long as the patch is there.

The third is the sealer. Elastomeric coatings, film-forming water repellents and the wrong paint on a breathing wall all do the same thing — they stop water getting out. On a Palm Beach house the moisture has multiple ways in whatever you do, so the only question that matters is whether it can leave. Mineral and silicate finishes, limewash and vapour-open paints let it; most of what is on the shelf does not.

And the fourth is the hurricane season. Shutter tracks, panel anchors and clips are routinely drilled straight into cast stone, coral keystone and original stucco, a few more each year, by a crew that will not be there next season and keeps no drawing. Those holes are permanent. Storm protection on a historic house has to be planned once, fixed in reversible locations with stainless fasteners, recorded on a drawing, and then used for twenty years — which is a thing we do, and a thing almost nobody is asked for.

Not on a historic house

  • High-pressure washing of stucco, render, stone or old timber
  • Sandblasting or any abrasive cleaning of masonry — the surface skin never comes back
  • Portland cement patching or repointing over lime
  • Elastomeric or film-forming coatings on a breathing wall
  • Silicone water repellents on porous stone and keystone
  • Urethane or topical sealers on Cuban tile and terrazzo
  • Replacing sound original tile for want of underlayment
  • Scrapping steel casements that are repairable
  • Chlorine run-off into historic planting and specimen trees
  • New anchors into historic fabric without a recorded reason

What we do

The landmark programme

This is not a separate service from the rest of our estate work. It is the same twelve programmes, run with different materials, different pressures and a materially heavier record. Work requiring a licensed trade is performed by a contractor licensed in that trade, scheduled into the same calendar and inspected on completion, contracting directly with you.

Gentle cleaning of the envelope

Washing calibrated to the material in front of us, not to the machine we arrived with.

  • Test patches agreed before any elevation is started
  • pH-appropriate products, never chlorine onto planting
  • Fresh-water salt rinse on a cycle and after onshore blows
  • Biological growth treated rather than blasted
  • Pressure, product and dwell time recorded for every surface

Masonry, stucco and stone watch

The slow failures caught while they are still small and cheap.

  • Crack lines traced, dated and measured visit to visit
  • Rust bloom and spalling photographed from fixed positions
  • Existing cement patches identified and flagged
  • Cast stone, coral keystone and quoin condition recorded
  • Lime-compatible repair specified and subcontracted when due
  • Movement reported to your architect or engineer, not patched over

Roof, tile and rainwater

Clay tile is an asset on the roof, not a consumable.

  • Salvaged tile stock inventoried and stored on site

  • Valleys, flashings and parapet copings cleared
  • Gutters, scuppers and concealed drainage run and proven
  • Roofing work placed with licensed roofers who relay rather than replace
IV

Steel casements, timber and ironwork

The elements that are repairable right up until the day they are not.

  • Every casement opened, operated and lubricated on a cycle
  • Glazing putty and rebate corrosion caught early
  • Wrought iron rails, gates and grilles de-rusted and recoated
  • Pecky cypress and Dade County pine monitored for moisture
  • Shutters, louvres and porch timbers kept in finish
  • Original glass handled as original glass

Interiors and historic finishes

The floors are usually older than the roof and treated worse.

  • Cuban cement tile and terrazzo cleaned and sealed correctly
  • Products used on each floor recorded so the next crew knows
  • Humidity held in the range the building and its contents need
  • Plaster, millwork and ceiling condition walked and photographed
  • Leaded and stained glass checked for bowing and cement loss
  • Specialist conservators engaged where conservation is required
VI

Storm protection without new holes

Planned once, drawn, and used for the next twenty seasons.

  • Survey of every existing anchor, track and clip
  • Reversible fixing locations agreed and drawn before any new hole
  • Stainless fasteners and isolated bedding in masonry
  • Panels, tracks and hardware labelled by opening and stored dry
  • Deployed each May, closed before a storm, removed after
  • Salt rinse of the envelope once the weather has passed

Grounds, walls and historic landscape

The setting is usually part of what was designated.

  • Irrigation kept off walls, stone and timber
  • Root intrusion at footings, walks and seawalls watched
  • Loggias, fountains, urns and garden ornament maintained
  • Coquina and keystone paving cleaned without acid
  • Gates, piers and boundary walls kept in order

The record and the calendar

The deliverable that outlives any single contractor.

Designation

What changes when a house is landmarked

Owners are often told that designation means they can no longer touch their own house. That is not what it means, and the distinction is worth understanding before a project rather than during one.

In the Town of Palm Beach, over 328 landmark properties, sites and vistas are protected under the Historic Preservation Ordinance at Chapter 54 of the Town Code. A seven-member Landmarks Preservation Commission, six of whom must be Town residents, meets monthly on the third Wednesday and reviews proposed changes to designated properties, issuing a Certificate of Appropriateness where the work is approved. The same structure exists in different form in Fort Lauderdale, Miami Beach, Coral Gables, Delray Beach and Naples. What this means in practice on the island is set out on the Palm Beach page.

The broad principle in most of these ordinances is that routine maintenance and repair in kind — the same material, the same profile, the same appearance — sits outside the review process, while anything that changes material, configuration or appearance requires it. Where that line falls for a specific property is a question for the Town or city, not for a contractor, and we will always tell an owner to ask them rather than guess. What we will do is keep the house firmly on the maintenance side of the line for as long as possible, which is both the cheaper place to be and the one that needs nobody's permission.

There is a financial dimension as well. Florida Statutes sections 196.1997 and 196.1998 allow counties and municipalities to grant an ad valorem tax exemption on the increase in assessed value arising from qualifying improvements to historic property, and the Town of Palm Beach runs a tax abatement programme administered through its Landmarks Preservation Commission. One honest correction worth making, because it catches people out: the well-known twenty per cent federal historic rehabilitation credit applies to income-producing certified historic structures, not to an owner-occupied private residence. For a house, the local exemption is the one that matters. We are not tax advisers and you should take this to yours — we raise it because owners frequently do not know the programme exists.

Worth knowing

  • Designation generally restricts changes to the exterior and the setting, not the way you live inside
  • Like-for-like maintenance is normally outside the review process
  • Material changes, replacements and additions normally are not
  • Boards meet monthly, so an application has a calendar, not a deadline you can shorten
  • A documented maintenance history makes an application easier to argue
  • Relaying salvaged original roof tile is usually preferred to replacement
  • Local ad valorem exemption programmes exist in several municipalities
  • The federal rehabilitation credit does not apply to private residences
  • Your architect, preservation consultant and the Town are the authorities — we are not

Common questions

Before you call

Can you pressure wash a historic house?

Not in the way the phrase is normally meant. High pressure erodes lime stucco and render, opens the surface so it soils faster afterwards, drives water into a wall that cannot shed it, and leaves old timber permanently furrowed. The correct approach on historic fabric is the gentlest effective means: a test patch in an inconspicuous place first, low pressure or none at all, an appropriate cleaning agent given time to work, soft brushes, and a great deal of clean water. The result is slower and costs more in labour. It is also the only version that leaves the building intact.

Do I need approval to have my landmarked house cleaned and maintained?

Generally no. Most historic preservation ordinances, including the Town of Palm Beach's, treat routine maintenance and repair in kind as outside the review process, and reserve the Certificate of Appropriateness for work that changes material, configuration or appearance. The line is drawn slightly differently in each municipality, so the Town or city is always the right place to confirm it for your property. We will tell you when we think something is approaching that line rather than proceeding and finding out afterwards.

My stucco is cracking and staining rust. What is happening?

Almost certainly chloride-induced corrosion of steel inside the wall — a lintel, reinforcement, a casement frame or a balcony fixing. Airborne salt reaches the steel through the masonry, the steel rusts, and the rust takes up several times the volume of the metal it came from, so it splits the masonry from inside. The tell is a crack that follows a straight line rather than wandering, often with a rust bloom along it. It does not improve on its own and patching the surface only hides the progress. It wants to be seen by an engineer, and the useful thing we can do first is photograph and measure it from fixed points so that there is evidence of how fast it is moving.

Should my old roof tile be replaced?

Usually not. A good clay barrel tile can last well beyond a century, while the underlayment beneath it — which is the part actually keeping water out — has a service life closer to twenty or thirty years. On most historic houses the right answer is to lift and catalogue the original tile, renew the underlayment, and relay the original with salvaged units making up breakages. On a designated property that is often the only treatment a board will approve, and it is worth knowing that before a roofer quotes you for a tear-off.

Can you fit hurricane protection without damaging the house?

You cannot fit it without any intervention, but you can fit it once, deliberately, instead of a few more holes every season. We survey the anchors already in the building, agree reversible fixing locations with you and your architect before anything new is drilled, use stainless fasteners isolated from the masonry, and record every penetration on a drawing. The panels and tracks are then labelled by opening and stored dry, so the same hardware goes back in the same holes for the next twenty years rather than a new crew improvising in a hurry.

What is cyclical maintenance?

A written schedule of recurring tasks set against each element of a building — monthly, quarterly, annual and multi-year — carried out by the same people and recorded every time. It is the standard approach recommended for stewards of historic buildings, and it is the opposite of the way most private houses are run, which is to wait for something to visibly fail and then call somebody. On an old building the gap between the first sign and the visible failure is usually several years, and the cost difference between the two moments is an order of magnitude.

Is there a tax benefit to maintaining a historic property in Florida?

There can be, for qualifying improvements rather than for routine upkeep. Florida Statutes sections 196.1997 and 196.1998 permit counties and municipalities to grant an ad valorem tax exemption on the increase in assessed value arising from approved work on historic property, and several South Florida municipalities including the Town of Palm Beach operate such a programme. The twenty per cent federal rehabilitation tax credit is a different thing and applies only to income-producing certified historic structures, not to a private home. We are not tax advisers and this is a question for yours, but it is worth asking before a project rather than after it.

Will you work alongside our architect and preservation consultant?

That is the normal arrangement and the one we prefer. They set the treatment and carry the applications; we keep the building between their involvements, maintain the record they will need, and carry out the ordinary work to the specification they have written. On a historic house the owner usually has good professionals already — what is missing is someone at the property regularly enough to notice things.

Which areas do you cover?

The same nine counties as the rest of the firm: Palm Beach, Broward, Miami-Dade and Monroe on the Atlantic, and Manatee, Sarasota, Charlotte, Lee and Collier on the Gulf. Historic work concentrates in Palm Beach, Coral Gables, Miami Beach, Old Fort Lauderdale, Delray Beach and the Naples historic district. Response targets by county are on the coverage page.
