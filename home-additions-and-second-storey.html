Building here · The existing house

Adding to a Florida house, and why it is often harder than building a new one

A new house starts from a survey and a soil report. An addition starts from a building nobody fully understands: a foundation sized for the house that was built, walls whose reinforcing is a matter of inference, a floor level fixed decades before the current flood map, and a permit history that may or may not describe what is standing. Whether you already own the house or are buying one with the intention of enlarging it, the existing structure decides the project. This page explains how, before anybody draws the extension.

The existing house

Six things the house has already decided

On an addition the parcel still governs, but the building standing on it governs more. Each of these can be established before design, and each is capable of changing what the addition is.

What the foundation will carry

The footings were sized for the loads of the original house and nothing else. Their width, depth and reinforcing are rarely on record for an older building, and the soil beneath them was probably never tested. Until an engineer has exposed and measured them, every statement about what the house can support is an assumption.

How the walls are built

Most houses here are concrete block with a poured tie beam at the top, and the questions are which cells are filled and reinforced, how the beam is tied to the roof and whether the corners and openings were built the way current practice would require. Age is a guide and not an answer.

Where it sits against today’s setbacks

Older houses frequently stand closer to a boundary than the present code allows and are permitted to remain as legal non-conforming structures. That status generally does not extend to new work. An addition has to meet the current setback, and a second storey directly above a non-conforming wall may not be permitted at all.

Its floor level against the flood map

If the parcel is in a flood hazard area and the existing floor is below the elevation now required, the house is tolerated as it stands. How much may be spent on it before that tolerance ends is defined by a rule, and it is the most consequential number in the project.

The lot’s remaining allowance

Lot coverage, impervious area and any floor area ratio are measured across the whole property. The existing house, the drive, the pool deck and the terraces have already used part of each. The addition gets what is left, which on a built-out lot may be very little.

The permit history

Every permit issued on the address is public. The record shows what was approved, what was left open and, by omission, what was built without one. An enclosed porch or a converted garage that never had a permit becomes part of the application once you ask to add to it.

None of this is an argument against adding to a house. A well-placed addition is often the right answer, and on a lot whose existing house could not be rebuilt to today’s setbacks it may be the only way to keep what is there. It is an argument for spending money on investigation before spending it on design.

The code

How much of the old house the new work drags in

This is the question owners ask as “will I have to bring the whole house up to code?” The answer is in a separate volume of the Florida Building Code, and it is more precise than yes or no.

The Florida Building Code has a volume called Existing Building, and it sorts work into categories. Repairs are one. Alterations are graded in three levels by how much of the building they disturb, from replacing finishes and fixtures, through reconfiguring rooms and systems, to work across most of the building. Additions are a category of their own. A project that adds a wing and remodels the rooms it joins is both an addition and an alteration, and each part is judged under its own chapter.

The starting point for an addition is simple: the new part is new construction and meets the current code in full. That means current wind design, engineered connections from roof to foundation, current energy requirements, and in the windborne debris region impact protection for every new opening. In Miami-Dade and Broward those openings carry High Velocity Hurricane Zone approvals. In Palm Beach County and on the Gulf coast in Collier they qualify under the statewide ASTM standards. The two are not interchangeable, and the difference is set out here.

The existing house is not automatically brought up to date. What the code asks is whether the addition makes the old structure work harder. Under the additions chapter, as it is applied in practice, an existing element whose gravity load rises by more than about five per cent has to be shown to comply as though new, and existing elements resisting wind may stay as they are only if the addition does not raise the demand on them by more than about ten per cent. Those are engineering tests, run by the structural engineer on the actual building, and the section in force on your application date is the one that counts. The 9th Edition (2026) takes effect on 31 December 2026, and applications filed before then stay under the 8th.

That is why the form of an addition matters so much. One that stands on its own foundation and carries its own wind load, meeting the house at a joint, can leave the old structure almost untouched in the calculation. One that sits on the existing walls, or removes a wall that was bracing the house, hands the existing structure new load and brings it into the analysis.

The roof is the commonest place the two meet. Section 553.844(5) of the Florida Statutes provides that where an existing roof was built or replaced in compliance with the 2007 Florida Building Code or a later edition, and a quarter or more of it is being repaired or replaced, only the affected portion need meet the current code. An older roof does not have that protection, and tying an addition into it can turn a junction into a re-roof. The date of the last roofing permit is worth knowing before the roofline is designed.

What the addition can draw in

  • Existing footings, if new load lands on them
  • Existing walls, if they carry or brace the new work
  • The existing roof, where the new one ties in
  • Smoke alarms, as a condition of the permit
  • The electrical service, if the new load exceeds it
  • Air-conditioning capacity and duct layout
  • The flood rule, by cost, on a flood-zone parcel
  • Unpermitted earlier work, once it is on the drawings

The flood rule

The fifty per cent line

On a parcel in a flood hazard area this decides the scope of the project before the design does. Establish it first, and in writing.

The Florida Building Code and the federal flood insurance rules share a definition. A substantial improvement is any reconstruction, rehabilitation, addition or other improvement whose cost equals or exceeds fifty per cent of the market value of the structure before the work starts. The structure, not the property. On this coast, where the land is often most of the value, the building may be worth a small fraction of what was paid, and half of that fraction is a figure a good addition passes easily.

Cross the line and the existing house is treated, for flood purposes, as though it were being built today. Its lowest floor has to be at the currently required elevation. For a slab-on-grade house sitting below that figure, compliance means raising the building or replacing it, and the addition has become a different project. That is why owners find themselves, late and unwillingly, comparing an extension with a teardown.

Stay under the line and the code is more accommodating than people assume. For a horizontal addition that is structurally connected and is not a substantial improvement, the Existing Building volume requires that it not create or extend a non-conformity, and allows its floor to sit at the lower of two levels: the existing house’s lowest floor, or the elevation that would be required of a new building. So a modest side addition can meet the existing floor without a step. New foundations, and foundations raised or extended upward, are treated differently and must meet the flood requirements for new construction whether or not the threshold is reached.

Three details decide which side of the line a project falls on. How market value is established: an appraisal of the structure, or a figure derived from the tax roll, and which the municipality accepts. What counts as cost: broadly the labour and materials of the whole job, including work an owner might think of as separate. And over what period: a floodplain ordinance may count improvements cumulatively across several years, so that last year’s kitchen is added to this year’s addition. Whether your municipality does is a question for its floodplain administrator.

The practical step is to ask that official for a substantial improvement determination before design is commissioned, with the valuation method stated. It is the same advice as for the design flood elevation on a new house, and for the same reason. The flood elevation page explains the terms.

Ask the floodplain administrator

  • Is the parcel in a special flood hazard area, and which zone
  • What elevation is required today, and what is the existing floor
  • How is the structure’s market value established
  • Which costs are counted toward the fifty per cent
  • Are improvements counted cumulatively, and over how long
  • What would trigger compliance of the whole house
  • At what elevation may an addition’s floor be set
  • Will the determination be given in writing

Going up

What a second storey does to the house beneath it

A second floor is not placed on a house. It is carried by it, down to the ground and sideways against the wind, and the single-storey house was not designed to do either.

  1. Investigate before designingExpose the footings at several points, establish their size and what they bear on, and find out which block cells are filled and reinforced and how the tie beam is built. Scanning and a few carefully chosen openings answer most of it. Without this the engineer is designing on assumption and will say so in the drawings.
  2. Follow the weight to the groundA second storey adds its own floor, walls, roof, finishes and occupants to walls and footings that were sized for a roof alone. Where they are adequate, they are shown to be. Where they are not, the usual answers are new footings and columns threaded inside or alongside the existing walls, so the upper floor stands on its own structure and the old house is relieved of it.
  3. Then follow the windDoubling the height roughly doubles the wall the wind pushes against and lifts the roof into stronger wind. The force has to travel through the new floor, acting as a horizontal plate, into walls stiff enough to take it down to the foundation. A ground floor opened up with wide sliding doors has few such walls. This, more often than the weight, is what forces work on the existing storey.
  4. Accept that the roof comes offThe existing roof and its structure are removed to form the new floor. For a period the house below is protected by temporary coverings, in a region where summer rain arrives daily and the storm months run from June through November. When that period falls, how long it lasts and who carries the risk of water damage to the rooms below are contract questions to settle with the licensed contractor before work starts.
  5. Find room for the stairA stair with a comfortable rise takes a considerable area out of the ground-floor plan, and it has to land where the upper plan wants it. It is frequently the element that reshapes the existing rooms more than anything structural does.
  6. Re-plan the servicesA second floor needs its own cooling, with ducts and a condensate route, drainage stacks that have to pass down through finished rooms, and an electrical service that may no longer be large enough. Each passes through the storey you were hoping to leave alone.
  7. Check the envelope the town allowsHeight is measured from a fixed datum. Floor area ratio, where there is one, counts the new floor. And an upper storey may be required to step in from a wall that is closer to the boundary than the current setback. The zoning answer comes before the structural one.
  8. Plan to live somewhere elseA house without a roof, with its ground floor propped and its services cut, is not habitable. The cost of living elsewhere for the duration belongs in the comparison with a new house or a different property, and it is the line most often left out.

Going out

A side or rear addition, and the joint between two buildings

Extending outward avoids most of what makes a second storey difficult. What it introduces is a junction, and the junction is where additions fail.

Two foundations, two histories

The existing house finished settling long ago. The new footing has not started. If the two are rigidly tied, the difference appears as a crack along the line where they meet. The engineer either designs the connection to carry that movement or separates the structures with a joint that is detailed to stay watertight. Both work. Doing neither does not.

The slab and the floor level

The new slab has to meet the old at a level, with a moisture barrier that is continuous across the joint and termite protection that is not interrupted by it. In a flood zone the level itself is governed, and whether the addition may match the existing floor depends on which side of the fifty per cent line the project sits.

The roof junction

Where a new roof meets an old one there is a valley or a wall flashing, and in this climate that is where the water goes. The new roof structure must also be anchored against uplift independently of whatever the old one relied on. A junction designed on paper and built by one roofer under one permit behaves differently from one improvised between two.

A sound rule for a lateral addition: decide deliberately whether it is part of the old structure or a separate one standing against it, and draw every detail at the joint to match that decision. The failures are in buildings where nobody chose.

The garage

A garage conversion is a change of what the room is

It looks like the simplest addition of all, because the walls and roof exist. The code sees a space built to one standard being asked to meet another.

Start with the floor. A garage slab is normally set lower than the house and falls toward the door so that water runs out. A habitable room needs a level floor with a moisture barrier, at a height that relates to the rest of the house. Building it up is straightforward, and it reduces the ceiling height, which has a minimum of its own.

In a flood hazard area the floor is the whole question. The flood rules allow an enclosed area below the required elevation only on condition that it is used solely for parking, building access or storage, and many garages exist on exactly that basis. Convert one to living space and it is no longer such an enclosure. It becomes the lowest floor of the house, and the lowest floor must be at the required elevation. A garage that sits below it cannot lawfully become a bedroom by changing the finishes. This is the commonest reason a conversion is refused, and the commonest reason an unpermitted one surfaces at sale.

Then the opening. The garage door was a door, and what replaces it is a wall, usually with a window in it. That wall is new exterior construction: structure tied into the existing frame, impact protection for the glazing in the windborne debris region, insulation and a finish that matches. The room also needs what any habitable room needs: cooling sized for it, an emergency escape opening if it is to be a bedroom, smoke alarms, and enough outlets and lighting on circuits the existing panel can support.

Zoning has its own view. Many codes require a set number of off-street parking spaces per dwelling, and some require them to be enclosed. Removing the garage may leave the property short, and the driveway that remains may or may not count. Where a homeowners’ association exists, its declaration frequently says something about garages as well. Both are read before the drawings are paid for.

It is worth being clear about the permit, because owners have heard that small jobs are now exempt. Since 1 July 2026 a local government must exempt a single-family owner from a permit for work valued under $7,500, on written request. The exemption excludes any electrical, plumbing, structural, mechanical or gas work, and any property partly or wholly in a flood hazard area. A garage conversion involves most of the excluded categories. It needs a permit, and the permit calendar sets out the clock that then applies.

What a conversion has to answer

  • Is the parcel in a flood hazard area
  • Is the garage slab at or above the required elevation
  • What replaces the door, structurally and for impact
  • Is required off-street parking still met
  • What does the association’s declaration say
  • Can the existing cooling and electrical service carry it
  • If a bedroom, where is the emergency escape opening
  • Was any earlier conversion work ever permitted

Permit and people

The permit clock, and who may lawfully do which part

An addition is structural work on a dwelling. The rules about who may contract for it are the same as for a new house, and the temptations to bend them are greater.

Section 553.792 gives the local government thirty business days to decide a residential permit for a structure under 7,500 square feet, and five business days to tell you what is missing from the application, failing which it is deemed complete. For listed permits under $15,000 on an existing single-family home there is now a five-business-day decision. Review boards, variances, association approval and the floodplain determination sit outside those clocks, and on an addition to an older house they are usually where the time goes.

The structural, roofing, electrical, plumbing and mechanical parts of an addition each require the appropriate licensed contractor. Chapter 489 is specific that a contractor who undertakes work by others is still contracting, so a person who takes a single contract for an addition and passes the licensed parts to subcontractors needs the licence. Section 489.117(4)(d) does allow unlicensed people to work on a single-family home under the supervision of a certified or registered general, building or residential contractor. The licensed contractor is the party answerable.

Owners planning an addition are often offered a shortcut: take out the permit yourself as owner-builder and let someone else run the job. Section 489.103(7) allows an owner to act as their own contractor, but the owner must sign the application in person, must personally provide direct onsite supervision of all the work, and may not delegate that except to a licensed person. Unlicensed workers must be the owner’s own employees, with withholding and workers’ compensation. The disclosure the owner signs warns that unlicensed people use these permits to conceal their involvement. We do not work under a client’s owner-builder permit.

Two money rules apply to an addition exactly as to a new house. Where the direct contract exceeds $5,000 the building department will require a recorded notice of commencement before the first inspection, and the statutory warning is that failing to record one may result in paying twice. And under section 489.126 a contractor who takes more than ten per cent of the price as an initial payment must apply for permits within thirty days and start within ninety days of their issue, unless you have agreed otherwise in writing.

Some of the finishing of a completed addition falls into a different category. Section 489.117(4)(a) places painting, flooring, cabinetry, decorative stone and tile, plastering, stuccoing and caulking, among other scopes, outside state contractor licensure. Those are separate scopes with their own edges. They do not make the addition itself exempt work, and nobody should tell you they do.

Before you sign anything

  • Check the licence number on the state register
  • Confirm the class covers structural work on your house
  • Confirm the contracting name matches the licence
  • Ask who the permit applicant will be
  • Decline to pull an owner-builder permit for someone else
  • Record the notice of commencement before work starts
  • Read the deposit against section 489.126
  • Settle who carries weather risk while the roof is open

Before you draw

Send the address before you commission the design

Most of what decides an addition is on public record. It is cheaper to read it before the architect’s first invoice than after the building department’s first comment.

The order in which an addition usually happens is the expensive one. A design is commissioned, the design is submitted, and the building department’s comments then reveal the setback, the coverage already used, the open permit from a previous owner and the flood determination. Each of those was on record before the first drawing. The structural work itself belongs to a licensed contractor and a structural engineer engaged by you; the reading can be done before either is appointed.

So we read the property and its documents, at no charge, before you commit to a design or to a purchase. From an address we return the zoning district and the setbacks that now apply, how much coverage and floor area the existing house has already used, the flood zone and the elevation currently required against what is on record for the existing floor, whether the parcel is inside the High Velocity Hurricane Zone, the permit history including anything left open, any overlay, historic designation or review board, and the questions to put to the floodplain administrator with the reasons for each.

It is advisory work on land and paper, done before any design or construction exists, and it is most valuable to two readers. One already owns the house and is choosing between extending, building up and starting again. The other is about to buy a house because of what they intend to add to it, and has not yet found out whether the lot and the flood rule will allow it. For both, the answer costs nothing now and a great deal later.

We also perform directly the trades listed in section 489.117(4)(a), including painting, flooring, cabinetry, decorative stone and tile, plastering, stuccoing and caulking, and the statute provides that a local government may not require a state or local licence to obtain a permit for those scopes. The trades pages say where each one stops. And if the answer to the review is that a new house suits the lot better than an addition, the custom home page takes up from there.

Questions

What owners ask

Can I add a second story to my house in Florida?

Often, but the existing house decides. Its footings and walls were sized for a single storey and a roof, so an engineer has to establish what they are and whether they can carry a second floor and resist the larger wind load that comes with the added height. Where they cannot, the upper floor is commonly supported on new footings and columns of its own. Zoning also has to allow it: height is measured from a fixed datum, floor area ratio counts the new floor, and an upper storey may not be permitted directly above a wall that is closer to the boundary than the current setback. The roof is removed during the work, so the house is not habitable while it proceeds.

Do I have to bring the whole house up to code if I build an addition?

Not automatically. The addition itself is new construction and meets the current Florida Building Code in full. The existing house is assessed under the Existing Building volume, which asks whether the addition increases the load on existing structure beyond defined limits; where it does, the affected elements must be shown to comply. Separately, on a parcel in a flood hazard area, an improvement costing fifty per cent or more of the structure’s market value is a substantial improvement, and the whole building must then meet current flood requirements, which can mean raising the existing floor.

What is the 50% rule for additions in a flood zone?

A substantial improvement is any reconstruction, rehabilitation, addition or other improvement whose cost equals or exceeds fifty per cent of the market value of the structure before the work starts. The value is that of the building, not the land. If the threshold is met, the existing building must be brought into compliance with current flood requirements, including the elevation of its lowest floor. Some floodplain ordinances count improvements cumulatively over several years. The floodplain administrator for the municipality makes the determination, and it should be requested in writing before design begins.

Can I convert my garage into a bedroom or living space in Florida?

It requires a permit and it depends chiefly on the flood zone. Outside a flood hazard area the work involves raising and levelling the slab, replacing the garage door with a structural exterior wall and impact-protected glazing where required, and providing cooling, electrical circuits, smoke alarms and an emergency escape opening for a bedroom. Inside a flood hazard area, an enclosure below the required elevation may be used only for parking, building access or storage, so a garage below that elevation generally cannot become habitable space. Zoning may also require the off-street parking the garage provided.

Is a home addition under $7,500 exempt from a permit in Florida?

Almost never. Since 1 July 2026 a local government must exempt a single-family owner or the owner’s contractor from a permit for work valued under $7,500, on written request with cost documentation. The exemption excludes any electrical, plumbing, structural, mechanical or gas work, and any property partly or wholly in a flood hazard area, and a project may not be split to come under the figure. An addition is structural work by definition.

How long does a permit for an addition take in Florida?

Section 553.792 gives the local government thirty business days to decide a residential permit for a structure under 7,500 square feet, and five business days to identify anything missing from the application, after which it is deemed complete. If the deadline is missed the fee is reduced by ten per cent per business day. Those clocks do not include zoning variances, architectural or historic review, association approval or the floodplain determination, which on an addition to an older house often take longer than the building review.

Can I be my own contractor for an addition to my house?

Section 489.103(7) allows an owner-builder permit on conditions. The owner must appear and sign in person, must personally provide direct onsite supervision of all the work, and may not delegate that except to a licensed person. Unlicensed workers must be the owner’s own employees with withholding and workers’ compensation, and selling or leasing within a year raises a presumption of violation. The statutory disclosure warns that unlicensed people use these permits to conceal their involvement.

Is Amalfi a home addition contractor?

Not today. The structural work of an addition is carried out by a licensed contractor engaged by the owner. What Amalfi does today is read a property and its public documents before design or purchase, at no charge, covering setbacks, remaining coverage, flood elevation, permit history and overlays, and perform directly the trades that section 489.117(4)(a) places outside state contractor licensure, including painting, flooring, cabinetry, decorative stone and tile, plastering, stuccoing and caulking.
