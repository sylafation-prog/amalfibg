Historic · Diagnosis

The crack that runs straight is the expensive one

On a house of this age on this coast, most cracks are telling you about something you cannot see. A crack that wanders is usually movement, settlement or shrinkage, and it is often cosmetic. A crack that runs dead straight for four or five feet, with a rust-coloured bloom somewhere along it, is a steel member corroding inside the wall — and by the time it reaches the surface it has been doing so for years.

The mechanism

What is actually happening inside the wall

Concrete and lime render protect embedded steel chemically, not physically. On the ocean the chemistry gives way long before the material does.

Fresh concrete is strongly alkaline, and that alkalinity holds a thin passive oxide film on the surface of any steel inside it. While the film is intact the steel does not rust, however wet the wall gets. Airborne chloride — salt — destroys it. Spray and salt-laden rain work chloride through stucco and masonry over years, and once enough of it reaches the steel the passive film breaks down and ordinary corrosion begins.

The problem is not the loss of metal. It is the volume. Rust occupies several times the space of the steel it came from, and that expansion has nowhere to go. It pushes outward against the surrounding masonry with enormous force, which is why this failure is called rust jacking. The wall splits from the inside, along the line of whatever is corroding.

This is why the geometry of the crack is diagnostic. Settlement produces cracks that wander, step through joints and taper. Rust jacking produces a crack that is straight, because the thing causing it is straight: a lintel over a window, a reinforcing bar, the frame of a steel casement, the fixing of a balcony rail.

What happens, in order

  1. Chloride-laden air arrives off the water, every day, whether it rains or not.
  2. It passes through the porous lime stucco and reaches the steel inside the wall.
  3. The steel corrodes. Rust occupies several times the volume of the metal it came from.
  4. The masonry has nowhere to give, so it splits — from the inside out.
  5. The crack and the rust bloom finally show at the face, years after it began.
  • 1Lime stucco — soft, permeable, and the sacrificial layer by design
  • 2Hollow clay tile or poured concrete body
  • 3Embedded steel lintel, reinforcement or casement frame
  • 4Straight cracks running from the ends of the steel to the face
  • 5Rust bloom in the stucco, following the line of the bar

Reading it

What to look for, and where

Four signs, in rough order of how early they appear.

Where to expect it: over window and door openings, around original steel casement frames, at balcony and railing fixings, at the base of columns, along floor lines on a concrete-framed house, and anywhere a bracket, awning or shutter track has been bolted through the face. The east and south elevations of an oceanfront house go first.

A rust bloom in the render

Often the first visible sign, and frequently mistaken for staining from a gutter or a fixing. A brownish discolouration bleeding through the stucco in a line, rather than in a splash or a drip pattern. If you can draw a straight line through it, that line is the steel.

A straight crack

Running along the top of an opening, down the side of a casement, horizontally at a floor level, or vertically through a column. Straight, roughly parallel-sided, and often with a matching crack a known distance away — the two ends of the same bar.

Hollow or drummy render

Tap along the line with a knuckle or a small wooden mallet. Where the render has been pushed off its substrate but has not yet fallen, it sounds hollow. That material is already detached and will come off, usually in a sheet and usually at an inconvenient moment.

Spalling

The visible end state: a piece of the face gone, exposing corroded steel behind it. By this point the steel has direct access to air and water and the rate of corrosion increases sharply, which is why spalling spreads faster than the quiet years that preceded it.

Why it accelerates

The quiet years, then the fast ones

For a long time nothing appears to be wrong. Chloride is working inward through a dense material at a slow, fairly predictable rate, and nothing on the surface shows it. Then the passive film goes, corrosion starts, and the wall begins to be loaded from within. Still nothing visible, for a while.

Once the first crack opens, the sequence changes character. The crack is now a direct path for water and salt to reach the steel, bypassing all the material that was protecting it. Corrosion that took fifteen years to start can do serious structural damage in three or four once the envelope is breached. This is the single best argument for finding it early, and the reason a photograph from a fixed position every quarter is worth more than an opinion once a year.

Making it worse

Three well-meant responses that accelerate the damage

Each of these is done every year, by competent people, on good houses.

Patching the surface

Filling and painting the crack conceals the only information you had about the rate of movement, and traps moisture against the corroding steel rather than letting the wall dry. The crack reappears, usually within a season, and now nobody knows how fast it moved in between.

Cement over lime

Patching historic lime render with modern Portland cement installs something harder and far less permeable than its surroundings. Moisture is pushed into the softer original material next to the repair, and the original fails in a widening ring around a patch that stays perfectly intact.

Sealing the elevation

Elastomeric coatings and film-forming water repellents stop water leaving a wall that it is still entering, around openings and up from the slab. On a corroding wall that is the worst possible outcome: a wetter environment at the steel, for longer, with no way to dry.

What to do

The order that keeps the options open

Diagnosis before repair, and evidence before diagnosis.

  • Photograph it from a fixed position. A marked spot on the ground, the same lens, the same framing, with a scale in shot. One image tells you almost nothing; four over a year tells you whether this is urgent.
  • Measure and date it. Crack width at marked points, length, and the extent of hollow render mapped by tapping. Written down, with the date.
  • Stop sealing and stop patching until somebody who knows has looked at it.
  • Get a structural engineer where the crack is at a lintel, a column, a balcony or a floor line. This is a structural question and it is theirs, not a contractor’s.
  • Then repair properly: cut back to sound material, expose the steel beyond the corrosion, clean or replace it, treat it, and rebuild in a material compatible with the original — which on a lime-rendered wall is not Portland cement.
  • And reduce the cause. Fresh-water rinsing of salt off the elevations on a cycle is not cosmetic on this coast. It is the only part of the mechanism an owner can actually slow down.

What upkeep contributes

The value is the time series

An engineer looking at a crack on one morning can tell you what it is. They cannot tell you how fast it is going, and the difference between a crack that has been stable for six years and one that opened in the last eight months is the difference between watching it and opening the wall.

That is what a maintenance record provides and nothing else does: the same elevations photographed from the same positions every visit, crack widths measured at marked points, hollow areas mapped, and the whole thing dated. It is unglamorous, it costs very little, and on a house like this it is the most valuable document an owner can hold.

Questions

Quick answers

How do I tell a serious crack from a cosmetic one?

Geometry is the first test. Cracks that wander, step through joints or taper are usually movement, settlement or shrinkage and are often cosmetic. A crack that runs dead straight for several feet, especially with a rust-coloured bloom along it or a matching crack a fixed distance away, generally indicates steel corroding inside the wall. Tap along the line: hollow-sounding render means the material has already been pushed off its substrate. Anything at a lintel, column, balcony or floor line should be looked at by a structural engineer.

What causes rust stains to bleed through stucco?

Corrosion of steel embedded in the wall — a lintel, reinforcement, a casement frame or a fixing. Airborne chloride works through the masonry over years and destroys the passive oxide film that alkaline concrete maintains on the steel. Once that film goes the steel rusts, and rust products migrate to the surface and discolour the render. The stain usually appears before the crack.

Why does rust crack concrete and masonry?

Because rust occupies several times the volume of the steel it formed from. That expansion is confined inside the wall, so it exerts very large pressure on the surrounding material and splits it outward along the line of the corroding member. The common name for this is rust jacking. It is a volume problem rather than a strength problem, which is why it can crack perfectly sound masonry.

Should I just patch and repaint the crack?

No, for two reasons. Filling it hides the only evidence you had of how fast it is moving, and it traps moisture against the corroding steel instead of letting the wall dry. On a lime-rendered historic wall, patching with modern cement is actively harmful: the hard patch survives and the softer original material fails around it. Photograph and measure it first, and get the cause diagnosed.

How fast does this get worse?

Slowly, and then not slowly. Chloride takes years to penetrate to the steel and corrosion can run for a long time with nothing visible. Once the face cracks, however, salt and water have direct access to the steel and the rate increases substantially. That change of pace is why photographing from fixed positions on a quarterly cycle is worth so much more than an annual opinion.

Can anything slow it down?

Rinsing salt off the elevations with fresh water on a regular cycle, and after onshore winds, is the one part of the mechanism an owner can genuinely affect. Keeping the wall vapour-open matters too — avoiding film-forming sealers and cement patches so moisture can leave. Neither reverses corrosion that has started, but both slow the arrival of the next one.
