Estates · Interior systems and household

Water, power, alarms and the lift

Four systems inside an estate are maintained on a cycle and each one sits on a different side of a different licence line. Water treatment is unregulated until it touches a pipe. The generator has twenty-four statutory exemptions and not one of them is maintenance. Life safety is three separate regimes that are routinely spoken of as one. And the lift is governed by a chapter most people have never heard of, which names exactly three actions an owner’s agent may take on it.

Water treatment

No category reaches water treatment; the connection is what is regulated

Softeners, reverse osmosis units and whole-house filters are among the most commonly serviced items on a South Florida estate and among the least clearly regulated. The reason is that Chapter 489 does not have a water treatment category at all.

There is no water treatment contractor in section 489.105(3), and there is no water treatment specialty in the Construction Industry Licensing Board’s rules at 61G4-15. What exists instead is the plumbing contractor at section 489.105(3)(m), whose unlimited scope reaches water and sewer systems. So the regulated event is not the treatment of the water; it is the connection. The moment work breaks into potable water piping, it is plumbing, and a plumbing licence is required regardless of what the device on the end of the pipe is for.

The pool paragraphs reinforce this from the other direction. Sections 489.105(3)(j) to (l) expressly exclude direct connections to potable water lines from the pool contractor’s scope, which only makes sense if potable water connections belong to somebody else. They do. They belong to the plumber, and they belong to the plumber whether the fitting is upstream of a softener, a reverse osmosis unit, an ice machine, a fountain or a pool autofill.

What follows is a workable and honest division. Operating and consuming a system that is already installed and already connected requires no licence: salt and media refills, cartridge and filter element replacement, putting a unit into and out of bypass, running a regeneration cycle, testing and logging hardness, total dissolved solids, iron and pressure. None of that disturbs a pipe. On a coastal estate it is also the work that matters most often, because hard make-up water and well iron are the inputs that ruin fixtures, glass, stone and appliances long before a unit itself fails.

Installing, replacing or re-piping a softener, a reverse osmosis unit or a whole-house filter is on the other side of the line, as is adding a bypass loop, moving a drain line, cutting into a manifold or fitting a new isolation valve. Those belong to a licensed plumber with whom the owner contracts directly. The useful thing an owner can take from this is that the two halves are easy to tell apart on an invoice: if a pipe was cut, a licence was required.

Performed directly, no licence required

  • Salt and media refills
  • Cartridge and filter element replacement on an existing system
  • Putting a unit into and out of bypass
  • Initiating and observing a regeneration cycle
  • Hardness, total dissolved solids, iron and pressure testing, logged
  • Reading and recording the unit’s own fault and cycle history
  • Sampling the supply at the source and at the tap
  • Reporting a failing unit, with readings, to the owner’s plumber

The generator

Twenty-four exemptions, and none of them is maintenance

Standby generators are sold on a service plan and serviced, very often, by whoever is available. The statutory position is tighter than the market behaves as though it is, and it is worth reading before an owner signs anything.

Section 489.505(12) of the Florida Statutes defines the electrical contractor’s scope as installing, repairing, altering, adding to or designing electrical wiring, fixtures, appliances, apparatus, raceways and conduit, and it reaches equipment that generates, transmits, transforms or utilises electrical energy in any form. A standby generator plainly generates electrical energy, so the question is not whether the statute reaches it. The question is whether anything in Part II of Chapter 489 lets an unlicensed person maintain one.

Section 489.503 is the exemption section, and it contains twenty-four of them. None is a general maintenance exemption. The dollar thresholds that exist are for government work, owner-built commercial work and repair supervised by a licensed broker, and none of them describes a service visit at a private house. More pointedly, section 489.503(6)(c) expressly declines to exempt a person employed by an owner who acts in the capacity of a contractor — which closes the route an owner might otherwise reach for, of putting the service person on the payroll and calling the work exempt.

That sounds like a dead end and it is not, because the statute is about electrical work. Engine maintenance is not electrical work. Oil and oil filter, air and fuel filters, coolant level, belt condition, battery service and load testing, enclosure and air-path clearance, a scheduled exercise run with the hours and any fault codes recorded, and verification that the exercise timer is actually set and actually firing — those are the maintenance of an internal combustion engine and its ancillaries, and they are the great majority of what keeps a standby set alive.

The line falls at the transfer switch and at anything carrying conductors. The transfer switch in every respect, any conductor or breaker work, a load-bank test that requires connection into the switchgear, and a controller or wiring fault are electrical work and belong to a licensed electrical contractor. Gas supply is a third party again: a fuel line is Mechanical under section 489.105(3)(i) or falls to the Gas Line Specialty at Rule 61G4-15.026. Three licences, in other words, can be engaged by one machine, and the one that fails most often — a dead battery — needs none of them.

The failure that actually happens

  • A flat or sulphated starting battery, found at the moment of a cut
  • An exercise timer that was never set, or was reset after a power event
  • Stale fuel, a blocked filter or water in a diesel tank
  • Coolant low enough to trip on temperature under load
  • An air path blocked by hedge growth or stored furniture
  • A fault code logged weeks earlier that nobody read
  • A transfer switch that has never been exercised under load
  • No record of run hours, so nothing is known about service intervals

The generator division

Engine maintenance on one side, conductors on the other

The same division, set out task by task, because this is the system on which an owner is most likely to be told that everything is included.

Performed directly — engine maintenance

  • Scheduled exercise run, observed, with the run recorded
  • Oil and oil filter
  • Air and fuel filters
  • Coolant level and condition
  • Battery service, load testing the battery, and replacing it
  • Belt inspection
  • Enclosure condition and air-path clearance
  • Logging run hours and fault codes
  • Verifying that the exercise timer is set and firing

Licensed to others

  • The automatic transfer switch, in every respect
  • Any conductor, lug, breaker or panel work
  • Load-bank testing requiring connection into the switchgear
  • Controller faults and control wiring faults
  • Anything inside an enclosure carrying live conductors
  • Bonding and grounding
  • The gas or fuel supply line — Mechanical, 489.105(3)(i)
  • Or Gas Line Specialty, Rule 61G4-15.026
  • All of the above contracted by the owner with the licensed trade

One practical note on the transfer switch, because it is the item owners most want tested and least often have tested properly. A monthly exercise run usually runs the engine without transferring the load: it proves the engine starts and proves nothing about the switch. A genuine transfer test interrupts the supply and watches the house change over, which on a large estate has real consequences for everything on a controller, a compressor or a UPS. It is electrical work and it belongs to a licensed electrical contractor. What an unlicensed steward can usefully do is see that it is scheduled, attend it and record what happened.

Life safety

Three regimes that must not be blurred

A smoke alarm, a monitored fire alarm system and a fire extinguisher are regulated by three different statutes administered by two different agencies, and a company that speaks of “life safety” as one service is telling you something about its reading rather than about its capability.

Smoke and CO alarms — nothing required

Battery replacement, pressing the test button, cleaning the chamber and vents, recording the result and tracking the end-of-life date printed on the back of the unit require no licence of any kind. The end-of-life date is the one worth holding on a calendar: a sealed alarm reaching its tenth year is a device that will begin to chirp or fail silently, and on a large house the replacement is a dozen units rather than one.

Fire alarm systems — licensed, and the trigger is wide

Section 489.505(2) defines the alarm system contractor by reference to work requiring the skill to “lay out, install, maintain, repair, monitor, or service” alarm systems for compensation. Maintaining and servicing are inside the definition, which is wider than most people assume. An alarm system under 489.505(1) is a device or combination used to signal or detect a burglary, fire, robbery or medical emergency. Fire requires Alarm System Contractor I; everything else is Contractor II.

Extinguishers and pre-engineered systems — the Fire Marshal

Under section 633.304 the business requires a licence from the Division of State Fire Marshal in classes A to D, and each individual performing the work requires a permit, either Portable or Pre-engineered. There is no homeowner or agent exemption in the section; the only exclusion at 633.304(9) is for fire chiefs, fire inspectors, fire marshals and insurance inspectors. This is a different agency and a different statute from the two above.

Whether a residential portable extinguisher is caught by section 633.304 turns on whether it is one required by statute or by rule, and the section does not answer that question. Rather than guess at it, the safe division is the one worth adopting on any estate: the gauge, the seal, the inspection date and the location are checked and recorded on the cycle, and a licensed fire equipment dealer services the unit. The same logic governs the alarm — because “maintain” and “service” sit inside the statutory definition at 489.505(2), a monitored fire or burglar alarm is tested and reported on by its licensed alarm contractor rather than by anybody with a screwdriver, and the useful thing a steward does is see that the test is scheduled, attend it and record the result.

Lifts

Chapter 399 has no such thing as an elevator contractor licence

An estate lift is the one system on the property regulated neither by the construction chapter nor by the Fire Marshal. It sits in Chapter 399, administered by the Department of Business and Professional Regulation through its Division of Hotels and Restaurants — which is itself the first surprise.

The vocabulary is specific and it is worth learning, because the phrase people reach for does not exist. Chapter 399 does not use “elevator contractor licence”. It uses registered elevator company, certified elevator technician, elevator certificate of competency, certified elevator inspector and elevator helper. An owner asking a lift company for its “contractor licence” will be shown something, and the thing to ask for instead is the elevator company registration number.

Two structural rules follow from that vocabulary. New installations must be performed by a registered elevator company, under section 399.03(5). And permits issue only to registered companies, under section 399.03(1) — which means the permit itself is a check on who is doing the work, and an owner can verify the registration before anything is ordered rather than after something is installed.

Private residences then sit outside most of the chapter. Elevators, stairway chairlifts and wheelchair lifts in private residences are excluded from the chapter’s coverage at section 399.02(3)(u), require no certificate of operation, and are exempt from the pre-use inspection that applies to other conveyances. That is why an estate lift has no certificate in a frame beside the door the way a hotel lift does, and why its condition is, in practice, entirely a matter of what the owner chooses to have checked.

Which is why this page publishes the clearance requirements below rather than stopping here. An exemption from inspection is not an exemption from physics. A private residence lift nobody inspects is a lift whose door clearances, locking device and controller behaviour are known to nobody — and the hazard those clearances exist to prevent involves small children, which is not a risk most owners would leave unexamined merely because no statute compels them to.

The words Chapter 399 actually uses

  • Registered elevator company — required for new installations, 399.03(5)
  • Permits issue only to registered companies — 399.03(1)
  • Certified elevator technician
  • Elevator certificate of competency
  • Certified elevator inspector
  • Elevator helper, under direct supervision
  • Administered by DBPR’s Division of Hotels and Restaurants
  • Private residence lifts excluded from most of the chapter — 399.02(3)(u)
  • No certificate of operation and no pre-use inspection for those lifts

The Max Grablin Act

Clearances on a private residence lift, and the four-inch sphere

Section 399.031 of the Florida Statutes may be cited as the Maxwell Erik “Max” Grablin Act. It governs clearances between elevator doors in a private residence, and the hazard it addresses is specific: the gap between a swinging hoistway door and the car door or gate, in which a small child can be trapped as the car moves.

So the publishable field test on an estate lift is a four-inch sphere, and it is a check a steward can make and record on every visit: both doors fully closed, the sphere offered at every point along the gap, the result photographed and written down. Pass or fail, it is a safety fact an owner with grandchildren in the house will care about more than anything else on this page. One honest caveat goes with it: section 399.02(3)(u) excludes private residence elevators from the chapter, while section 399.031(4) applies this section to all new private residence elevators. Those two provisions are in tension and this page does not claim to resolve them. An owner should confirm with the Division, against the Florida Building Code and the Division’s own rules, which private residence lifts section 399.031 reaches before treating the measurements above as a compliance obligation rather than as a prudent check.

Three actions

The statute names three actions a steward may take on a lift

Section 399.031(3) describes a fault condition and then names, precisely, what the owner or the owner’s agent may do about it. It is the cleanest illustration of this whole division: the statute grants three actions, and a steward takes those three and no others.

  1. Check for obstructions above and below the carThe condition arises where the hoistway door locking device’s closed-and-locked contacts open while the car is outside the unlocking zone. When that happens the controller must cut power to the motor and the brake, and the car may not restart. The first permitted action is to look — above the car and below it — for the obstruction that may have caused it.
  2. Return the locking device contacts to the normal operating positionThe second permitted action. It is done with a master elevator key, which section 399.15(3) allows to be issued to owners and to owners’ agents, and it restores the contacts rather than bypassing them.
  3. Manually reset the controller with the master elevator keyThe third and last. Until all three have been done, a visual indicator must be visible at all landings showing the condition — which is itself a useful thing for a steward to know, because an indicator showing at a landing is not a bulb fault, it is a lift reporting that it has shut itself down and is waiting for those three actions.

Everything else on a lift belongs to a registered elevator company: not the door operator, not the controller board, not the governor, not the ropes, not the interlocks beyond returning those contacts, not the pit and not the machine room. The right way to read the three actions is as a boundary rather than a permission. A steward who takes the three, records them, and calls the registered company when the condition repeats is doing exactly what the statute contemplates.

Who does what

The division of work inside the house

The household vendor layer is where an owner’s time actually goes. The cycles, the readings and the record are our own work; each licensed trade holds its own scope and is contracted in your name, which is what keeps the licence, the insurance and the liability with the party that holds them.

Our own people, on the cycle

  • Water treatment refills, cartridge changes, bypass operation and logged testing
  • Generator engine maintenance, exercise runs, battery service and fault logging
  • Smoke and CO alarm battery, test, clean, and end-of-life date tracking
  • Extinguisher gauge, seal, date and location checks, recorded
  • Lift door clearance checks against 399.031, including the four-inch sphere
  • The three actions at 399.031(3), with a master elevator key
  • Scheduling, attending and recording the licensed trades’ visits
  • Holding the calendar, the readings, the photographs and the vendor register
  • Naming, in the record, which licence each outstanding item requires

Held by the licensed trade, in your name

  • Any work breaking into potable water piping — licensed plumber, 489.105(3)(m)
  • Softener, reverse osmosis or whole-house filter installation and re-piping
  • Transfer switch, conductors, breakers and controller faults — electrical, 489.505(12)
  • Gas and fuel supply — Mechanical 489.105(3)(i) or Gas Line Specialty 61G4-15.026
  • Fire and burglar alarm systems — Alarm System Contractor I or II, 489.505(2)
  • Extinguisher and pre-engineered system service — Fire Marshal licence, 633.304
  • All lift work beyond the three statutory actions — registered elevator company
  • New lift installation and its permit — registered company only, 399.03(1) and (5)
  • Each licence or registration number given to you, and verifiable with the issuing agency

There is a reason worth knowing for keeping each trade contract in your own name rather than behind an intermediary, and it is in section 489.105(3): a contractor is defined as a person who for compensation undertakes the work “himself or herself or by others”. A firm that signs your plumber’s agreement, or your electrician’s, or your elevator company’s, and presents the result as its own has taken on the statutory position of the contractor — and the licence had better be theirs. Contracted directly, the licence, the insurance and the liability stay with the party that actually holds them, each number is checkable against the issuing agency, and the record names the licence behind every item on it.

Questions

What owners ask

Does water treatment work require a licence in Florida?

Not as such — there is no water treatment category in section 489.105(3) and no water treatment specialty in the Board’s rules. What is regulated is the connection: any work breaking into potable water piping is plumbing under 489.105(3)(m) and requires a licensed plumber. Salt and media refills, cartridge replacement on an existing system, bypass operation and hardness and dissolved solids testing disturb no pipe and require no licence.

Can an unlicensed person maintain my standby generator?

Engine maintenance, yes. Section 489.503 contains twenty-four exemptions and none is a general maintenance exemption — 489.503(6)(c) expressly declines to exempt a person employed by an owner who acts in the capacity of a contractor — but engine maintenance is not electrical work. Oil and filters, coolant, battery service and load testing, belts, air path, exercise runs and fault logging are maintenance of an engine. The transfer switch, conductors, breakers, controller faults and load-bank testing into the switchgear are electrical work under 489.505(12).

Is servicing a fire alarm system licensed work?

Yes, and the trigger is wider than people expect. Section 489.505(2) defines the alarm system contractor by reference to work requiring the skill to “lay out, install, maintain, repair, monitor, or service” alarm systems for compensation, so maintaining and servicing are inside the definition. Fire requires Alarm System Contractor I; everything other than fire requires Contractor II. A standalone smoke or CO alarm is a different thing entirely — battery, test button and end-of-life date require nothing.

Who services fire extinguishers?

Under section 633.304 the business requires a licence from the Division of State Fire Marshal in classes A to D, and each individual performing the work requires a Portable or Pre-engineered permit. There is no homeowner or agent exemption; the only exclusion at 633.304(9) covers fire chiefs, inspectors, marshals and insurance inspectors. Whether a residential portable extinguisher is caught turns on whether it is required by statute or rule, which the section does not answer — so the conservative line is right: check gauge, seal, date and location and record it, and have a licensed dealer service it.

Is there an elevator contractor licence in Florida?

No. Chapter 399 does not use that phrase. It uses registered elevator company, certified elevator technician, elevator certificate of competency, certified elevator inspector and elevator helper. New installations must be performed by a registered elevator company under 399.03(5), and permits issue only to registered companies under 399.03(1) — so the number to ask a lift company for is its elevator company registration.

Who does what under an interior systems programme?

The cycles and the record are ours: water treatment refills, cartridge changes, bypass operation and logged water testing; generator engine maintenance, exercise runs, battery service and fault logging; smoke and CO alarm testing and end-of-life dates; extinguisher gauge, seal, date and location; lift door clearance checks including the four-inch sphere, and the three actions at 399.031(3) with a master key. Each licensed trade holds its own scope and is contracted in your name — your plumber for anything breaking into potable water piping, your electrical contractor for the transfer switch and conductors, your alarm contractor for the alarm system, a Fire Marshal licensee for extinguisher service, and a registered elevator company for everything on the lift beyond those three actions.

What is the Max Grablin Act?

Section 399.031 of the Florida Statutes, which may be cited as the Maxwell Erik “Max” Grablin Act. It governs clearances between elevator doors in a private residence, addressing the gap between a swinging hoistway door and the car door or gate in which a small child can be trapped as the car moves. It sets a maximum sill gap of 3/4 inch for swinging doors and 2 1/4 inches for sliding; a 75-pound horizontal force test over a 4-inch by 4-inch area for sliding car doors and gates, with deflection limited to 3/4 inch; the same 75 pounds through a 4-inch sphere for folding doors; and five permitted landing-to-car configurations in which the governing figure is always 4 inches.

Does the Max Grablin Act apply to my home lift?

That is the one question this page will not answer with confidence, and the reason is in the statute. Section 399.02(3)(u) excludes private residence elevators from the chapter, while 399.031(4) applies that section to all new private residence elevators. The two are in tension and we do not claim to resolve them. An owner should confirm with DBPR’s Division of Hotels and Restaurants, against the Florida Building Code and the Division’s rules, which private residence lifts 399.031 reaches. The clearance checks are worth making either way.
