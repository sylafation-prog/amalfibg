Building here · The order of things

Who to hire first, and why the order costs money

Most owners begin with the architect, which is reasonable enough — it is the part of the process people have heard of. The thing worth getting right is what happens in the fortnight before that first meeting, because an architect handed a survey, a zoning envelope and a soil report designs a house that fits, and one handed an address designs twice.

The sequence

The order that works

It is not a rule, it is an order of information. Each step produces the thing the next step needs, and skipping one means the next is done on assumption.

  1. Survey the land, before anything is designedA boundary and topographic survey, a tree survey where there is canopy, and an elevation certificate if the parcel is in a flood zone. Until these exist, nobody knows where the buildable envelope is, how far the ground falls, which trees are protected or how high the finished floor has to sit. Everything drawn before the survey is drawn on a guess.
  2. Establish what the land permitsZoning district, setbacks, lot coverage, floor area ratio, height measured from the required datum, overlays, and anything historic. On the water, add the waterward setback and the seawall position. This is a reading exercise, not a design exercise, and it belongs to the owner.
  3. Have the soil tested, if the house is substantialSoil borings and a geotechnical report. On a coastal lot, the foundation design follows the soil, and the soil is capable of changing the structural approach entirely. Finding that out after schematic design is an expensive way to learn it.
  4. Bring in the architect, with the constraints in handThe architect now has the survey, the zoning envelope and the geotechnical report, and the first scheme can be one that fits rather than a drawing dismantled at the second zoning meeting. Good architects ask for exactly this before they start; the order below is one most of them would recognise as their own.
  5. Bring the builder in during design, not after itThe step most often skipped, and the one that produces the greatest saving. A builder reading drawings at fifty per cent completion can price the structure, flag the details that cost disproportionately and say whether the house on the page is the house in the budget. A builder reading finished drawings can only say how much more it is than you thought. Architects are generally the ones arguing for this, not against it — a bid that lands far over budget costs them the client's confidence as much as it costs you the scheme.
  6. Engineer the structure and the systemsStructural, civil, mechanical, electrical and plumbing, in the order the design needs them. In the high-velocity hurricane zone, structural engineering is not an optional refinement; nothing clears plan review without it.
  7. Permit, and expect the clock to be measured in business daysThe statutory review periods are fixed, the local layer on top of them is not, and the seasonal working restrictions in several towns here can move a start date by months. Build the calendar before you commit to one.
  8. Know who is holding each threadConsultants, submittals, long-lead items and the property itself all need an owner. Establish at the outset which of them your architect's fee covers and which your builder's does, because the ones nobody names are the ones that go missing.

The survey

The survey comes before the drawing

It is the cheapest document in the process and the one that governs every other one. Owners routinely spend five figures on design before spending four on the survey.

Boundary survey

Where the property actually is, as opposed to where the fence, the hedge and the neighbour believe it is. Encroachments found here are a negotiation. Encroachments found during construction are a stoppage.

Topographic survey

The shape of the ground, in elevations. It drives the amount of fill, the drainage design, the driveway, the retaining and whether the garden you have in mind is a garden or an earthworks project.

Tree survey

Species, size and condition of what is on the lot. Protected specimens have setbacks of their own, and in several municipalities a protected tree in the wrong place will move a house before it is removed.

Elevation certificate

A surveyor's statement of elevations against the flood datum. It determines the required finished floor and it is the document that sets flood insurance. On a flood-zone parcel it is not optional and it is not interchangeable with a topographic survey.

Geotechnical report

Soil borings, bearing capacity, water table. Not a survey, but it belongs in the same early phase, because the foundation follows the soil and the soil is capable of rewriting the structure.

As-built survey, on an existing house

Where the existing structure sits relative to today's setbacks. On older houses the answer is frequently that it does not comply and is only permitted to exist because it predates the rule. That changes what a renovation may do.

The architect

Florida does not require an architect for a house

This surprises almost everybody, including people who have built here. It is in the statute, it is unambiguous, and it does not mean what owners often hope it means.

Section 481.229 of the Florida Statutes sets out who is exempt from the requirement that plans be prepared and sealed by a registered architect. Subsection (1)(b) exempts “any one-family or two-family residence building, townhouse, or domestic outbuilding” appurtenant to one — and it adds the words regardless of cost. A twenty-million-dollar house on the water is, in the eyes of Chapter 481, the same category as a modest one. Subsection (1)(c) separately exempts any other type of building costing less than $25,000, excluding schools, auditoriums and buildings intended for public use.

So the architect's seal is not what gets a house through plan review in Florida. What gets a house through plan review is a complete set of documents that satisfies the building official and the Florida Building Code, and in practice that means sealed structural engineering, sealed mechanical, electrical and plumbing where the jurisdiction requires it, product approvals for every opening and assembly, and energy calculations. None of that is the architect's seal. Much of it is the engineer's.

Which raises the real question, and it is not a legal one. If the law does not require an architect, why hire one? Because on a house of any ambition the architect is not performing a statutory function, they are performing a design and coordination function, and the difference shows. Someone has to resolve how the house sits on the land, how the plan works in use, how the elevations read, how the millwork meets the stone and how the whole thing holds together as one idea rather than a set of competent parts. A good architect also runs the consultants, which is work that otherwise lands on the owner.

The honest position is this: the exemption is real and it is occasionally the right answer — a straightforward house, a designer who knows the municipality, an engineer carrying the technical load. On a significant house it is usually a false economy, and the saving is recovered by the contractor in change orders against an incomplete set. The exemption is worth knowing because it tells you what you are buying when you hire an architect here. You are not buying permission. You are buying design and coordination, and you should judge the fee on that.

What actually gets a house permitted

  • A complete document set the building official will accept
  • Sealed structural engineering — not optional in the HVHZ
  • Sealed MEP where the jurisdiction requires it
  • Product approvals or Notices of Acceptance for every opening
  • Energy code calculations
  • Flood design documentation, in a flood zone
  • Zoning compliance against setbacks, coverage and height
  • Tree and landscape review where applicable

The engineer

The engineer, and the buildings that need a special inspector

On a house the engineer is the technical backbone. Above a certain size, the state adds a layer of inspection that has nothing to do with the building department.

For a single house, the structural engineer resolves the frame, the foundations and the wind loads, and in the high-velocity hurricane zone the wind loading governs an unusual amount of the design. Openings, attachments, roof-to-wall connections and the load path from the roof to the footing are all engineered and all inspected. The engineer also specifies to the product approval system, which means every window, door, roof assembly and shutter has to carry documented approval rather than a manufacturer's assurance.

Civil engineering handles the site: grading, drainage, the connection to the street and to utilities, and the stormwater the municipality will require you to retain on your own land. On a low lot this is a larger piece of work than owners expect, and it interacts directly with the required finished floor elevation.

Above the scale of a house, Florida adds the threshold building. Section 553.71(12) defines it as “any building which is greater than three stories or 50 feet in height,” or one with an assembly occupancy classification exceeding 5,000 square feet in area and an occupant content greater than 500 persons. A threshold building requires a special inspector — an engineer or architect engaged under section 553.79(5) to inspect the shoring and structural work as it is built, on a plan filed with the building official before construction begins.

That matters to anyone building a small condominium, a mixed-use building or a plaza with assembly space, and it matters to the schedule as much as the budget, because the special inspector's attendance is a sequencing constraint. It does not apply to a single-family house of ordinary height. It is one of the clearest lines in Florida construction between a house and a building, and it is worth knowing which side of it a project falls on before the design is fixed.

Where engineering decides the budget

  • Wind load design, which governs structure in the HVHZ
  • The load path from roof to footing
  • Foundation type, which follows the soil report
  • Product approvals for every opening and assembly
  • Site drainage and on-site stormwater retention
  • Finished floor elevation against the flood datum
  • Special inspector, above three stories or 50 feet
  • Shoring and formwork design on a concrete frame

The builder

When the builder comes in, and under which model

Two established ways to arrange it, each with a cost attached. The expensive version is the one where the builder first sees the drawings when they are finished.

Design, then bid, then build

  • The design team completes documents, then the work goes to several builders to price
  • Strength: genuine price competition on an identical scope
  • Strength: the design is resolved before anyone is committed to a number
  • Weakness: nobody prices the design while it can still be changed cheaply
  • Weakness: the first real number arrives at the point of least flexibility
  • Weakness: an incomplete set invites low bids and recovers the difference in change orders
  • Best where the design is unusual and the owner wants it protected from value engineering

Design and build together

  • The builder is engaged early and prices the design as it develops
  • Strength: the budget is tested continuously rather than once, at the end
  • Strength: details that cost disproportionately are caught while they are still lines
  • Strength: long-lead items are identified early enough to matter
  • Weakness: no competitive tension on the construction price
  • Weakness: it depends entirely on the builder's honesty about cost
  • Best where the budget is the binding constraint and the relationship is trusted

There is a middle route that most good projects actually use: competitive selection of the builder early, on fee and on track record rather than on a lump sum for drawings that do not yet exist, then open-book pricing through design. It keeps the early cost discipline and still subjects the builder to a choice. Whichever model you use, the decision worth defending is the timing. A builder reading drawings at the halfway mark earns their fee twice over. A builder reading finished drawings is being asked to price a decision that has already been made.

Questions

What owners ask

Do I need an architect to build a house in Florida?

No. Section 481.229(1)(b) of the Florida Statutes exempts “any one-family or two-family residence building, townhouse, or domestic outbuilding” appurtenant to one from the requirement for an architect's seal, and it says regardless of cost — so the exemption applies to a large waterfront house as much as a small one. What you do need is a document set the building official will accept, which in practice means sealed structural engineering, product approvals for every opening, energy calculations and, in a flood zone, flood design documentation. The exemption removes a legal requirement, not the technical work. On a significant house, hiring an architect is usually still the right decision — for design and coordination rather than for permission.

Who should I hire first to build a house?

The surveyor. A boundary and topographic survey, a tree survey where there is canopy, and an elevation certificate in a flood zone. Those documents define the buildable envelope, the ground, the protected trees and the required finished floor, and everything designed before they exist is designed on assumption. Zoning research belongs in the same phase, and soil borings follow immediately on anything substantial. The designer comes after, with the constraints in hand.

When should the builder get involved?

During design, not after it — ideally around the halfway point of the documents, when the structure and the principal details are legible but still changeable. That is when a builder can price the scheme, flag the details that cost disproportionately and tell you honestly whether the house on the page is the house in the budget. A builder who first sees finished drawings can only tell you what the difference is, at the moment it is most expensive to fix.

What is the difference between design-bid-build and design-build?

In design-bid-build the documents are finished first and then priced by several builders, which gives real price competition on an identical scope but delivers the first genuine number at the point of least flexibility. In design-build the builder is engaged early and prices as the design develops, which tests the budget continuously but removes competitive tension. Most well-run projects use a middle route: select the builder early on fee and record rather than on a lump sum for drawings that do not exist yet, then price open-book through design.

What is a threshold building in Florida?

Section 553.71(12) defines it as any building greater than three stories or 50 feet in height, or one with an assembly occupancy classification exceeding 5,000 square feet in area and an occupant content greater than 500 persons. A threshold building requires a special inspector under section 553.79(5) — an engineer or architect engaged to inspect shoring and structural work as it is built, on a plan filed with the building official before construction starts. It does not apply to a single-family house of ordinary height, and it is one of the clearest statutory lines here between a house and a building.

Do I need a geotechnical report for a house?

On anything substantial on this coast, yes, and early. Soil borings establish bearing capacity and the water table, and the foundation design follows them. The expensive version of this is to discover the soil after schematic design, because the answer can change the structural approach and therefore the budget. It is a small cost placed in the right phase.

Who keeps track of everything during the build?

Establish this explicitly at the outset, because it is the gap most projects fall into. Chasing submittals, keeping long-lead items visible before they become delays, and reading the schedule rather than being told about it afterwards are real work, and no consultant is automatically paid to do them. On most projects they fall to the owner by default, usually at the worst moment. Ask your architect what their fee covers and your builder what theirs does, and name an owner for whatever is left over.
