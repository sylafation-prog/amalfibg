Building here · Code

The High Velocity Hurricane Zone

If you are building in Miami-Dade or Broward you are building inside the strictest construction code in the United States, and it will cost you roughly eight to twelve per cent more than the same house an hour up the coast. It exists because of one afternoon in August 1992, and the case for it is better than almost any other building regulation in the country.

Where

Two counties, and a line that matters

The High Velocity Hurricane Zone covers Miami-Dade and Broward counties. Palm Beach County and everything north and west of it are outside it.

That line runs along the Broward and Palm Beach county boundary, between Deerfield Beach and Boca Raton. A house in Hillsboro Beach is in the zone. A house in Highland Beach, two miles north, is not. Same ocean, same wind, different code.

The distinction is historical rather than meteorological, which is worth knowing because it explains why the two sides of that line behave so differently in the market. The 1994 South Florida Building Code created the HVHZ after Hurricane Andrew, and the unified Florida Building Code of 2002 absorbed it as a set of chapters that apply only to those two counties.

On the Gulf coast — Naples, Fort Myers, Sarasota — none of it applies. The wind design is different, the product approval regime is different, and the cost base is different. A builder quoting the same house in Fort Lauderdale and in Naples is quoting two different buildings.

The practical consequence for a buyer comparing lots across that county line is that construction cost is not the same, insurance is not the same, and the pool of suppliers whose products are approved is not the same. It is a real variable in a purchase decision and it is almost never mentioned by anyone selling land.

In the zone

  • Miami-Dade County — all of it
  • Broward County — all of it, including Hillsboro Beach and Lighthouse Point
  • Not Palm Beach County, including Boca Raton, Highland Beach and Gulf Stream
  • Not Monroe County or the Keys
  • Not the Gulf coast counties — Collier, Lee, Charlotte, Sarasota, Manatee

Why

Andrew, and what it changed

The code is a direct response to a single storm, and the reason it is written the way it is becomes obvious once you know what happened.

Before 1992 Florida had more than four hundred separate local building codes and no unified wind standard. On 24 August 1992 Hurricane Andrew came ashore in south Miami-Dade and destroyed roughly 63,500 homes. The post-mortem found that a great deal of the damage was not structural failure under extreme load but the predictable consequence of poor detailing, unverified products and inconsistent enforcement.

The specific failure mode mattered. Buildings lost a window or a door, the building envelope was breached, the internal pressure rose, and the roof came off from the inside. Houses were not crushed by wind; they were inflated by it. Once that was understood, the code reorganised itself around keeping the envelope intact — which is why the HVHZ is obsessive about openings, fasteners and tested assemblies rather than simply demanding thicker walls.

The 1994 code created the zone. The unified Florida Building Code took effect on 1 March 2002. Each subsequent major storm prompted further amendment, and the record since is the argument for the whole exercise: a review after Hurricane Milton in 2024 found no homes built after March 2002 had been destroyed.

It is worth holding that in mind while reading the cost figures. The eight to twelve per cent is buying a demonstrably different outcome, and on a house you intend to keep for decades on a barrier island it is among the better money in the budget.

There is a second, quieter return. Insurers price to code, and a house built to HVHZ standards with documented product approvals and a wind mitigation inspection generally prices better than one that is not. On a waterfront property in Florida that difference is not small, and it recurs every year.

What it requires

Where the money actually goes

The zone is not a percentage added to everything. It changes specific things substantially and leaves the rest alone, which is why it is worth knowing which is which.

Openings, above all

Every window, door, skylight and garage door has to be impact-rated and approved, tested against large and small missile impact and cyclic pressure under the TAS 201, 202 and 203 protocols. This is the single largest cost item the zone creates, and on a house designed around glass it is a very large number.

Product approval

Components must carry a Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance or a Florida product approval. That is a real constraint on specification, not paperwork: if the European window system an architect has drawn has never been tested to these protocols, it cannot be used, however good it is. Check approvals before the design commits to a product.

The load path

The code is written around a continuous connection from roof to foundation — straps, ties, anchors and fasteners at specified spacings, inspected. It adds labour and hardware rather than headline cost, and it is the part that keeps the roof attached.

Roofing

Underlayment, attachment and secondary water barrier requirements exceed the rest of the state. On a tile roof the fastening schedule alone is a different job from the same roof in Sarasota.

Inspection

More of them, and more rigorous. Expect the structure to be looked at at stages that would pass unremarked elsewhere, and build the inspection calendar into the programme rather than treating it as incidental.

Documentation

Approvals, product data and installation instructions have to be assembled and kept. It matters at closeout, it matters to the insurer, and it matters again in fifteen years when something needs replacing and nobody can remember what was installed.

The money

Eight to twelve per cent, and where it lands

The figure published by builders working in the zone is roughly eight to twelve per cent over an equivalent market outside it. That average conceals a very uneven distribution.

It is not a uniform surcharge. Concrete, framing labour, drywall, paint and most finishes cost what they cost on either side of the county line. The zone concentrates its cost in a few categories, and glazing is most of it.

Which means the premium scales with the design. A house with modest punched openings absorbs the zone cheaply. A house with a forty-foot glass wall facing the Intracoastal absorbs it expensively, because every square foot of that wall has to be a tested, approved, impact-rated assembly and the field of suppliers who make one at that size is small.

The second place it lands is specification freedom. Outside the zone an architect can specify almost any window system in the world. Inside it, the question is always whether the system holds an approval, and the answer narrows the palette considerably. That is a design constraint with a cost attached rather than a line item.

The third is time. Product approval checks, additional inspections and a more demanding plan review all add to the programme, and programme is money on a financed build.

What the premium is not is a reason to build across the county line. Palm Beach County is outside the HVHZ and typically runs ten to twenty-five per cent above comparable Miami-Dade waterfront anyway, for reasons that have nothing to do with wind code and everything to do with review boards, seasonal working restrictions and access. The expensive counties and the strict counties are not the same counties.

Where the premium concentrates

  • Impact-rated glazing — the dominant item
  • Approved door, skylight and garage door assemblies
  • Structural connectors and the fastening schedule
  • Roof underlayment, attachment and secondary water barrier
  • Additional inspections and a longer review
  • A narrower supplier field, which removes the cheap end
  • Documentation and approval management

Afterwards

What the zone means once you live there

The code governs how the house is built. Keeping it to that standard afterwards is a separate discipline, and it is the one we are actually in.

An HVHZ house is a system of tested assemblies, and the testing assumed they would be maintained. Impact glazing relies on its frames, its anchors and its sealant. The load path relies on connectors that do not corrode. The roof relies on fasteners and an underlayment with a service life considerably shorter than the tile above it.

The climate works against all of that continuously. Airborne chloride reaches connectors and fixings. Ultraviolet degrades sealant. Joints open. A house certified to resist 170 mile per hour wind in year one is only still that house in year fifteen if somebody has been looking at it.

The practical items are unglamorous and cheap relative to the consequence: keeping sealant joints sound around every opening, keeping salt rinsed off exposed metalwork, keeping the record of what was installed so a replacement matches the approval, and keeping the shutter or impact hardware working rather than seized.

There is an insurance dimension too. Wind mitigation credits depend on documented features, and the document is the thing owners lose. Holding the approvals, the product data and the mitigation inspection in one place is worth real money every year, and it is part of what we keep on the properties we look after.

What we keep on an HVHZ house

  • The product approvals and installation data for every opening
  • Sealant joints around openings inspected and renewed on a cycle
  • Exposed connectors and fixings checked for corrosion
  • Fresh-water rinsing of metalwork after an onshore blow
  • Shutter and impact hardware exercised rather than left seized
  • Roof fastening and underlayment condition monitored
  • The wind mitigation record held and kept current

Questions

What owners ask

Which counties are in the High Velocity Hurricane Zone?

Miami-Dade and Broward, in their entirety. Palm Beach County is outside it, which means Boca Raton, Highland Beach, Gulf Stream, Manalapan and Palm Beach itself are not subject to HVHZ provisions. Monroe County and the Keys are outside it, as are all the Gulf coast counties. The boundary runs along the Broward and Palm Beach county line, so Hillsboro Beach is inside the zone and Highland Beach, a couple of miles north, is not.

What wind speed does the code design for?

Ultimate design wind speed for a normal single-family house, which is Risk Category II, is 175 mph in Miami-Dade and 170 mph in Broward. Risk Category I structures design to 165 and 156 respectively, and Risk Category III to 185 and 180. These are ultimate design speeds used in the engineering calculation rather than a guarantee about any particular storm.

How much does the HVHZ add to construction cost?

Builders working in the zone publish figures around eight to twelve per cent over an equivalent market outside it. The premium is very unevenly distributed: concrete, framing, drywall and most finishes cost the same either side of the line, while impact-rated glazing, approved door and skylight assemblies, structural connectors and roofing carry nearly all of it. A house with large expanses of glass pays far more of the premium than a house with modest openings.

What is a Notice of Acceptance?

A Miami-Dade product approval confirming that a building component has been tested to the required protocols and may be used in the zone. Components need either a Miami-Dade NOA or a Florida product approval. It matters at design stage rather than at purchase: if a window system has never been tested to these protocols it cannot be used in the zone regardless of its quality, so approvals should be checked before a design commits to a particular product.

Is it cheaper to build in Palm Beach County because it is outside the zone?

No, and this surprises people. Palm Beach County waterfront typically runs ten to twenty-five per cent above comparable Miami-Dade waterfront, for reasons unrelated to wind code: architectural and historic review boards, seasonal construction restrictions that shorten the working year, difficult barrier-island access, and a smaller pool of contractors able to work inside those constraints. Escaping the HVHZ does not escape the cost.

Does building to HVHZ standards reduce my insurance?

Generally yes, though the mechanism is the documentation rather than the construction. Wind mitigation credits depend on evidenced features — opening protection, roof deck attachment, roof-to-wall connection, roof shape — confirmed by a wind mitigation inspection. The credits can be substantial on a Florida coastal property, and the common failure is not the absence of the features but the absence of the paperwork proving them.

Does the code apply to a renovation, or only new construction?

It applies to the work being done. Replace windows and the new windows must be approved, impact-rated and installed to the approval. Re-roof and the current attachment and underlayment requirements apply. Substantial alterations can trigger wider compliance, and in a flood hazard area the substantial improvement rules can require the whole structure to be brought up to current elevation standards. The threshold question is worth asking the building department before scoping a large renovation.

Do you work on HVHZ properties?

We maintain them. Amalfi looks after finished houses — keeping sealant joints sound around every opening, metalwork rinsed and free of corrosion, hardware working, and the product approvals and wind mitigation record held in one place where an insurer and a future contractor can find them. On new construction the house is built by a licensed general contractor engaged by you; we are useful before that, reading the parcel, and afterwards, keeping what was built.
