Building here · The water’s edge

Building on the water, and how little of an inland house carries over

An owner who has built well inland arrives at a waterfront parcel with most of the right instincts and very few of the right facts. The house is the familiar part. What is new is everything around it: a floor height set by a flood map, a wall at the end of the garden with a legal minimum elevation, a dock answerable to several authorities, a town that may close the site for the season, and one bridge between the suppliers and the lot. This page sets those out for someone weighing a waterfront parcel in Palm Beach, Fort Lauderdale, Miami or Naples, before anyone has been appointed.

The squeeze

The floor rises, the ceiling does not

On a waterfront lot the single most consequential design fact is a number in feet, and it arrives before the architect does.

In a flood hazard area the Florida Building Code, Residential requires the lowest floor to be at or above the base flood elevation plus one foot, or the design flood elevation, whichever is higher. A municipality may set more. In Broward the county has also adopted a future-conditions flood map that looks past present-day levels and is used to set minimum floor elevations for new buildings, so the governing figure can be above the federal one. Whatever the number turns out to be, it is not negotiable and it is not the owner’s.

The height limit comes from a different rule and is measured from a fixed reference: the crown of the road, existing grade or a stated datum. It is a ceiling in absolute space. The flood rule pushes the floor up from below, the height limit holds the roof down from above, and the house is whatever fits between. Lose four feet at the bottom and on most designs a storey has gone. Owners discover this at the second zoning meeting. It can be worked out from public documents in an afternoon, and the flood elevation page shows the arithmetic.

The number also designs the ground floor. A living level several feet above the garden needs an arrival: steps or a ramp from the motor court, a terrace that mediates between the house and the water, a pool whose deck has to reconcile the floor above with the wall below. Handled from the first sketch, that becomes the character of the house. Handled as a correction, it becomes a flight of stairs added to the front of an inland design.

Nearer the open coast the rule changes in kind, not degree. In the zones mapped for wave action the building is carried on piles or columns, the space beneath the lowest floor is left free of obstruction or enclosed only with breakaway walls, and nothing below may be used for more than parking, access and storage. A room at ground level is not available at any price. An owner comparing two lots a few streets apart may be comparing two different buildings.

On the ocean itself there is a further line. Section 161.053 of the Florida Statutes establishes coastal construction control lines, county by county, around the zone subject to a hundred-year storm surge. Building or excavating seaward of the line requires a permit from the state in addition to everything local. It has its own engineering standards and its own timetable, and it does not run on the municipal permit clock.

Establish before you offer

  • The flood zone letter, and whether wave action is mapped
  • Base flood elevation, and the local freeboard above it
  • Whether a future-conditions map governs instead
  • The height limit, and the datum it is measured from
  • What remains between the two
  • Whether the lot lies seaward of the control line
  • Existing grade, from a current survey
  • The governing figure in writing, with its ordinance reference

The wall

The seawall comes first, in every sense

It is the oldest structure on the lot, the least inspected at purchase, and the one a new house is most likely to oblige you to rebuild.

Broward County has written down how high a tidal barrier must be. Chapter 39, Article XXV of the county code, at section 39-407(a), sets a minimum top elevation of five feet NAVD88 for a barrier that is new, substantially repaired or substantially rehabilitated. The model ordinance was adopted on 31 March 2020, it is designed for conditions through 2070, and municipalities were required to adopt consistent elevations by 13 February 2022. An application made before 1 January 2035 may be permitted at four feet if the wall is designed to be raised to five by 1 January 2050. The standard does not apply to oceanfront beaches or seaward of the control line.

The part that matters to a buyer is the definition of substantial. It means modifying more than fifty per cent of the property’s shoreline length, or an appurtenant structure at a cost above fifty per cent of the barrier’s cost, and substantial work must then extend along the entire shoreline. A wall that needs most of its length repaired is, in law, a wall that needs replacing to the new height from one boundary to the other. The City of Fort Lauderdale set its own minimum top of wall at 5.00 feet NAVD88 under Ordinance C23-05, adopted on 23 March 2023. What that ordinance requires of an existing private wall, and by when, is a question to put to the city in writing for the specific parcel. The seawall care page sets out the standard in full.

A higher wall is not a self-contained object. Raise the cap and the garden behind it either rises too, which changes drainage toward the house and the neighbours, or sits below the wall, which changes the view from the terrace. The tie-backs run under the lawn to anchors that the pool and its piping must avoid. The wall, the pool, the grade and the finished floor are one section drawing, and it is the first drawing a waterfront house needs.

It is also first in sequence. Marine work wants access across the lot, room for a rig and somewhere to put material. Once a house occupies the site, that access is a side yard a few feet wide, and the work moves to a barge at a different price. Pile driving for a wall is exactly the category of work the barrier-island towns prohibit in season. So the wall is designed with the house, permitted ahead of it and, where the calendar allows, built before the structure closes the lot.

State law does ease one case. Section 403.813(1)(e) exempts from state environmental permitting the restoration of a seawall at its previous location, or upland of it, or within eighteen inches waterward. That exemption is from the state permit only. The municipal permit, the county standard and any federal authorisation are separate questions, and each has its own answer.

What to know about the wall you are buying

  • Its surveyed top elevation, in NAVD88
  • Which municipality’s minimum applies
  • Its age, construction and last documented assessment
  • Whether past repairs were permitted and closed
  • Whether the new house would trigger a rebuild
  • Where the tie-backs and anchors run
  • Whether upland access survives once the house is built
  • Whether its permit was issued under the four-foot transition

The water’s edge

Six authorities, one shoreline

An inland house answers to a building department. A dock, a lift or a wall answers to several bodies at once, and none of them is bound by another’s approval.

The municipality

Zoning for the dock itself: how far it may project, its setback from the extended side lot lines, lift and davit rules, and sometimes the neighbour’s view corridor. Also the building permit for the structure and its electrical supply.

The county

Environmental licensing in several counties, and in Broward the tidal barrier standard that the municipal rules are required to track. County review is its own application on its own timetable.

The state

The Department of Environmental Protection regulates work in, on or over the water. Section 403.813(1)(b) exempts a private dock of 1,000 square feet or less of over-water area, or 500 in Outstanding Florida Waters, built on pilings, used for recreational mooring and the only such dock on that stretch of shoreline. Larger or different, and it is a permit. Where the bottom is state-owned, authorisation to use it is a separate matter.

Federal authorities

Structures in navigable waters also fall under federal jurisdiction, exercised through the Army Corps of Engineers. Much residential work proceeds under general authorisations, and some does not. It is the layer owners most often learn about from a delay.

Protected resources

Mangroves are protected by state statute, and what an owner may trim, to what height and by whom is tightly defined. Seagrass, manatee protection zones and, on the ocean, nesting sea turtles each bring conditions on the design, the lighting or the months in which marine work may proceed.

The dock that is already there

An existing dock is not a right to the same dock again. It may predate the current rules, exceed the present size limits or sit inside a setback that now applies. Replacement is assessed as a new structure in most respects, so the dock in the listing photographs is evidence of what was once permitted and nothing more.

The practical consequence is that the marine programme is a project of its own, with its own designer, its own permits and its own licensed contractor, running in parallel with the house and frequently finishing later. It belongs on the calendar from the first week. What a buyer should check on a seawall and dock covers the purchase stage.

The town

The calendar that most architects have never read

Several of the barrier-island towns do not regulate construction noise so much as close the site. What that does to a waterfront structure is specific and severe.

The pattern recurs along the island and in the smaller towns behind it. A season is defined, running broadly from late autumn into spring. Inside it, work is limited to weekdays or to reduced hours, and the loudest categories are not restricted but prohibited: pile driving, dredging, dry sandblasting, jackhammering, and in at least one town the mixing and pumping of concrete. Some towns stop all work across the end of December. Some bring planting, pruning and mowing inside the ordinance. Some set no decibel limit, which makes the test whether the work can be heard and is objected to.

Now set that against what a waterfront house is made of. The foundation is piles, and pile driving is on the prohibited list. The frame is reinforced concrete, and concrete cannot be placed at height without a pump. The seawall is driven or drilled. Dock piles are driven. Every one of the heavy, early, sequence-critical operations on a waterfront site falls into the category the season removes.

So the structure goes up before the season opens or waits until it closes. There is no version in which it proceeds slowly. A permit that issues a few weeks before the season begins may leave time to mobilise and not to pour, and the frame then starts the following spring with the summer storm months ahead of it. The sequence of the whole job has to be designed backwards from the town’s dates: when the piles must be in, when the last elevated slab must be placed, what interior work can lawfully fill the closed months.

That reaches into the design. A structural system that needs fewer large pours, a foundation type that can be installed by a quieter method where the soil allows, a decision to complete the wall and the piling in one off-season and the frame in the next: these are architectural and engineering choices made for a municipal calendar, and they are only available to a team that knew the calendar existed before the scheme was fixed.

On the oceanfront a second calendar overlaps the first. Sea turtle lighting ordinances switch off or shield illumination visible from the beach from dusk to dawn for roughly eight months of the year. That constrains site lighting and working hours during construction and governs the glazing, the exterior fittings and the interior light spill of the finished house. The ordinance for the specific town is read before a programme is written. The dates differ from town to town and are amended, and nothing here should be taken as the rule for any one of them.

Work the season most often removes

  • Pile driving, for the house and for the dock
  • Concrete mixing and pumping, in at least one town
  • Seawall installation by driven methods
  • Dredging
  • Jackhammering and demolition by breaker
  • Dry sandblasting
  • Saturday working, in whole or in part
  • All work, across the end of December, in some towns

Getting there

Access is priced into every trade, quietly

A waterfront lot is long, narrow, fully built to both sides and reached by a road that was laid out for cars. Everything arrives through that.

The bridge

Islands and finger streets are reached by bridges, and bridges have posted limits. A loaded concrete truck, a crane and a piling rig are each a question to answer before the method is chosen, and towns commonly want notice, a route and sometimes an escort for heavy vehicles.

Nowhere to put anything

The buildable area is the site. There is no lay-down space, so material arrives when it is needed and not before, and a late delivery stops a trade that has nowhere else to work. Some projects stage from a yard elsewhere and shuttle.

The street

Trades’ vehicles have to park somewhere, and several towns regulate where and for how long. Working hours, delivery windows and street cleanliness are enforced by neighbours with the town’s telephone number.

The barge

Where the water is deep enough, material and plant can arrive by barge. It solves the bridge and the side yard, and it brings its own dependencies: tide, draft, the neighbour’s dock and whatever protected bottom lies off the wall.

The neighbours’ houses

Piling and compaction send vibration into structures a few feet away. A dated condition survey of adjoining properties before work starts, and monitoring during it, is the difference between a conversation and a claim. Ask who is commissioning it and when.

The water table

It is close to the surface, and it moves with the tide. Any excavation for a pool, a lift pit or a deep footing has to be kept dry while it is built, and where the pumped water may be discharged is itself regulated. Ask how it is being handled before the pool is drawn.

The glazing row deserves a second look, because it is where the four markets on this coast part company. In Miami-Dade and Broward a unit must carry approval under the High Velocity Hurricane Zone protocols. In Palm Beach County and in Naples it qualifies under the statewide standard. A system chosen from a house on one coast may have no approval for the other, and an owner who falls for a window before knowing the zone has chosen a problem. The hurricane zone page explains the difference between the tests.

Four coasts

Palm Beach, Fort Lauderdale, Miami and Naples are four different waterfronts

The estate market treats them as neighbours. The codes do not.

Miami-Dade and Broward form the High Velocity Hurricane Zone and are the only two counties in it. Risk Category II design wind speeds are 175 mph in Miami-Dade and 170 mph in Broward. Every opening and assembly carries a Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance or a Florida product approval tested to the zone’s protocols, under which the test missile may not penetrate the system. Published figures put the premium for building inside the zone at roughly eight to twelve per cent.

Broward adds two things that belong to it. The county’s future-conditions flood map can set a floor elevation above the federal figure, and the tidal barrier standard fixes the height of a rebuilt seawall. A Fort Lauderdale canal lot is therefore governed by a floor number and a wall number that both look decades ahead, and the house is designed between them.

Palm Beach County lies outside the zone. Openings there qualify under ASTM E1886 and E1996, the statewide standard. It is nonetheless the most expensive place on this coast to build, commonly ten to twenty-five per cent above comparable Miami-Dade waterfront in published figures, and the reasons are not in the wind code. They are review boards that shape the design, towns that close the site for the season, and access across the bridges. The constraint has moved from the product to the calendar.

Naples is in Collier County, on the Gulf, and is also outside the High Velocity Hurricane Zone. Its openings are approved on the ASTM standards. Its design wind speeds, its flood exposure and its municipal rules are its own, set by the code’s maps and by Collier County and the City of Naples, and they are read from those sources for the parcel. Nothing about a house in Fort Lauderdale should be assumed to hold there, and the reverse is equally true.

The standards are not interchangeable. A Miami-Dade approved product passes the statewide test. A product approved only to the statewide test is not acceptable in Miami-Dade or Broward. An owner with houses on both coasts, or an architect arriving from one to the other, should treat every specification as particular to the county it was written for.

What is particular to each

  • Miami-Dade: HVHZ, 175 mph, TAS protocols
  • Broward: HVHZ, 170 mph, future-conditions flood map
  • Broward: five feet NAVD88 for a rebuilt tidal barrier
  • Palm Beach County: ASTM standards, review boards, town seasons
  • Collier County: ASTM standards, Gulf exposure, its own local rules
  • Everywhere: the municipality’s code governs, not the county’s reputation

Before you buy on the water

Send the parcel before you appoint anyone

Every item on this page can be established from public documents while the lot still belongs to someone else.

A waterfront purchase is usually decided on the view, the frontage and the depth of water at the dock. The things that decide what can be built there are on paper: a flood map, a zoning section, a county ordinance on seawalls, a town’s construction calendar, a permit record. Very few buyers read them before closing, and the house and the marine work are then designed, by the licensed professionals the owner engages, around whatever those documents turn out to say.

So we read waterfront parcels, at no charge, before you are committed to them. From an address we return the flood zone and the elevation likely to govern, the height limit and its datum and therefore the space between, the zoning envelope and waterward setback, whether the lot lies inside the High Velocity Hurricane Zone, the municipality’s seawall minimum against what a survey shows, the dock’s permit history where one can be found, the overlays and any review board, and the town’s construction season with the categories of work it prohibits.

That is advice about land and documents, given before any design or construction exists. It is most useful in the gap this page is written for, between deciding you want to be on the water and appointing an architect to draw it. The owner who arrives at that first meeting knowing the floor height, the wall height and the town’s calendar gets a scheme that fits the first time.

We also perform directly the trades that section 489.117(4)(a) of the Florida Statutes places outside state contractor licensure, including painting, flooring, cabinetry, decorative stone and tile, plastering, stuccoing, caulking and ornamental iron, and the statute provides that a local government may not require a state or local licence to obtain a permit for those scopes. On a finished waterfront house those are the trades the salt keeps busy, and the estates pages set out how they are run as a programme.

Questions

What owners ask

What is different about building a house on the water in South Florida?

Five things an inland project does not have. The finished floor is set by the flood map and local freeboard, while the height limit is measured from a fixed datum, so the house is squeezed between the two. The seawall may have to be rebuilt to a minimum elevation. The dock answers to municipal, county, state and federal authorities separately. Several barrier-island towns prohibit pile driving, and at least one prohibits concrete pumping, during a defined season. And access is by a narrow lot and often a weight-limited bridge. Each is knowable before purchase.

How high does a new seawall have to be in Broward County?

Broward County Code Chapter 39, Article XXV, section 39-407(a) sets a minimum top elevation of five feet NAVD88 for a tidal flood barrier that is new, substantially repaired or substantially rehabilitated. Applications made before 1 January 2035 may be permitted at four feet if the barrier is designed to be raised to five feet by 1 January 2050. Substantial repair means modifying more than fifty per cent of the property’s shoreline length, and substantial work must extend along the entire shoreline. The standard does not apply to oceanfront beaches or seaward of the Coastal Construction Control Line. Municipalities adopt their own consistent figure, so the city’s ordinance is the one to read.

Do I need a permit to build a dock in Florida?

Usually several. Section 403.813(1)(b) of the Florida Statutes exempts from state environmental permitting a private dock with 1,000 square feet or less of over-water surface area, or 500 square feet in Outstanding Florida Waters, provided it is built on pilings, is used for recreational, non-commercial mooring, does not impede flow or navigation and is the only such dock on that stretch of shoreline. That exemption does not remove the municipal building and zoning permits, county review where it applies, or federal jurisdiction over structures in navigable waters. An existing dock is not automatically replaceable like for like.

Can I build during the winter season in Palm Beach County’s island towns?

It depends on the town, and the ordinance has to be read for the specific one. Several barrier-island towns limit construction to weekdays inside a defined season and prohibit the loudest work outright, including pile driving, dredging and jackhammering, and in at least one town the mixing and pumping of concrete. Where pumping is prohibited a concrete frame cannot be poured in season, so the structure is completed before the season opens or waits until it closes. The dates and the prohibited categories differ from town to town.

What does it cost to build a waterfront home in South Florida?

Published 2026 figures from builders working in this market put hard construction cost on direct waterfront at roughly $900 to $1,300 per square foot and on oceanfront or overlay-constrained sites from $1,300 upward, with Palm Beach County commonly ten to twenty-five per cent above comparable Miami-Dade waterfront. Those are market bands, not quotations from us, and they exclude land, design fees, permits, landscape, the pool, the dock and the seawall. The seawall in particular is frequently the largest unmodelled item on a waterfront purchase.

Is building on the water in Naples different from Fort Lauderdale or Miami?

Yes. Miami-Dade and Broward are the High Velocity Hurricane Zone, where openings must be approved under the TAS 201, 202 and 203 protocols. Naples is in Collier County on the Gulf, outside that zone, and openings qualify under ASTM E1886 and E1996. A product approved only to the statewide standard is not acceptable in the zone, although a Miami-Dade approved product satisfies the statewide standard. Design wind speeds, flood exposure and municipal rules in Collier County are its own and should be read from local sources for the parcel.

What is the Coastal Construction Control Line?

A line established county by county under section 161.053 of the Florida Statutes around the part of the beach and dune system subject to severe fluctuation in a hundred-year storm surge. Building or excavating seaward of it requires a permit from the state, in addition to every local approval, with its own engineering standards and timetable. It applies on the open coast and not along the Intracoastal or the canals.

Does Amalfi build waterfront homes?

Not today. A waterfront house is built by a licensed general or building contractor engaged by the owner, and the seawall and dock by a licensed marine contractor. What Amalfi does today is read a waterfront parcel and its documents before purchase, at no charge, covering flood elevation, height datum, seawall standard, dock history, overlays and the town’s construction season, and perform directly the trades that section 489.117(4)(a) places outside state contractor licensure, such as painting, decorative stone and tile, stuccoing and caulking.
