Building here · Permits and approvals

How long a building permit actually takes

Almost nobody tells owners this: Florida law puts your permit application on a clock, and the building department loses ten per cent of its own fee for every business day it runs over. The statute is section 553.792. It is four pages long, it has been on the books for years, and in a decade of renovations very few people have ever had it quoted to them.

The statute

The clock the building department is on

Section 553.792 of the Florida Statutes sets how long a local government has to approve, approve with conditions, or deny a complete application. The periods are business days, which matters more than it sounds.

Statutory review periods under Fla. Stat. §553.792. All periods are business days.
DaysWhat it covers
30Residential units, accessory structures, and trade permits — electrical, plumbing, mechanical, alarm — for a structure under 7,500 square feet, reviewed by the local government’s own plans reviewer.
60The same residential and trade permits where the structure is 7,500 square feet or larger. Also signs and non-residential buildings under 25,000 square feet, multifamily up to 50 units, site-plan approvals and subdivision plats not requiring a public hearing, and lot grading.
12A site-specific permit issued under a master building permit.
10A single-family dwelling for an owner in a Community Development Block Grant – Disaster Recovery programme, applied for by a licensed contractor.

Read the first row again, because it catches most people out. A house of 7,500 square feet or more moves from the thirty-day column to the sixty-day column, and on this coast a great many new houses sit either side of that line. A 7,400 square foot house and a 7,600 square foot house are not on the same schedule, and nobody mentions it when you are still deciding how big to build.

Before any of that starts, there is a second deadline that protects you. Within five business days of receiving your application the local government must tell you in writing what is missing from it. If it does not, the application is automatically deemed complete and the main clock has already started. That provision exists precisely because applications used to sit in a drawer while nobody said why.

If the department does find the application deficient, it has to say so within the applicable period. You then get ten business days to submit the corrections, and missing that window means denial. Once your corrections are in, the department has ten business days to approve or deny, unless you agree in writing to give it longer.

You can waive these timeframes in writing, and on a complex project it is sometimes sensible to. What a local government cannot do is require you to waive them as a condition of looking at your application at all. If that is ever suggested, it is worth knowing it is not permitted.

The deadlines that protect you

  • Five business days to be told what is missing, or the application is deemed complete
  • Thirty business days to decide on a house under 7,500 square feet
  • Sixty business days once the structure reaches 7,500 square feet
  • Ten business days for you to correct a deficiency, or it is denied
  • Ten business days for the department to decide after your correction
  • A waiver must be yours to give, never a condition of review

The penalty

What it costs the city to be late

This is the part of the statute that owners find hardest to believe, and it is the reason it is worth knowing the dates.

Ten per cent a day

Miss the initial deadline and the permit fee is reduced by ten per cent of the original fee for every business day the decision is late. Two weeks over and the fee is gone. It is not a credit against a future permit or a goodwill gesture; it is a statutory reduction.

Twenty per cent after resubmittal

Miss the ten-business-day deadline that follows your corrections and the reduction doubles to twenty per cent per business day. The legislature clearly took the view that a department which has already seen the drawings once has less excuse.

When it does not apply

No reduction where both parties agreed in writing to an extension, where the applicant caused the delay, or where a force majeure or other extraordinary circumstance intervened. A deficiency notice sent inside the window also stops the clock, which is why departments send them.

Why you rarely hear about it

Because almost nobody tracks the dates. The reduction is not automatic in practice: somebody has to know the application was complete on a given day and that the decision came late. Keep a dated record of every submission and every response and the question answers itself.

What it is really for

Not the refund. The fee on a residential permit is small next to carrying cost on a construction loan. The value is that a department which knows you are counting tends to move, and that you can tell your lender a defensible date instead of a hopeful one.

Surcharges follow

Where fees are refunded, the state surcharges under sections 468.631 and 553.721 are recalculated with them. A small point, but it tells you the legislature meant the reduction to be real rather than symbolic.

New in 2026

What House Bill 803 changed

Florida changed the permit rules again with effect from 1 July 2026. Two parts of it matter to an owner, and one of them is being widely misunderstood.

The headline is a permit exemption for small residential work. On a single-family home, work valued under $7,500 — based on a licensed contractor’s written proposal or estimate — may be exempt from a building permit.

The misunderstanding is that people hear “exempt” and start work. It is not automatic. You submit a Residential Permit Exemption Request form with a job cost estimate through the city’s permitting portal and you must receive written approval before anything begins. An exemption you assumed rather than obtained is an unpermitted job.

The carve-outs are wide, and between them they remove most of what people would want the exemption for. Electrical, plumbing, mechanical, gas and structural work still require permits. So does anything in a flood hazard area — which on this coast is a great deal of the housing stock. Commercial, multifamily, condominium and apartment properties are excluded entirely. And a project cannot be divided into smaller pieces to slip under the threshold.

Zoning, setbacks and association rules continue to apply regardless. An exemption from the building permit is not permission to build something the zoning code does not allow, and it is certainly not permission to ignore an architectural review board.

The second change is quieter and affects schedules: a single-family permit now expires one year after issuance, or on the effective date of the next Florida Building Code, whichever is later. If you are phasing work across a long programme, that is a date worth having in the calendar rather than discovering.

HB 803, in short

  • Under $7,500 on a single-family home may be exempt
  • Written approval required first — never assume it
  • Electrical, plumbing, mechanical, gas and structural still need permits
  • Anything in a flood hazard area still needs a permit
  • No commercial, multifamily, condominium or apartment
  • No splitting a project to get under the number
  • Zoning, setbacks and association rules unaffected
  • Single-family permits now expire at one year or the next code edition

The other clock

Private providers, and why some projects move faster

Florida lets you hire your own licensed plans reviewer and inspector instead of waiting for the municipality’s. Most owners have never heard of it and most builders do not raise it.

Under the private provider provisions, a licensed engineer or architect can perform the plan review and the inspections that a building department would otherwise perform, and the department accepts the result. You pay for it twice in a sense — the private provider’s fee plus a reduced municipal fee — and you buy back weeks, sometimes considerably more during a busy season.

The economics usually favour it on a large project and rarely on a small one. On a house carrying a construction loan, six weeks of review time has a number attached to it, and that number is often larger than the private provider’s fee. On a bathroom, it is not.

There are limits worth knowing before you count on it. Fort Lauderdale, for instance, charges structural, electrical, mechanical and plumbing separately under the private provider route, and landscape, zoning and engineering review are not covered by a private provider at all — those stay with the city at full standard rates. So the time saved is real but partial, and anyone promising you a wholesale bypass of the municipality is overselling it.

The decision belongs in the preconstruction conversation rather than in a panic at week eight. It is one of the few genuine levers on a permit schedule, and the others are mostly about not creating deficiencies in the first place.

Where a private provider helps

  • A large or complex project with carrying cost running
  • A busy season when municipal review queues lengthen
  • A phased programme where one delayed trade stalls the rest
  • Where the design team already works with a provider they trust

The honest part

The statute governs review, not the calendar

Thirty business days is six working weeks, and it is only one segment of the line. The reason real projects take far longer is almost never the statutory review period.

  1. Design and engineeringMonths, not weeks, and entirely outside the statute. Architectural drawings, structural engineering, mechanical and electrical design, a survey, a soils report where the site needs one. On a custom house this is commonly six to twelve months before anything reaches a building department, and it is the stage owners most consistently underestimate.
  2. Zoning and land development reviewSeparate from the building permit and frequently the longer path. Setbacks, lot coverage, height, parking, landscape, tree removal. Where a variance is needed it goes to a board that meets monthly, and a continuance costs another month.
  3. Architectural or historic reviewThe one that surprises people most. In the Town of Palm Beach, landmarked properties go to the Landmarks Preservation Commission and non-landmarked ones to ARCOM, each with its own submittal deadlines and monthly hearing calendar. A single missed deadline is a month, and a request for revisions is another. We have set out how that process actually runs separately.
  4. Association and club approvalOn many of these streets there is a homeowners’ association or a club with its own architectural committee, and its approval is not a formality. It also does not run on any statutory clock at all, so it can quietly become the critical path while everyone is watching the city.
  5. The building permit reviewWhere the statute finally applies. Thirty or sixty business days, plus whatever a deficiency cycle adds. A clean, complete, well-coordinated submittal passes in one round; an incomplete one adds a month per round, and the department is within its rights.
  6. ConstructionTwelve to twenty-four months on a custom house here, and longer on a difficult site. And on several of the towns we work in it is longer still for a reason no statute mentions, which is the next section.
  7. Certificate of occupancyFinal inspections across every trade, then the certificate. Nothing moves in before it. Allow for the fact that the last ten per cent of a job is where schedules reliably come apart.

The local layer

The delays no statute touches

This is the part that does not appear in any cost guide, and on the streets where these houses are built it is often the single largest schedule risk.

Several of the towns on this coast restrict when construction may happen at all, and the restrictions tighten in exactly the months the weather is best. They are not advisory. They are ordinances, they are enforced, and they extend programmes by months.

In Gulf Stream, from 1 December to 1 May, construction runs weekdays only — no Saturdays — and the town prohibits pile driving, dredging, dry sandblasting, jackhammering and concrete mixing and pumping outright for that period. Pouring a structure through season is not a scheduling preference there; it is not permitted. The ordinance also defines regulated activity to include planting, pruning and mowing, so even the landscape falls under the same clock.

In the Town of Palm Beach, the season runs roughly from November to the end of April with weekday-only working, nothing between 24 December and 1 January, and section 42-198 barring dry sandblasters, pneumatic jackhammers, pile drivers and dredges in season and at weekends. In Highland Beach, Chapter 12 allows Saturdays only between nine and three, and even inside that window prohibits dumpster handling, concrete delivery and pumping, dump trucks, cranes and jackhammering.

The practical consequence is that a build in one of these towns is sequenced around the calendar, not merely scheduled against it. The noisy structural work goes in the summer; the quiet interior work goes in the winter. A builder who prices a Gulf Stream house on a Broward schedule is going to be wrong by a season, and the owner is the one carrying the loan.

We keep the published working rules for each of these towns on their own pages, because they change and because nobody else writes them down in one place.

Ask before you buy

  • Does this town restrict construction seasonally, and by how much
  • Which activities are prohibited outright, and in which months
  • What time may vehicles arrive, and where may they park
  • Is there a separate architectural or historic review, and when does it meet
  • Does the association have its own approval, and what is its calendar
  • Does the town use another municipality’s building department
  • Must contractors register with the town before starting

Questions

What owners ask

How long does a building permit take in Florida?

By statute, a local government has thirty business days to approve, approve with conditions or deny a complete application for a residential structure under 7,500 square feet, and sixty business days once the structure is 7,500 square feet or larger. Thirty business days is about six working weeks. Those periods apply to the review itself, not to the whole project: design, zoning review, architectural or historic review and association approval all sit outside them, and on a custom house they commonly add six to eighteen months before the building department ever sees the drawings.

What happens if the city misses the deadline?

The permit fee is reduced by ten per cent of the original fee for each business day the decision is late, and by twenty per cent per business day if the lateness comes after you have submitted corrections. There are exceptions: a written extension agreed by both sides, a delay you caused, or a force majeure. In practice the reduction is rarely claimed because almost nobody tracks the dates, which is an argument for keeping a dated record of every submission and every response.

Does HB 803 mean I do not need a permit for small work any more?

Not in the way most people have read it. On a single-family home, work valued under $7,500 may be exempt from a building permit, but the exemption is not automatic — you have to apply for it with a cost estimate and receive written approval before starting. Electrical, plumbing, mechanical, gas and structural work are excluded, as is anything in a flood hazard area, which covers much of this coast. Commercial, multifamily and condominium properties are excluded entirely, and a project cannot be split into pieces to get under the threshold.

Can I speed up the review?

Three ways, in order of effect. Submit a complete and well-coordinated application, because a deficiency cycle costs a month and most deficiencies are self-inflicted. Consider a private provider, where a licensed engineer or architect performs the plan review and inspections instead of the city — worth it on a large project carrying finance, rarely worth it on a small one, and it does not cover landscape, zoning or engineering review. And start the architectural, historic and association approvals early, because those run on monthly calendars that no amount of urgency compresses.

Why does a house in Palm Beach or Gulf Stream take longer?

Because several towns on this coast restrict construction seasonally. Gulf Stream prohibits concrete mixing and pumping, pile driving, dredging, dry sandblasting and jackhammering outright between 1 December and 1 May, and allows no Saturday work in that period. The Town of Palm Beach runs weekday-only working through its season and bars several activities outright. Highland Beach permits Saturdays only from nine to three and prohibits concrete delivery, cranes and jackhammering even then. These are ordinances rather than guidance, and they mean the structural work has to be sequenced into the summer.

Who pulls the permit, me or the contractor?

On permitted work it is the licensed contractor for that trade, who pulls their own permit and carries their own insurance. An owner may pull an owner-builder permit on their own residence in some circumstances, and it is almost always a worse idea than it looks: the owner then carries the liability, the code responsibility and the inspection obligations personally, and many lenders and insurers react badly to it. The question to ask of anyone quoting your work is simply which licence the permit will be pulled under, and to see it.

What is the five-day rule?

Within five business days of receiving your application, the local government must notify you in writing of anything missing from it. If it does not, the application is automatically deemed complete and the main review clock is already running from the submission date. It is a small provision with real value, because it stops an application sitting unreviewed while nobody tells you why.

Should I buy the lot before I know any of this?

You can, and plenty of people do, but the answers change what the lot is worth. Whether the town restricts construction seasonally, whether there is an architectural review board, what the setbacks and height limits allow, and what the flood elevation requires are all knowable before closing and all affect both the budget and the programme. We will read a parcel for you and tell you what you are dealing with, at no cost, because it takes an hour and it is the single most useful thing anybody can hand someone who has just bought land in a county they have never lived in.
