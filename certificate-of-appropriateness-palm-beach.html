Historic · Palm Beach

Two questions decide whether your project goes to a board

In the Town of Palm Beach almost every exterior change to a house is reviewed by somebody. Which body sees it, and whether that means a staff signature or a public hearing with mailed notice to the neighbours, comes down very largely to two things: whether the work puts back what was there, and whether it can be seen from the street.

Two commissions

Who reviews what, and the difference between them

Palm Beach runs two separate architectural review bodies, and both of them issue Certificates of Appropriateness. Which one has your property depends on whether it is designated.

The Architectural Commission, universally called ARCOM, reviews exterior work on properties that are not designated landmarks. The Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews work on those that are. The Commission is a seven-member board, six of whom must be Town residents, and it meets monthly on the third Wednesday. The Town protects over 328 landmark properties, sites and vistas under its Historic Preservation Ordinance, and the Commission also administers the Town’s tax abatement programme.

Owners are frequently told that a Certificate of Appropriateness is a historic-district thing. It is not. A perfectly ordinary 1980s house in Palm Beach goes to ARCOM for the same kinds of exterior changes. Designation does not add the review; it changes which commission conducts it, and it narrows what will be considered appropriate.

Three tiers

It is not one process, it is three

The Town adopted an approval matrix that sorts proposed work into full board review, Commission Chair review, and administrative approval by staff. Most owners only ever hear about the first one.

A full board review is a public hearing. It needs a pre-application meeting, two rounds of submittal, mailed notice to surrounding owners, a presentation and a vote, and it runs on the Commission’s monthly calendar rather than yours. Chair review is a middle tier in which the Commission Chair decides whether an item can go to staff or needs the full commission, judged on architectural compatibility and likely impact. Administrative approval is a staff signature, and it is where you want to be.

Staff can still refer a project upward if it may substantially affect neighbours or needs a zoning exception, and administratively approved work has to comply fully with the zoning code regardless. But the difference between the top tier and the bottom one is measured in months and in professional fees, and it is often decided by small choices made early.

The bottom tier

What staff can approve without a hearing

Reading the Town’s adopted matrix, the administrative column is more generous than most owners expect, and several of the items on it are exactly the things a maintenance programme does.

  • In-kind replacement of architectural features, including “S” tile
  • Removable hurricane shutters
  • Replacement mechanical equipment in its existing location
  • Solar panel installation
  • New awnings, or recovering an existing one
  • In-kind fence replacement, and new fences interior to the lot that are not on a property line and not readily visible
  • Additions not visible from the right-of-way, under the size thresholds — one-storey additions under 400 square feet, or second-floor additions under 200 square feet
  • Minor hardscape changes not readily visible from the right-of-way

Note what those items have in common. Nearly all of them either put back what was already there, or cannot be seen from the road. That is not a coincidence — it is the organising logic of the whole matrix.

The top tier

What goes to a full board

At the other end, the matrix sends the work that changes the building’s character, its height, or its presence on the street.

  • New residential or commercial buildings, and two-storey accessory structures
  • Two-storey additions visible from the right-of-way, and any addition at the third floor or above
  • Major façade alterations that affect the architectural style
  • Variance requests and the projects attached to them
  • Demolition of a landmark structure

Between the two sits a minor-review tier that catches one-storey additions visible from the road, replacing an architectural feature with an alternate design rather than in kind, new “S” tile installation as opposed to relaying what is there, new fences, walls and gates visible from the right-of-way, and new mechanical equipment that is visible from the road or sits within a setback.

The hinge

In kind, and readily visible

Two phrases do most of the work in this system, and an owner who understands them can predict the tier before an architect is engaged.

In kind

Put the same thing back — same material, same profile, same appearance — and you are in the administrative column. Put something different back and you move up a tier, even if the new thing is objectively better. Relaying existing “S” tile is administrative. Installing new “S” tile is not. Replacing an architectural feature in kind is administrative. Replacing it with an alternate design is a review.

Readily visible from the right-of-way

The Town is protecting the streetscape as much as the building. Work that cannot be seen from the road is treated far more leniently than identical work on a visible elevation, which is why the matrix repeats the phrase so often. An addition hidden behind the house, under the size thresholds, is a signature. The same addition on the street side is a hearing.

Neighbour consent

Several items in the matrix sit in an administrative column only if the immediately adjacent owner signs a written waiver on the Town’s own form. Without it, they escalate to minor review. Fences on a side or rear property line and certain mechanical equipment are the common examples, and the conversation with the neighbour is usually easier than the hearing.

The sequence

What the process looks like end to end

For anything above the administrative tier, the Town runs a uniform development review that covers ARCOM, Landmarks and Town Council applications alike.

  • Pre-application request. Email the Town’s development review address with the property address, the scope of work and a preliminary plan set.
  • Pre-application meeting. Monthly, and attendance is required. You come away with a checklist of required plans and documents, the deadlines, and a tentative hearing schedule.
  • First submittal. Checklist items uploaded through the Citizen Access Portal, plus one 11×17 miniset and a letter of intent delivered to Town Hall by the deadline. File names in capitals, in the Town’s naming format.
  • Staff review. Staff check the application for sufficiency and code compliance, and post comments to the portal on the published deadlines calendar.
  • Second submittal. A revised plan set answering the comments, with a letter of intent and a written narrative response.
  • Notice to Proceed. Issued once staff deem the application complete. It carries the hearing placement, the public notice steps, the mailer notices and the invoice.
  • Notice and fees. The USPS Certificate of Mailing is uploaded and the fees paid on the calendar.
  • Final submittal. Complete packages to Town Hall and uploaded digitally with the hearing presentation — one miniset for Landmarks, twelve for ARCOM.
  • The hearing. Agendas and backup are posted publicly beforehand.

Read that list again and the cost of the top tier becomes obvious. It is not the fee. It is the two mandatory submittal rounds, the monthly calendar, and the fact that none of it starts until a pre-application meeting that is also monthly.

What it means for upkeep

The quiet argument for a maintenance calendar

On a landmarked house, the practical job of a maintenance programme is to keep the work in the administrative column for as long as possible.

Almost everything that forces an owner into a hearing began as something small that was left. A casement window that is cleaned, lubricated and reglazed on a cycle stays a repair. Left for a decade it corrodes, swells, cracks the surround, and becomes a replacement-window application with a masonry repair attached. Roof tile that is counted, with a salvage stock kept on site, can be relaid in kind. Roof tile that has been losing units for fifteen years with no matching stock becomes a new installation, which is a different tier and a different conversation.

The same logic runs through the whole matrix. In-kind replacement is administrative, and in-kind replacement is only possible if somebody knows what was there, has photographs of it, and ideally has spare material. That is what the record is for, and it is why we build one from the first visit on any designated house.

None of this is advice on a specific property, and we are not your architect. Where a particular item falls is a question for the Town, the matrix is adopted but staff retain discretion to refer, and the thresholds and procedures change. Ask them. What we can tell you is which side of the line we are trying to keep you on.

Questions

Quick answers

Does every change to a Palm Beach house need a Certificate of Appropriateness?

No. The Town’s adopted approval matrix sorts work into full board review, Commission Chair review and administrative approval by staff, and a good deal of ordinary maintenance sits in the administrative column — in-kind replacement of architectural features including S tile, removable hurricane shutters, replacement mechanical equipment in its existing location, solar panels, awnings, and additions under the size thresholds that are not visible from the right-of-way. Where a specific item falls is a question for the Town, and staff can refer a project upward.

What is the difference between ARCOM and the Landmarks Preservation Commission?

ARCOM, the Architectural Commission, reviews exterior work on properties that are not designated landmarks. The Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews work on properties that are. Both issue Certificates of Appropriateness. The Landmarks Commission has seven members, six of whom must be Town residents, meets monthly on the third Wednesday, and administers the Town’s tax abatement programme.

What does “in kind” mean and why does it matter so much?

In kind means putting back the same material, profile and appearance. It is the single most useful phrase in the whole system, because in-kind replacement is generally administrative while replacing a feature with an alternate design generally is not. Relaying existing S tile is administrative; installing new S tile is a review. The practical consequence is that an owner who can document and match what is on the building has far more freedom than one who cannot.

How long does a full board review take?

The Town does not publish a single figure, and it depends on the project and the calendar. What is published is the shape: a mandatory monthly pre-application meeting, then a first submittal, a staff sufficiency review with comments posted on a deadlines calendar, a second submittal answering those comments, a Notice to Proceed with public notice requirements, a final submittal, and then a hearing on the Commission’s monthly agenda. Plan in months rather than weeks.

Is there a tax benefit for restoring a landmarked Palm Beach house?

There can be, for qualifying improvements rather than routine upkeep. Florida Statutes sections 196.1997 and 196.1998 permit local governments to grant an ad valorem exemption on the increase in assessed value arising from approved work on historic property, and the Town of Palm Beach runs a tax abatement programme administered through the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Separately, the twenty per cent federal rehabilitation credit applies only to income-producing certified historic structures and not to a private residence. Take the specifics to a tax adviser.

Do we need approval just to have the house cleaned and maintained?

Generally no. Routine maintenance and repair in kind normally sits outside the review process. The care that is worth taking is over method rather than permission: high pressure on lime stucco, a cement patch over soft render or a film-forming sealer on a breathing wall will each do permanent harm to a house of this age, and none of them requires anybody’s signature.
