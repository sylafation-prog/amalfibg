Commercial property · Broward County

Your plaza is not exempt

Almost everything written about Florida’s building inspection regime is about condominiums, because the milestone inspection statute applies only to condominium and cooperative property. Broward County’s own Building Safety Inspection Program is not so limited. Commercial buildings are not on its exemption list, which means a plaza, an office building, a retail centre or a warehouse of a certain age and size is caught — and almost nobody has written about what that involves.

Who is caught

The triggers, and the exemption list in full

The programme derives from Broward County Board of Rules and Appeals Policy 05-05 and is administered by each municipality’s Building Official. The exemption list is what matters most to a commercial owner, because of what is not on it.

The exemptions, as published: buildings of the United States government and the State of Florida; buildings on sovereign tribal lands; Broward County School Board school buildings; small residential buildings of up to four units and three storeys or fewer; fee simple townhouses; structures with a floor area under 3,500 square feet; and railroads and related facilities.

Read that list for what it omits. There is no exemption for commercial, retail, office, industrial or mixed-use property. A twenty-six-year-old strip plaza of 12,000 square feet in a Broward municipality is within the programme on the plain terms of the policy, and so is a warehouse, a medical office building and a restaurant pad.

The two rows highlighted above are the ones that matter most. The first is that the programme is not limited by property type, where the state milestone regime is. The second is that this inspection covers electrical systems as well as structural ones — the milestone inspection does not. An owner who has read a great deal of milestone content and concluded they understand the exercise has read about half of it.

The height point is worth isolating too. A milestone inspection requires three or more habitable storeys. The Broward programme has no height threshold at all — a single-storey commercial building of 3,500 square feet or more and twenty-five years or older is caught. That is a very large number of buildings and it is the category least likely to know.

Municipal pages describing this programme are not consistent with each other, and some are not internally consistent — at least one city’s page states a forty-year threshold in its introduction and twenty-five years in its notification section on the same page. The operative document is the notice from your municipality’s Building Official and the municipality’s own adopted procedure. Where a page you have found online disagrees with the notice in your hand, the notice governs, and a telephone call to the Building Division costs nothing.

Both at once

If the building is also a condominium

Many Broward buildings have both obligations, and the two are commonly run together. How exactly they combine is a question for the Building Official, not for a website.

A residential condominium building of three or more habitable storeys in Broward that is twenty-five years or older can fall within both the state milestone requirement and the county’s Building Safety Inspection Program. In practice the engineering work overlaps substantially and the two are frequently carried out together and reported together.

We would not state flatly that one satisfies the other, and neither should anyone selling you an inspection. The scopes are not identical — the county programme includes electrical, the milestone inspection does not; the milestone inspection has a defined phase one and phase two structure with its own reporting obligations to unit owners, which the county programme does not. The sensible step is to ask the Building Official in writing what will be accepted for both, and keep the answer.

What a mixed-use building should also establish early is which obligations attach to which part. A tower with ground-floor retail, a commercial podium or a separately platted commercial parcel can produce an unexpected answer, and the time to discover it is before an engineer is appointed to a scope that covers the wrong portion of the property.

For a commercial-only building none of the condominium machinery applies — no unit owner distribution, no reserve study, no phase one and phase two framework. There is a notice, an inspection by a licensed professional covering structure and electrical, a report, and then repairs within 180 days. It is a simpler obligation and a shorter clock.

The shorter clock is the part commercial owners underestimate. A condominium board holding a phase two finding has up to 365 days from the agency receiving that report to have repairs scheduled or commenced. A Broward commercial owner has 180 days from the report date to complete repairs, unless the Building Official allows longer. Half the time, and the obligation is completion rather than commencement.

The repairs

What actually comes back on a report

The findings on a twenty-five to forty-year-old Broward commercial building are predictable, because the failure mechanisms in this climate are predictable.

Spalled concrete at exposed edges

Slab edges, balcony and walkway soffits, columns at grade, stair landings. Chloride-driven reinforcement corrosion in a marine atmosphere expands the steel and pushes the cover off. Rust staining is the early sign and it appears long before anything falls.

Stucco delamination

Hollow areas behind the render, usually at parapets, under copings and at window heads where water has been getting in for years. Found by sounding rather than by looking, which is why a visual walk alone understates it.

Failed sealant at every joint type

Perimeter window and door joints, control joints, panel joints, penetrations. On a building of this age most joints are at or past end of life, and a significant proportion were installed incorrectly to begin with — undersized, no backer rod, three-sided adhesion.

Parapet and coping water entry

The commonest single source of water into a commercial envelope. Open coping joints, failed counterflashing, no drip edge, and a parapet that has been patched rather than detailed. Inexpensive to fix and expensive to ignore.

Walkway and stair structure

On a plaza or a garden-style commercial building, exterior walkways, stair stringers and landings take water directly and are frequently the first structural finding. Railing posts set into concrete without isolation are a reliable corrosion point.

Electrical findings

Unique to this programme and routinely the surprise. Corroded panels and disconnects in a salt atmosphere, deteriorated service equipment, exposed or damaged conductors, missing covers, grounding and bonding deficiencies, and exterior fixtures and conduit well past service life. These require a licensed electrical contractor and are not envelope work.

The envelope and finish findings — sealant, coating, stucco repair, waterproofing, stone and metalwork — are work we perform directly. They are also, on most reports of this kind, the majority of the line items by count even where they are not the majority by cost.

The structural and electrical findings are not ours and we will not take them. Structural concrete repair requires a licensed contractor, and on a building above three storeys specifically a certified general contractor, since a building contractor licence is limited to structures of three storeys or fewer. Electrical findings require a licensed electrical contractor. On a commercial building those parties contract with the owner directly.

Where this ordering matters practically is sequencing. Structural repair must precede coating, because coating a substrate that is about to be broken out is money spent twice. Electrical work at the envelope — penetrations, fixtures, conduit — should also precede final sealant and coating for the same reason. A survey that sets out the sequence is worth more to an owner than three unrelated prices arriving in whatever order the trades happen to respond.

The predictability of these findings is the practical opening for an owner who has just received a notice. Because the envelope failures on a building of this age in this climate are so consistent, the envelope can be surveyed and quantified before the engineer’s report exists — and on a 180-day repair clock measured from the report date, having the tender package ready the week the report lands rather than two months afterwards is most of the difference between comfortable and rushed.

What the survey cannot anticipate is the electrical findings, which vary with how the building has been maintained and altered over twenty-five years and which frequently include work nobody expected. Budget for them as a genuine unknown rather than as a contingency percentage on the envelope number, because they are not correlated with envelope condition at all.

What to do

If a notice has arrived, or is about to

Notices are issued between 1 June and 31 August for that calendar year, so by late November an owner who received one is already several months into a 180-day clock.

  1. Confirm the dates with the Building DivisionEstablish the date of the notice, the date the report is due, and what the municipality requires the report to cover. Different Broward municipalities administer this programme with different forms and different submission procedures, and published pages are not reliable. Get it from the Building Division and write it down.
  2. Appoint a licensed professional for both disciplinesThe report must address structure and electrical. Some firms cover both; many cover one and subcontract or decline the other. Establish this at appointment rather than discovering a gap at week twenty, and confirm the professional is licensed and acceptable to that municipality.
  3. Check whether the building is also subject to milestoneIf any portion is condominium or cooperative property of three or more habitable storeys, both regimes may apply. Ask the Building Official in writing what satisfies both and keep the reply. For a mixed-use or multi-parcel property, establish which obligation attaches to which portion before the engineering scope is set.
  4. Have the envelope quantified while the report is being producedDo not wait. The envelope findings are largely predictable on a building of this age, and quantifying them — linear feet of sealant by joint type, square feet of coating and substrate repair by depth, parapet and coping lengths, each by elevation — in parallel with the engineering means tendering can begin the week the report lands rather than two months after.
  5. Sequence structural, then electrical, then envelopeStructural repair breaks out substrate; electrical work at the envelope penetrates it. Both must precede final sealant and coating or that work is paid for twice. Set the sequence before any trade is engaged, because each will otherwise propose the sequence that suits their own mobilisation.
  6. Ask about an extension early if you need oneThe repair period is 180 days from the report date unless the Building Official allows longer. Officials do grant additional time, and they grant it to owners who approach early with an engineer engaged, a scope quantified and a programme, not to owners who appear a fortnight before expiry with nothing. Statewide, extensions under the milestone programme ran to roughly a fifth of completed inspections and were concentrated in coastal areas — they are obtainable, but they are obtained by being prepared.
  7. Keep the fileThe engineer's report, the quantified scope, the bids, the permits, the product approvals, the completion documentation by elevation and date, and the recertification itself. On a commercial building this file goes to lenders at refinancing, to insurers at renewal, to buyers in diligence, and to the Building Official again in ten years. Assembling it afterwards from invoices costs considerably more than keeping it as you go.

Where we fit

Our scope on a commercial recertification

Said plainly, because an owner engaging anyone on this should know what that party can and cannot lawfully take.

The scopes in the first column are among the job scopes the Florida Legislature placed outside state contractor licensing under Fla. Stat. §489.117(4)(a) — painting, waterproofing, caulking, pressure washing, plastering, stuccoing, and decorative stone, tile, marble, granite and terrazzo installation. The same provision bars a local government from requiring a state or local licence to obtain a permit for those job scopes.

Everything in the second column is contracted by the owner directly with the licensed party. We do not take it and subcontract it. Florida’s contracting statute treats the party responsible for a project as the contractor whether the work is performed by that party or by others under their contract, and contracts by unlicensed contractors are unenforceable under §489.128 — so an arrangement that routed licensed work through us would expose the owner, not protect them.

We also do not perform inspections, which means we never bid on findings we produced ourselves. On a programme where the same firms frequently offer inspection and repair together, that is worth stating.

For a commercial owner the division is usually simpler to administer than it sounds, because commercial buildings do not carry the condominium machinery. There is no board to convene, no owner vote for funding, no distribution obligation. An owner can appoint the engineer, appoint the structural and electrical contractors directly, and appoint us for the envelope, and sequence the four without a meeting. The constraint is the 180-day repair clock, not the governance.

Where a commercial property is held in a structure with lenders or multiple partners, the one thing worth settling at the start is who signs. We have seen the engineering completed and the repairs stall for six weeks on signature authority, with a Building Official deadline running throughout.

Questions

What owners ask

Are commercial buildings subject to Broward's Building Safety Inspection Program?

On the plain terms of the published exemption list, yes. The exemptions are buildings of the United States government and the State of Florida, buildings on sovereign tribal lands, Broward County School Board school buildings, small residential buildings of up to four units and three storeys or fewer, fee simple townhouses, structures with a floor area under 3,500 square feet, and railroads and related facilities. Commercial, retail, office, industrial and mixed-use property are not exempt. This is the principal difference from the state milestone inspection under Fla. Stat. 553.899, which applies only to condominium and cooperative property.

What are the age and size triggers?

Buildings 25 years of age or older, with structures under 3,500 square feet of floor area exempt as minor structures, inspected and then re-inspected every ten years. There is no height threshold, so a single-storey commercial building of 3,500 square feet or more that is twenty-five years or older is within the programme — which is the category of owner least likely to be aware of it, since milestone inspections require three or more habitable storeys.

How is this different from a milestone inspection?

Four main ways. It applies to buildings generally rather than only condominium and cooperative property. It covers electrical systems as well as structural ones, where the milestone inspection is structural only. It has no height threshold but does have a 3,500 square foot size threshold, which is the reverse of the milestone test. And its repair clock is shorter: 180 days from the report date to complete repairs unless the Building Official allows longer, against a milestone ordinance period of up to 365 days from the enforcement agency receiving a phase two report, measured to repairs being scheduled or commenced rather than completed.

When are notices issued and how long do we have?

Notices are sent by certified mail from the municipality's Building Official, issued between 1 June and 31 August for buildings due in that calendar year. The owner or association then has 180 days from receipt of the notice to submit a safety report from a licensed professional addressing structural and electrical safety. Where the report identifies deficiencies, repairs are to be completed within 180 days of the report date unless the Building Official specifies otherwise.

Does this satisfy the state milestone requirement?

Do not assume either way on the basis of a website, including this one. Many Broward buildings are subject to both and the engineering work overlaps substantially, so the two are commonly carried out and reported together. But the scopes are not identical — the county programme includes electrical and the milestone inspection does not; the milestone inspection has a defined phase one and phase two structure with its own unit owner reporting obligations that the county programme does not. Ask your municipality's Building Official in writing what will be accepted for both, and keep the reply.

What does the electrical portion typically find?

On a twenty-five to forty-year-old building in a salt atmosphere: corroded panels, disconnects and service equipment; deteriorated or damaged conductors; missing covers; grounding and bonding deficiencies; and exterior fixtures and conduit past service life. These findings require a licensed electrical contractor and are not envelope work. Owners who have prepared for this exercise by reading condominium milestone material are frequently surprised by the electrical component, because the milestone inspection does not include it.

Can one contractor take the whole repair scope?

Not lawfully, unless that contractor holds every licence the scope requires. Structural concrete repair needs a licensed contractor — on a building above three storeys specifically a certified general contractor, since a building contractor licence is limited to structures of three storeys or fewer. Electrical findings need a licensed electrical contractor. Florida's contracting statute treats the party responsible for a project as the contractor whether the work is performed by that party or by others under their contract, and contracts by unlicensed contractors are unenforceable under Fla. Stat. 489.128. Ask which licence each permitted portion will be pulled under and ask to see it.

In what order should the repairs be done?

Structural first, then electrical work at the envelope, then sealant and coating. Structural repair breaks out substrate and electrical work penetrates it, so any coating or sealant applied beforehand is paid for twice. Setting the sequence before engaging any trade matters, because each trade will otherwise propose the order that suits its own mobilisation, and on a 180-day repair clock there is no room to absorb a resequencing.
