Trades · Metalwork and architectural metal care

Metalwork care

Metal is the first thing on a waterfront house to tell you where you live. Railings bloom rust that nobody can explain because they are supposed to be stainless. Bronze goes from warm to black in two seasons. Gate hardware seizes. None of it is a manufacturing fault and almost none of it needs replacing — but all of it needs somebody who knows which metal they are looking at.

The premise

Chloride does not care what the invoice said

Airborne salt settles on every surface within a few hundred metres of open water, and it is hygroscopic — it pulls moisture out of the air and holds it against the metal long after the rain has gone.

It is always damp under the salt

A dry-looking railing in February is not dry where it matters. Deposited chloride draws humidity out of the air and maintains a thin film of salt solution against the surface, which is exactly the condition corrosion needs. This is why metal fails on elevations that never see rain, under soffits and on the sheltered side of the house.

Specification decides almost everything

Nearly every stainless problem on this coast is a grade problem. Nearly every aluminium problem is a coating or an isolation problem. Nearly every iron problem is a preparation problem. The metal that is failing was usually chosen by someone pricing a job inland, and no amount of maintenance fully rescues the wrong alloy in the wrong place.

Rinsing is the whole defence

The single highest-value thing anyone does for metal here costs nothing and takes minutes: fresh water over it, regularly, and especially after an onshore blow. Removing the chloride removes the mechanism. Most of the corrosion we are called to look at happened on properties where that has never once been done.

It is visible from the gate

Unlike most building failures, this one is on display. Pitted hardware, a streaked railing, a blackened lantern and a rust bloom down a cast-stone pier are the things a visitor registers before anything else, and they read as neglect even on a house where everything else is immaculate.

Replacement is usually the wrong answer

Owners are told to replace far more often than they need to be, because replacement is simpler to quote than remediation. Tea-stained stainless cleans and passivates. Blackened bronze cleans and waxes. Rusted iron is prepared and recoated. The exceptions are real but they are fewer than the industry suggests.

And the damage compounds inward

Surface discolouration is cosmetic. Pitting is not — it concentrates attack at a point and keeps going. Corrosion at an embedded fixing does not stay at the fixing; it expands and splits the masonry around it. Caught early these are cleaning jobs. Left alone they become masonry repair and, eventually, engineering.

The metals

Six materials, and the most common mistake is treating two of them as one

Architectural metal here is a small family with very different needs, and the distinction that matters most is invisible without asking.

Stainless — 304 against 316

The decisive fact on any coastal property. Grade 304 is the general-purpose stainless used almost everywhere inland and it corrodes in chloride. Grade 316 contains molybdenum, which substantially improves chloride resistance, and it is the marine grade. A 304 railing installed four hundred feet from the Atlantic will bloom rust within a year or two and the owner will be told the stainless was faulty. It was not; it was the wrong grade, and it was almost certainly chosen on price.

Bronze and brass

Copper alloys that develop a patina, and the first question is always whether that patina is wanted. Left alone outside here, bronze darkens toward black and brass goes dull and green at the edges. Neither is damage in the structural sense, but both are usually a surprise to an owner who chose the metal for its colour. Protected properly they hold a warm finish for years.

Wrought and mild iron

Gates, railings, window grilles, balcony rails, brackets, lanterns. Iron rusts; the only question is how well it is isolated from water and how well the coating was prepared. This is the metal where almost all of the failure is preparation rather than product, and where painting over rust — the universal shortcut — guarantees the coating comes back off in sheets within two seasons.

Aluminium

Usually anodised or powder-coated, occasionally mill finish. It forms its own oxide layer and resists general corrosion well, but it pits in chloride, chalks under ultraviolet, and is vulnerable to filiform corrosion creeping under a damaged coating from a scratch or a fixing hole. Anodising cannot be touched up the way paint can, which changes what repair means.

Copper and zinc

Roofing, gutters, downpipes, standing-seam work and decorative elements. They weather to a patina that is usually the point, and they are generally well-behaved. Their nuisance is what runs off them: copper runoff stains the stone and stucco beneath it, often permanently, and that staining is a drainage detail rather than a metal problem.

Galvanised and plated steel

Common on hardware, fixings, brackets and anything bought as a cost item. The protective zinc layer is sacrificial and finite, and in this environment it goes quickly. Once it does, the steel beneath is bare. Most seized hinges, weeping bracket fixings and rust streaks running down a wall from a single screw are this, and the answer is usually a better fastener rather than a repair.

Stainless

Tea staining, and why your stainless railing is rusting

It has a name, it is extremely common on this coast, and it is almost always cosmetic rather than structural — which is the opposite of what owners are usually told.

Stainless steel is not stainless because it does not corrode. It is stainless because chromium in the alloy reacts with oxygen to form an invisible passive layer of chromium oxide over the surface, and that layer is what protects the steel. It also repairs itself continuously, as long as it has oxygen and is not under chemical attack.

Chloride attacks it. Salt deposited on the surface breaks the passive film down locally, and where it breaks down the steel beneath begins to corrode. On a 304 railing near the ocean this shows first as a light brown or gold discolouration spreading across the surface, often in the direction of the prevailing wind. The industry term is tea staining, and in most cases it is confined to the surface and has not reduced the section of anything.

Three things make it worse and all three are decided before installation. Grade: 304 instead of 316. Surface finish: a rough or brushed finish holds chloride in its texture, while a fine, highly polished or electropolished surface sheds it, which is why two railings of identical grade can behave completely differently. And fabrication: welding leaves heat tint, a discoloured oxide scale beside the weld that has lost its chromium and is the first place corrosion starts. If it was not removed and the area re-passivated after fabrication, the welds will go first.

Pitting is the version that is not cosmetic. Instead of a general discolouration, attack concentrates at discrete points and drives downward, and crevice corrosion does the same thing in the oxygen-starved gap under a fitting, behind a flange or inside a joint. Pitting on a structural or guard element is a different conversation and gets looked at properly rather than polished.

The remedy for tea staining is cleaning and passivation. The surface is cleaned of chloride and of any embedded free iron — frequently picked up from a steel brush, grinder dust or an adjacent corroding fixing — and then treated, usually with a citric acid formulation, to restore the chromium oxide layer. Citric is preferred over nitric for this work: it is effective, far safer to handle, and does not require the handling precautions that make nitric unsuitable around a household. The reference standard for the treatment is ASTM A967.

How stainless is handled

  • Grade established before anything is proposed — 304 and 316 need different expectations
  • Welds and heat-tint areas checked first; they fail before the field does
  • Cleaning with non-ferrous media only; never a steel brush or shared abrasive
  • Embedded free iron identified and removed rather than polished over
  • Passivation with a citric acid formulation, to ASTM A967
  • Finish matched — a brushed grain is followed, not crossed
  • Pitting and crevice corrosion flagged rather than cosmetically treated
  • Fresh-water rinsing scheduled afterwards, because it is what makes the work last

Galvanic

Two metals, salt water, and a battery nobody designed

A great deal of coastal metal failure happens at the point where one metal is bolted to another, and it is entirely predictable from a chart.

When two dissimilar metals are in electrical contact and a conductive solution bridges them, you have a cell. One metal becomes the anode and corrodes preferentially; the other becomes the cathode and is protected. Salt water is an excellent electrolyte, and on this coast a film of it is present on almost every exterior surface almost all of the time.

How badly it behaves depends on how far apart the two metals sit in the galvanic series. Stainless fixings into aluminium is a common and aggressive pairing, and the aluminium goes. Aluminium brackets against steel does the same in reverse. Bronze against iron, galvanised fixings into copper, and any mild steel fastener into anything near the sea are all variations on it.

Relative area makes it much worse or much better, and this is the part that surprises people. A small stainless screw in a large aluminium panel is survivable, because the corrosion is spread over a wide anode. A small aluminium component bolted between large stainless members is eaten quickly. The same two metals, opposite outcome, decided by proportion.

The remedy is isolation, and it is cheap at the point of assembly and expensive afterwards: non-conductive washers and sleeves, isolation tape or membrane between faces, correct fastener selection, and keeping water out of the joint in the first place. Where we find a galvanic pairing already in place, the options are isolating it at the next service, changing the fastener, or coating one member — and sometimes accepting it and scheduling for it.

The version of this that becomes a building problem rather than a metal problem is steel embedded in masonry. A corroding anchor, balcony fixing, lintel or casement frame expands as it rusts to several times its original volume and splits the masonry from inside. That is rust jacking, it is the most expensive failure in this subject, and the straight crack with a rust bloom along it is the tell.

Where we find it

  • Stainless fixings through aluminium railings, shutters and frames
  • Mild steel fasteners used on anything exterior
  • Hinges, hasps and gate hardware of mixed metals
  • Light fittings and brackets fixed with whatever was in the van
  • Handrail brackets into cast stone with ferrous anchors
  • Dock and marine hardware, where the electrolyte is permanent
  • Copper or zinc runoff onto aluminium or galvanised elements below
  • Embedded steel in masonry — lintels, reinforcement, casement frames, balcony fixings

Copper alloys

Bronze and brass: wax or lacquer, and why it matters

Entry hardware, lanterns, gates, railings, sculpture, dock cleats and bathroom fittings. The decision here is about what you want the metal to look like in five years.

Left bare outside on this coast, bronze darkens steadily and eventually goes to a dark brown-black, and brass dulls and develops green copper salts at edges and runs. For some pieces that is the intended appearance and the correct answer is to let it happen evenly and keep it clean. For the entry hardware on a house that was chosen for its warmth, it is not.

There are two ways to protect it, and they behave very differently over time. A lacquer — the conservation standard being an acrylic resin containing benzotriazole as a corrosion inhibitor — forms a tough clear film and can hold a polished finish for several years. Its weakness is how it fails: not evenly, but in patches where it is worn or damaged, after which the metal tarnishes only in those places and the piece goes blotchy. Recovery means stripping the whole thing and starting again.

A microcrystalline wax is the alternative and generally the better one for anything handled or exposed. It is thinner, it is reversible, it fails gradually and evenly rather than in patches, and it is renewed by cleaning and reapplying rather than by stripping. It is the standard approach in museum and architectural conservation for exactly these reasons. The trade is that it needs doing more often, which on a property under a maintenance calendar is not a trade at all.

Either way the preparation decides the result. The surface is cleaned of chloride and corrosion products, worked back only as far as is genuinely needed, degreased completely, and coated while clean and dry. Over-polishing is the risk worth naming: aggressive buffing removes metal, softens cast detail and erases the history of a piece, and on antique or architectural bronze that loss is permanent. Less is almost always the correct instinct.

Interior brass and bronze — hardware, fittings, stair components — is a simpler problem and usually a lacquer or wax question decided by how much it is touched. Hands deposit salt and acid, which is why the brass around a handle goes first.

How we approach it

  • Agree the intended appearance before anything is cleaned
  • Identify whether an existing lacquer is present and how it is failing
  • Clean chloride and corrosion products with the gentlest effective method
  • Avoid aggressive buffing; cast detail and patina do not come back
  • Microcrystalline wax where the piece is handled, exposed or historic
  • Acrylic lacquer with corrosion inhibitor where a hard polished finish is wanted
  • Renewal scheduled rather than left until the finish is patchy
  • Antique, sculptural or significant pieces referred to a conservator

Ironwork

Painting over rust is why the gate is failing again

Iron is forgiving of almost everything except bad preparation, and bad preparation is the industry default because it is invisible on the day.

Rust is not a surface deposit sitting on sound metal. It is the metal, converted, and it continues converting underneath anything laid over it. A coating applied over rust is bonded to a layer that is itself detaching from the steel, so the coating comes away in sheets within a season or two, usually taking a plausible-looking film of paint with it and exposing metal in worse condition than before.

Correct preparation means taking the surface back. Hand and power tool cleaning will deal with light rust and loose material; heavier corrosion, pitting and old failed coatings want abrasive blasting, which on a removable gate or railing section is done off site and is the single best money spent on any ironwork job. The surface preparation standards exist precisely so that a specification can say which level was bought rather than leaving it to the day.

Then the coating system, which is three parts and not one. A zinc-rich or appropriate corrosion-inhibiting primer, which is what actually protects the steel. An intermediate build coat on anything substantial. And a topcoat chosen for ultraviolet and appearance rather than protection. Most failed ironwork here was given one coat of something decorative directly onto a surface that had been wire-brushed for ten minutes.

Rust converters have a place and are not a substitute. A tannic-acid converter chemically stabilises residual rust in pits that cannot be reached mechanically, and used that way within a proper system it is useful. Used instead of preparation, on a heavily corroded gate, it is a way of painting over the problem with an extra step.

The pieces that fail first are predictable and they are rarely the main members: the bottom rail where water sits, the foot of every post, the inside of hollow sections where water got in and cannot get out, fixing points, and the joints where two sections meet. Those are the places we look at first and the places a quick quote never mentions.

The ironwork sequence

  • Survey, with the bottom rails, post feet and fixings opened up and looked at
  • Removable sections taken off site where blasting is the right answer
  • Preparation to a named standard, not to whatever the day allows
  • Drainage of hollow sections checked and restored — water must be able to leave
  • Zinc-rich or inhibiting primer, correctly recoated within its window
  • Build coat where the section warrants it
  • Topcoat selected for ultraviolet at this latitude
  • Fixings replaced in a compatible metal, isolated where the pairing demands it

On a cycle

The cheapest intervention is fresh water

Everything in this subject responds to the same two actions, performed regularly rather than impressively.

Rinse the chloride off and renew the protective layer before it has gone. That is the whole programme. A railing rinsed after every onshore blow and waxed on a schedule will outlast an identical railing on the next property by years, and the difference in annual cost between the two is trivial compared with the difference in outcome.

What makes it work is that somebody is actually looking. Tea staining is easy at the point it is a faint gold cast and tedious once it is established. A wax film that is thinning is a fifteen-minute job; one that has gone is a clean-and-recoat. A seized hinge caught early is lubrication and a new fastener; left, it is a gate that has to come off. On the estate calendar the metal is walked, photographed and tested on every visit, and the work happens at the cheap end of each of those pairs.

The record matters here as much as anywhere. Which railings are 316 and which are 304, what was waxed and when, which fixings were replaced and in what metal, where a galvanic pairing is being managed rather than cured — that is not something anyone reconstructs later, and it is the difference between maintaining a property and rediscovering it annually.

There is also a household component, as with stone: a short written note on what may and may not be used on each metal. Chlorine-based cleaners on stainless, steel wool on anything, and a pressure washer on waxed bronze all undo a year of care in an afternoon, and nobody doing it knows.

Under the estate programme

  • All exterior metalwork walked and photographed on every visit
  • Fresh-water rinsing of metalwork after an onshore blow
  • Tea staining caught and treated while it is still a surface cast
  • Wax and protective coating renewed on a cycle, by element
  • Hardware, hinges, latches and gate furniture serviced and lubricated
  • Fixings checked for corrosion and galvanic pairing, replaced in kind
  • Light fittings and brackets cleaned and protected
  • A written record of grades, finishes, coatings and dates

The line

Where metalwork care ends

Ornamental iron installation is a named exempt job scope under Florida's contracting statute, and maintaining existing metal was never contracting. Structural and powered elements are a different matter.

Ours

  • Cleaning, polishing, waxing and protective coating of bronze, brass, copper and stainless
  • Stainless passivation and tea-stain remediation
  • Preparation and recoating of iron and steel railings, gates, grilles and brackets
  • Hardware service: hinges, latches, locks, gate furniture, shutter hardware
  • Corroded fixing replacement and galvanic isolation
  • Ornamental and decorative iron installation and repair
  • Light fitting and exterior lantern cleaning and protection
  • Metal condition survey, grade identification and a written care schedule

Not ours, and why

  • Structural steel and anything carrying load — engineer first
  • Guard and handrail replacement, which is permitted work with code loading and height requirements
  • Repair of embedded steel in masonry — rust jacking is a structural question
  • Gate operators, intercoms and anything powered — licensed electrical and low-voltage work
  • Marine and dock structural steel
  • Hurricane shutter and impact-opening installation
  • Anodising, re-plating and foundry work, which go to specialist shops
  • Conservation of significant sculpture or antique metalwork, which goes to a conservator

A guardrail is the one worth naming twice. Repairing and recoating an existing rail is maintenance; replacing one is permitted construction with code requirements for height and loading, and it is performed by a licensed contractor who contracts directly with you. We will tell you which side of that line a rail has fallen on, and corroded rails cross it more often than owners expect.

Questions

What owners ask

My stainless railing is rusting. Was it faulty?

Almost certainly not. It is far more likely to be grade 304 rather than 316. Both are genuine stainless, but 316 contains molybdenum and resists chloride far better, and it is the grade that belongs near the ocean. A 304 rail a few hundred metres from the Atlantic typically shows a light brown or gold cast within a year or two — the trade calls it tea staining and it is usually confined to the surface. It cleans and passivates, and with regular fresh-water rinsing it stays clean. Welds are the exception worth checking: if heat tint was never removed after fabrication, corrosion starts there first.

What is passivation?

Restoring the invisible chromium oxide layer that makes stainless steel stainless. That layer forms naturally and repairs itself, but chloride breaks it down locally and contamination with ordinary steel — from a wire brush, grinder dust or a corroding fixing nearby — prevents it reforming. Passivation cleans off the chloride and the embedded iron and chemically encourages the oxide layer back. We use citric acid formulations rather than nitric: as effective, far safer around a household, and the reference standard for the treatment is ASTM A967.

Should bronze be lacquered or waxed?

Waxed, for most things on a house. A microcrystalline wax is thinner, reversible, and fails gradually and evenly, so it is renewed by cleaning and recoating rather than by stripping. A lacquer gives a harder, longer-lasting polished finish but fails in patches where it is worn or damaged, after which the metal tarnishes only there and the piece looks blotchy — and the only way back is to strip it entirely. On a property under a maintenance calendar, the wax's more frequent renewal is not a drawback.

Can you make my bronze look new again?

Usually, and the more useful question is whether you want that. Bronze can be polished back to a bright finish, but aggressive buffing removes metal, softens cast detail and erases patina permanently, and on an antique or architectural piece that is a real loss. Our default is the gentlest treatment that achieves the appearance you want, agreed before anything is cleaned. Genuinely significant pieces we refer to a conservator rather than take on.

Why is the aluminium around my stainless screws corroding?

Galvanic corrosion. Two dissimilar metals in contact with salt water between them form a cell, and the less noble one — the aluminium — corrodes to protect the other. It is worse when a small stainless fastener sits in a large aluminium member, and worse again right at the coast where the electrolyte is always present. The remedy is isolation: non-conductive washers and sleeves, isolation tape between faces, and the right fastener. It is cheap at assembly and awkward afterwards, but it is usually correctable at the next service.

Do you need a licence for metalwork in Florida?

Not for this work. Ornamental iron installation is named in Fla. Stat. 489.117(4)(a) as an exempt job scope, and cleaning, polishing, passivating and recoating existing metal was never within the contracting statute. A business tax receipt and current insurance apply. Structural steel, anything load-bearing, guardrail replacement with its code height and loading requirements, powered gates and impact openings are licensed work and go to a licensed trade that contracts directly with you.

How often does exterior metal need attention here?

Rinsing: after every onshore blow, which in practice means routinely. Protective coatings: bronze and brass under wax generally want renewal once or twice a year on exposed elevations and less often where sheltered; a lacquer runs longer but fails harder. Painted ironwork on the coast is commonly on a three to five year cycle rather than the eight to ten it might manage inland, and the bottom rails and post feet will need attention before the rest of it does. All of those shorten on an oceanfront elevation and lengthen considerably on a property where the rinsing actually happens.

There is a rust stain running down my cast stone from a railing. Can it be removed?

Sometimes, and the order matters. The source has to be dealt with first — a corroding fixing or an unprotected ferrous element — or the stain returns. Then the stone is treated: iron staining is drawn out with a poultice using a dedicated rust remover, which on calcareous stone is often acidic and means any etch has to be corrected afterwards. Where the iron has oxidised within the body of the stone rather than been deposited on it, the stain is frequently permanent, and we would rather test a small area and tell you that than sell a series of attempts.
