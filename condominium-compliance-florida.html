Condominiums · Boards and managers

The inspection is the easy part

A great deal has been written about Florida’s milestone inspection and reserve study requirements, and most boards now understand what they are. Far less has been written about the part that costs money and takes years: turning a report into quantified scope, comparable bids, a funding vote, permits, and finished work on a building people are living in. That is what this section is about.

Where boards get stuck

Six stages, and the four that go wrong

The inspection stages are well documented and well served by professionals. The repair stages are where associations lose years and money, and they are barely written about at all.

StageWho drives itWhere it goes wrong
1 · Inspection commissionedBoard, engineer or architectRarely. This stage is well understood.
2 · Report received and distributedBoard and managerOccasionally — the 45-day owner distribution is missed, or the summary goes out without explanation and the owners panic.
3 · Scope quantifiedNobody, usuallyConstantly. The engineer’s report is a condition assessment, not a bill of quantities. Boards tender a narrative and get three prices that cannot be compared.
4 · Bids obtained and comparedBoard, managerConstantly. Different inclusions, different unit rates, different assumptions about access and substrate. The 5% competitive bidding rule is met in form and defeated in substance.
5 · Funding securedBoard, ownersOften. The vote is started after prices arrive instead of alongside, adding months to a fixed clock.
6 · Work permitted, sequenced, completedContractors, building departmentOften. Phasing decided by cash rather than plan; no documented completion record for the next study.

The four highlighted stages have something in common: no professional is clearly responsible for them. The engineer’s appointment ends at the report, or extends to design and administration but not to quantifying the work for tender. The manager runs the association, not a construction programme. The contractors arrive with prices built on their own assumptions, because nobody gave them a common basis to price against.

That is where the cost gets lost. Not in anyone’s margin — in the gap between a report that describes condition and a contract that commits to quantities. A board that closes that gap before tendering gets three prices it can compare, a scope it can phase, and a record it can hand to the next reserve study. A board that does not gets a number, a hope, and change orders.

Everything on this page is about those four stages. The two inspection stages are covered thoroughly elsewhere on this site and by a great many professionals, and we see no value in writing it again.

One structural reason these stages are orphaned is worth naming. The engineer is engaged under a professional services contract that Fla. Stat. §718.3026 exempts from competitive bidding, so there is rarely commercial pressure on the scope of that appointment. The contractors are engaged under a contract that must be competitively bid. The work of defining what is being bid falls between the two, and whoever ends up doing it is usually doing it for free, at speed, and without visiting the building.

The second reason is time. The repair clock is fixed — a county ordinance must require repairs to be scheduled or commenced within no later than 365 days of the enforcement agency receiving a phase two report — and the funding vote is not. Special assessments, lines of credit and loans each require a majority of the total voting interests, which means notice, a meeting and a quorum. A board that starts the funding process after prices arrive has subtracted two or three months from a clock it cannot extend by choice.

None of this is legal advice and we are not lawyers. It is a plain reading of statutes that are long and amended frequently, written from the construction side, so that a board can ask its counsel, its engineer and its contractors better questions. Confirm any date or obligation that bears on a decision against the current statute and with the association’s attorney.

Act this month

One deadline is about five weeks away

Dated deliberately, because it expires.

An association whose milestone inspection is due by 31 December 2026 may have the structural integrity reserve study performed alongside it — but in no event later than 31 December 2026. Where both are outstanding, that is a hard outside date and it falls about five weeks from now.

Two reasons to care beyond the deadline itself. Commissioning both together is materially cheaper than commissioning them separately. And a milestone inspection completed within the past five years can substitute for the visual portion of the reserve study, which removes duplicated work from the fee.

If that applies to your building and nothing has been commissioned, this is the week. There is nothing we can do about it and nothing we want to sell you for it — it is an engineer, an architect or a certified reserve specialist. We mention it because boards are reading pages like this one in November and the date is not widely flagged.

Two further dates to hold in view, both of which have already passed and both of which still generate work. Buildings that reached thirty years before 1 July 2022 were due to have completed their milestone inspection before 31 December 2024; buildings reaching thirty between 1 July 2022 and 31 December 2024 were due by 31 December 2025. An association that has missed either is in a different posture from one working to a future deadline, and that is a conversation for counsel rather than for a contractor.

Note also that a local enforcement agency may set the first milestone inspection at twenty-five years rather than thirty where local conditions — the statute gives proximity to salt water as the example — warrant it. On a barrier island or an intracoastal building, do not assume thirty.

Separately and without a deadline attached: through 31 December 2028, an association that completed a milestone inspection in the previous two calendar years may vote — by a majority of total voting interests — to pause or reduce reserve contributions for up to two consecutive annual budgets in order to pay for the repairs that inspection identified. After the pause a reserve study must be performed before contributions resume. It is a deferral mechanism with a condition attached, and it is the only flexibility most associations now have.

Quantifying

What a quantified scope actually contains

This is stage three, the one nobody owns, and it is the single highest-leverage document in a repair programme.

  1. Elevation by elevation, not building-wideNorth, south, east and west do not fail at the same rate and should never be priced as an average. A south and west elevation taking afternoon sun and driving rain will be two to three times the condition problem of a north elevation on the same building. Surveying and pricing by elevation is what makes phasing possible later.
  2. Linear feet of sealant, by joint typePerimeter window and door joints, control and expansion joints, panel joints, dissimilar-material junctions, railing penetrations, through-wall penetrations. Each counted, each with its existing joint dimension recorded, because joint width governs the backer rod size and the sealant choice and a 2:1 width-to-depth ratio is what makes the joint survive movement.
  3. Square feet of coating, by substrate and conditionSound substrate, substrate requiring patching, substrate requiring removal and rebuild. Stucco, concrete, precast, previously coated with an unknown system. Each category prices differently because each takes different preparation, and “paint the building” prices the whole of it at the rate of the worst of it or the best of it, depending on who is bidding.
  4. Counts of repeated elementsBalcony edges, soffit areas, railing posts and base plates, drip edges, copings, window and door openings. Repeated elements are where a unit rate is genuinely reliable, and they are where a per-unit price in the bid lets a board verify a lump sum rather than accept it.
  5. Failure mode, not just conditionA sealant joint that has failed adhesively at one face is a different repair from one that has failed cohesively through the middle, and both are different from one that was installed at the wrong dimension and is failing because of it. Recording why a thing failed is what stops the same failure being specified again.
  6. Photographs, referenced and datedEvery condition category photographed, every photograph keyed to elevation and location. This is the evidence base for the bid comparison, the change order argument, the warranty claim and the next reserve study. It costs almost nothing to produce during the survey and cannot be reconstructed afterwards.
  7. A written specification, products namedSealant to ASTM C920 with the class and grade stated. Coating system with the manufacturer, the product, the number of coats and the wet and dry film thickness. Surface preparation and substrate moisture limits. Without this, three bidders price three different systems and the cheapest is the one proposing the least.

The 5% rule

Competitive bidding, and the exemption that surprises boards

Section 718.3026 is better known than it is understood, and one of its exceptions explains something boards find confusing.

Where an association’s payments for materials, equipment or services exceed 5 per cent of its total annual budget including reserves, it must obtain competitive bids. Contracts for services, and contracts for materials or equipment not fully performed within a year, must be in writing. A facade restoration on almost any association clears the 5 per cent threshold comfortably, so the bidding requirement applies.

The association is expressly not required to accept the lowest bid, which is worth telling owners early and in writing. A board that has documented why it did not take the lowest number is in a far better position than a board that took it because it felt obliged to.

Now the exception that explains a pattern boards notice and misread. Contracts for the services of attorneys, accountants, architects, community association managers, timeshare management firms, engineers and landscape architects are exempt from the competitive bidding requirement. So the engineer who performs your inspection need not be competitively bid. The contractor who performs the repairs the engineer recommends must be.

That asymmetry is not an oversight. Professional services are selected on judgement and fit; construction is selected on price against a defined scope. But it does mean a board can find itself with a single-sourced engineer and a competitively bid contractor, and it should understand which of those two decisions it has actually tested.

The other exceptions, briefly: associations of ten or fewer units may opt out by a two-thirds vote of owners; contracts with association employees are exempt; the section does not limit obtaining products and services in an emergency; and it does not apply where the business is the only source of supply in the county. The emergency exception is real and is also the one most often stretched. A deadline the board has known about for eighteen months is not an emergency.

Our page on tendering a restoration goes through unit pricing, the inclusions that differ between bids, and how to put three prices on the same basis. It is the practical companion to this section.

Conflicts

We do not inspect, so we never bid on our own findings

HB 913 added a disclosure regime to both the milestone and reserve study statutes. It is worth understanding what it was aimed at.

An architect or engineer bidding to perform an inspection or a reserve study must disclose in writing any intent to bid on the repair work their own report will recommend. Separately, a design professional or contractor bidding on that recommended work may not hold a direct or indirect interest in the firm that performed the inspection or study, nor be a relative of someone who does, unless the relationship is disclosed in writing. The relationship test in the reserve study provision runs to the third degree.

Where the required disclosure has not been made, the consequence is not a fine. The contract may be voided and terminated, and the professional may face disciplinary action.

The conflict the Legislature was addressing is specific and easy to state: the party who tells you how much work your building needs also selling you the work. The arrangement is not prohibited. It must be disclosed, and a board that has never asked has not protected itself.

We are on one side of that line by construction, not by claim. Amalfi does not perform milestone inspections, does not perform reserve studies, and holds no interest in any firm that does. We cannot bid on our own findings because we never produce the findings. When we survey a building, the engineer’s report already exists and we are quantifying against it.

The practical step for a board takes one email and we would say this even about ourselves: ask every bidder, in writing, to confirm whether they hold any direct or indirect interest in the firm that performed your inspection or study, and whether any relative of theirs does. Keep the replies. It is required of them in any event, and it is the kind of document that matters a great deal if a dispute ever arrives.

A survey produced by a party with no stake in the engineering is a differently motivated document from one produced by a party who will profit from the scope it recommends. Not automatically a better one — but the board should know which it is holding, and should be able to show that it asked.

Scope boundary

Which licence pulls which permit

Structural repair and envelope restoration arrive in the same report and are routinely bid as one package. They are not the same work and they are not performed under the same authority.

The distinction is not presentational. Florida’s contracting statute defines a contractor as the party responsible for a project whether the work is performed by that party or by others under their contract. A firm holding no general contractor certification cannot lawfully take the structural scope on a high-rise and subcontract it out, and a board that permits that arrangement has a contract that may be unenforceable under §489.128 and an insurance position that may not survive examination.

So the question to put to every bidder is narrow and answerable: which licence will each permitted portion be pulled under, and may we see it? A bidder who names the licence and produces it has told you something. A bidder who explains that it is all handled has told you more.

One further wrinkle specific to tall buildings. Among the few carve-outs preserved from the Legislature’s preemption of local contractor licensing is rooftop painting, coating and cleaning above three storeys, which a municipality may still licence if it did so before January 2021. On a mid-rise or high-rise that is a question to settle before mobilising.

The sequencing point deserves more weight than it usually gets, because it is where money is actually lost. Structural repair breaks out substrate; window and door replacement opens the envelope; electrical and mechanical work at the perimeter penetrates it. Every one of those must precede final sealant and coating, or that sealant and coating is bought twice. On a programme running to a fixed repair deadline there is no slack to absorb a resequencing, so the order should be set before any trade is engaged rather than negotiated between trades as they mobilise.

This is also the practical argument for quantifying and specifying the envelope early even where the envelope work will run last. The quantities and the specification are what let the board hold the envelope tender ready and release it the week the structural contractor clears an elevation, instead of starting a tender process at that point.

Questions

What owners ask

Who is responsible for turning the engineer's report into a scope we can tender?

In practice, often nobody, and that is where most of the cost and delay in a repair programme originates. The engineer's appointment typically ends at the report or extends to design and contract administration, neither of which produces a bill of quantities. The manager runs the association. Contractors price against their own assumptions. Someone has to convert condition into quantities — linear feet of sealant by joint type, square feet of coating by substrate and condition, counts of repeated elements, each by elevation — before three prices can be compared on the same basis. That conversion is a distinct piece of work and a board should commission it deliberately rather than assume it is included.

Do we have to competitively bid the repair work?

Yes, in almost all cases. Under Fla. Stat. 718.3026, where an association's payments for materials, equipment or services exceed 5 per cent of its total annual budget including reserves, competitive bids must be obtained, and contracts for services must be in writing. A facade restoration clears that threshold on nearly any association. The association is expressly not required to accept the lowest bid. Exceptions include associations of ten or fewer units opting out by a two-thirds owner vote, contracts with association employees, genuine emergencies, and a sole source of supply within the county.

Does the engineer's contract have to be competitively bid too?

No. Section 718.3026 exempts contracts for the services of attorneys, accountants, architects, community association managers, timeshare management firms, engineers and landscape architects from the competitive bidding requirement. So the inspecting engineer may be single-sourced while the repair contractor must be bid. Boards should understand which of those two decisions they have actually tested on price, because the engineering appointment shapes the scope that everything else is priced against.

Can the firm that inspected our building also do the repairs?

Only with written disclosure, and the obligation is statutory following HB 913. An architect or engineer bidding to perform an inspection or reserve study must disclose in writing any intent to bid on the repair work their report recommends. A design professional or contractor bidding on that recommended work may not hold a direct or indirect interest in the inspecting firm, nor be a relative of someone who does, unless disclosed in writing. Where disclosure has not been made the contract may be voided and terminated and the professional may face discipline. Ask every bidder the question in writing and keep the answers.

What deadline is closest right now?

An association whose milestone inspection is due by 31 December 2026 may have the structural integrity reserve study performed alongside it, but in no event later than 31 December 2026. Where both are outstanding that is a hard outside date. Commissioning them together is cheaper than separately, and a milestone inspection completed within the past five years can substitute for the visual portion of the reserve study. Separately, through 31 December 2028 an association that completed a milestone inspection in the previous two calendar years may vote by a majority of total voting interests to pause or reduce reserve contributions for up to two consecutive budgets to pay for the identified repairs, after which a reserve study must be performed before contributions resume.

Can reserves still be waived?

For unit-owner-controlled associations required to obtain a structural integrity reserve study, no. For budgets adopted on or after 31 December 2024, members may not vote to waive or reduce reserves for the statutory components, and those funds may not be used for any other purpose. The statutory components are the roof; the structure, including load-bearing walls and primary structural members and systems; fireproofing and fire protection systems; plumbing; electrical; waterproofing and exterior painting; and windows and exterior doors; plus any other item with a deferred maintenance or replacement cost above $25,000 whose failure would negatively affect those items. The narrow exception is a multicondominium association operating a Division-approved alternative funding method.

Which licence does the repair contractor need?

It depends on the scope. Structural concrete repair, roofing, window and door replacement, electrical, plumbing and mechanical work all require a licensed contractor pulling the permit in their own name, and on a condominium above three storeys the structural scope requires a certified general contractor — a building contractor licence is limited to structures of three storeys or fewer. Florida's contracting statute treats the party responsible for a project as the contractor whether the work is performed by that party or by others under their contract, so an unlicensed or inadequately licensed firm cannot take the scope and subcontract it, and contracts by unlicensed contractors are unenforceable under Fla. Stat. 489.128. The envelope job scopes — painting, caulking, waterproofing, stone and decorative work — are placed outside state licensing by Fla. Stat. 489.117(4)(a).

Can we phase the work across budget years?

Envelope and non-structural work, often yes, where the engineer agrees the sequencing does not worsen the condition and the association documents the plan. Items identified as substantial structural deterioration carry a repair clock and are not generally candidates for deferral. Where phasing is used, surveying and pricing by elevation is what makes it possible — a south and west elevation taking afternoon sun and driving rain is usually a materially worse condition problem than a north elevation on the same building, and that is the natural first phase. Document which elevations fall in which year and why. Phasing by plan and phasing by cash shortage are visibly different in the file.
