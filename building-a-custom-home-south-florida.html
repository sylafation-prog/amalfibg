Building here · The whole arc

Building a custom home on this coast, from the land to the certificate of occupancy

Most guides to building a house begin with the house. On this coast that is the wrong end. Between Naples and Palm Beach, through Fort Lauderdale and Miami, the parcel decides more than the design does — how high the floor sits, what the glass must survive, when work may happen and how much of the lot is yours to use. This page is written for an owner who has bought land, or is about to, and has not yet appointed anybody. Read it before you do.

The parcel

Six things the land has already decided

None of these is a design choice. Each is fixed by a map, a code or an ordinance before you arrive, each is published, and together they describe the house more exactly than a brief does.

How high the floor sits

In a flood hazard area the lowest floor is set by a number, not by taste. The Florida Building Code, Residential puts it at the base flood elevation plus one foot or the locally adopted design flood elevation, whichever is higher, and a municipality may ask for more. Height limits are measured from a fixed datum, so every foot the floor rises is a foot taken from the top. The mechanism is set out here.

How much of the lot is yours

Setbacks, lot coverage, impervious area, floor area ratio where the town uses one, and height. On the water there is a separate waterward setback. The house standing on the lot today is no guide: it may be a legal non-conforming structure, and that status belongs to the old building, not to its replacement. What fits on the lot works through each control.

What the wind requires

Miami-Dade and Broward are the High Velocity Hurricane Zone, with their own product approval protocols. Palm Beach County and Collier County are not, and qualify openings under different test standards. Which side of that line the parcel sits on decides the glass, the roof assembly and the supplier list before a single elevation is drawn.

When work may happen

Several towns on this coast restrict or suspend construction through the season, and some prohibit the loudest work outright. That is not a noise rule in the usual sense. It is a calendar the structure has to be sequenced around, and it is set by the municipality the parcel happens to fall in.

What is under the surface

Soil, and the water table. On a coastal lot the foundation follows the borings, and the borings can change the structural approach entirely. A geotechnical report is a small cost that belongs before design, because it is the document most capable of rewriting a budget after the fact.

What is already there

A house to demolish, protected trees, easements, a seawall with a remaining life, a dock that may not be replaceable like for like, and a permit history that may include open permits. All of it transfers to you at closing, and almost all of it can be read beforehand.

That is why the order matters. An owner who commissions drawings first learns these six things from the drawings being rejected. An owner who reads the parcel first hands the designer a set of constraints, and the first scheme is one that fits.

The coast

Four markets, and they are not one market

Owners move along this coast as though it were a single place. For building purposes it is four regimes, and the differences are in the code rather than in the taste.

MarketHurricane zoneHow openings are approvedWhat tends to govern
Miami and Miami-DadeHVHZ. Risk Category II design wind speed 175 mphMiami-Dade Notice of Acceptance or Florida product approval, tested to TAS 201, 202 and 203The product approval system, flood elevation, and the density of what is already built around the lot
Fort Lauderdale and BrowardHVHZ. Risk Category II design wind speed 170 mphThe same TAS protocols as Miami-DadeThe county’s future-conditions flood map, the tidal seawall standard, and canal-lot access
Palm Beach and Palm Beach CountyOutside the HVHZFlorida product approval on ASTM E1886 and E1996Architectural review boards, the barrier-island towns’ working seasons, and access
Naples and Collier CountyOutside the HVHZ. Gulf coastFlorida product approval on ASTM E1886 and E1996Its own flood exposure, wind maps and municipal rules, which are read locally and not inferred from the Atlantic side

The standards are not interchangeable. Under the HVHZ protocols the test missile may not penetrate the system; under the statewide standard it may, provided the opening stays within defined limits afterwards. A Miami-Dade approved product satisfies the statewide standard, and the reverse is not true, so a window specified for a house in Naples or Palm Beach may be inadequate in Fort Lauderdale. Published figures put the cost of building inside the zone at roughly eight to twelve per cent above a comparable market outside it. The hurricane zone page covers the tests themselves.

The architect

Florida does not require an architect for a house. Hire one anyway, and choose on this

It is the question almost every owner at this stage is carrying, and the statute answers it in a way few people expect.

Section 481.229(1)(b) of the Florida Statutes exempts “any one-family or two-family residence building, townhouse, or domestic outbuilding” appurtenant to one from the requirement that plans carry a registered architect’s seal, and it adds the words regardless of cost. A modest house inland and a very large one on the ocean are the same category under Chapter 481. Subsection (1)(c) separately exempts any other building costing less than $25,000, leaving out schools, auditoriums and buildings for public use.

The exemption removes a legal requirement. It does not remove any of the technical work. A building official still wants a complete document set, sealed structural engineering, a product approval for every opening and assembly, energy calculations, and in a flood zone the flood design documentation. Most of that is the engineer’s seal, not the architect’s. So the honest reading is that the law tells you what you are buying when you appoint an architect here: not permission, but design and the coordination of everyone else’s drawings. On a house of any ambition that is worth paying for, and an incomplete set is recovered from the owner later in change orders.

Which leaves the question that actually separates one architect from another on this coast, and it is not talent. It is whether they have built here. An architect who has knows that an opening is a tested assembly, that the frame, the anchorage and the substrate are part of the approval, and that a window dimension chosen for the elevation has to exist as an approved product at that size and pressure. One who has not will draw a wall of glass that nobody is approved to supply.

The same architect knows that the flood datum is the first design fact rather than a compliance check at the end. The required floor height decides the entry sequence, the number of risers between the motor court and the front door, where the garage can sit, how the pool terrace meets the living room and what happens to the storey count under the height limit. A house designed at grade and then lifted to comply is recognisable from the street.

And they know the calendar. In the barrier-island towns a structure has to be sequenced around the season, which reaches back into how the house is framed and when the drawings must be finished. Finally, they start from the survey. A scheme drawn before the boundary, the topography and the zoning envelope are established is usually dismantled at the second zoning meeting, at the owner’s expense.

Questions that sort architects quickly

  • Which houses have you taken through permit in this municipality
  • What was the design flood elevation on the last one, and what set it
  • How do you confirm an opening exists as an approved product at that size
  • Which test standard applies to this parcel, TAS or ASTM
  • Has this town a construction season, and how did it shape your last programme
  • Do you begin from a current survey, or from the listing
  • Who is your structural engineer, and when do they join
  • When, during design, is the scheme first priced by a builder

The sequence

From land to certificate of occupancy, in the order it works

Each step produces the information the next one needs. Skipping one does not save the time; it moves the discovery to a point where it costs more.

  1. Read the parcel before the contract is firmZoning district and the code section behind it, flood zone and elevation, overlays, the town’s working season, any review board, the permit history of the existing structure, and on the water the seawall and dock. Everything here is public. It is the cheapest work in the whole project and the only stage at which walking away is free.
  2. Commission the surveysA current boundary and topographic survey, a tree survey where there is canopy, and an elevation certificate in a flood zone. These fix the buildable rectangle and the ground. The survey from the last sale is not a substitute.
  3. Test the soilBorings and a geotechnical report establish bearing capacity and the water table, and with them whether the house sits on conventional footings or on piles. It belongs before design, not after schematic approval.
  4. Appoint the designer, with the constraints in handArchitect or, lawfully in Florida, a residential designer working with an engineer. They receive the survey, the envelope and the soil report on the first day, which is what makes the first scheme a real one.
  5. Clear the boards that sit outside any clockWhere a town has an architectural or historic review board, or a homeowners’ association has an architectural committee, its approval comes before the building permit and runs on its own meeting calendar. Since 1 July 2026 an association may not require a building permit as a condition of its architectural review, which removes one circular delay.
  6. Bring a licensed builder in while the drawings can still changeAround the midpoint of design, a builder can price the structure and name the details that cost out of proportion. The same exercise on finished drawings only reports the gap. Check the licence class against the house before the conversation goes further; the section on who may do the work explains why.
  7. Engineer it, and close the product approvalsStructural, civil, mechanical, electrical and plumbing. Every opening, roof assembly and shutter is specified to a documented approval. This is also when long-lead items are identified, because glazing and switchgear ordered after permit arrive after they are needed.
  8. Apply for the permitThe application starts a statutory clock measured in business days. The contractor is the applicant on a contracted house. Zoning, landscape, engineering and floodplain review run alongside the building review and are not all inside the same deadline.
  9. Record the notice of commencementBefore work begins, section 713.13 requires the owner to record a notice of commencement with the clerk and post a copy on site, and the building department will want it before the first inspection. It is the owner’s document, and it is what the construction lien law is organised around.
  10. Site, foundation and shellDemolition where there is a house, fill and compaction, piles or footings, the slab, and then the structure. In a flood zone the building department will want the floor elevation documented by a surveyor as the work proceeds, not only at the end. Ask at what stage.
  11. Dry-in, rough-ins and inspectionsRoof on, openings installed to their approvals, then the mechanical, electrical and plumbing rough-ins, each inspected before it is covered. The sequence of inspections is the real schedule of a house, and a failed one holds everything behind it.
  12. Finishes, finals and the certificateInterior finishes, final inspections by each discipline, the final survey and elevation certificate, and close-out of every sub-permit. The certificate of occupancy issues only when all of them are closed. Section 553.837 then requires the builder to warrant a newly constructed home for one year against defects that amount to a material violation of the building code.

No way back

The decisions that cannot be undone, and when each one locks

A custom house feels endlessly adjustable while it is being designed. It is not. A handful of decisions close early, and each is expensive in proportion to how late it is reopened.

DecisionWhen it locksWhat reopening it costs
The parcel itselfAt closingEverything. The flood position, the envelope, the town and its calendar are bought with the land
Finished floor elevationBefore schematic designThe entry sequence, the stair, the terraces, the drainage and the storey count all hang from it
Position of the house on the lotAt zoning or board approvalA fresh submission, and in a town with a review board a fresh hearing
Foundation typeWhen the soil report is acceptedThe structural design, and usually the price
Structural systemAt permit submissionRe-engineering and a revision through plan review
Opening sizes and locationsWhen the shell is formedAn opening is a structural element with an approved product in it. Moving one is not a finish change

The pattern is that the cheap decisions come first and are made with the least information. The parcel is chosen before the survey, the floor height before the design, the foundation before the price. That is the whole case for doing the reading early: it is the only way to make the irreversible decisions knowingly.

The people

What each professional is for, and what none of them is paid to do

A custom house is produced by six or seven separate appointments. Each has a defined product, and the gaps between them are where owners lose money.

The surveyor

Produces the boundary, the topography, the trees and, in a flood zone, the elevation certificate. Returns during construction to confirm the building is where and as high as it was drawn, and at the end for the final survey. The first appointment and one of the last.

The geotechnical engineer

Produces a report on what the ground will carry and where the water is. Does not design the foundation; tells the structural engineer what it must be designed for. On piled work, often returns to observe installation.

The architect or designer

Produces the design and, properly engaged, coordinates the consultants’ drawings into one set. Not legally required for a house in Florida, and on a significant one usually the appointment that decides whether the result is a house or an assembly of competent parts.

The structural engineer

Produces the sealed structure: wind loads, the load path from roof to footing, the foundation, and the anchorage of every opening. In practice the seal a building official cares most about, and the consultant whose early involvement saves the most redrawing.

The civil engineer

Produces the site: grading, drainage, stormwater retained on your own land, utility connections, and the driveway’s meeting with a floor that sits higher than the road. On a low lot this is a larger piece of work than owners expect.

The licensed contractor

Holds the contract with you, is the permit applicant, engages the trades and is answerable for the work. The licence is the point. Every certified and registered contractor appears in the Department of Business and Professional Regulation’s public register, with the class and its status.

What none of them is engaged to do is read the parcel before you own it. The surveyor is hired after the contract, the architect after the closing, the builder after the drawings. The decisions with the largest consequences are made in the one period when nobody is retained to inform them. Who to hire first sets out the order of appointments in more detail.

The money

Where the money goes, and the two statutes that protect it

A cost per square foot describes a finished house. It is not a budget for a new one, and it says nothing about how the money moves while the house is being built.

Published 2026 figures from builders active in this market put high-end production custom work on the mainland at roughly $450 to $650 per square foot, bespoke custom at $650 to $900, direct waterfront at $900 to $1,300, and oceanfront or overlay-constrained sites from $1,300 upward, with Palm Beach County commonly ten to twenty-five per cent above comparable Miami-Dade waterfront. Those are market data, not our prices. The cost page sets out what the bands exclude, which is most of what surprises people: land, demolition, design fees, permits and impact fees, landscape, pool, dock, seawall and furniture.

Inside the hard cost, the money does not follow the floor plan. It follows the structure and the envelope first, because in a hurricane region the frame, the roof and the glass are engineered assemblies with a narrow field of approved suppliers. Then the foundation, which the soil and the flood elevation set between them. Then the systems, and last the finishes, which is the category an owner’s taste moves most and usually discovers latest.

Florida’s construction lien law is the part almost no owner reads before signing, and it governs every payment. Under Chapter 713, a subcontractor or supplier you have never met can lien the property if they are not paid, even where you have paid the contractor in full. The mechanism the statute gives you is the notice of commencement, recorded before work starts, and the notices to owner that those subcontractors and suppliers must serve within forty-five days of starting. Each notice names someone whose release you should hold before the corresponding payment leaves your account. The statute’s own warning is printed in capitals on the permit paperwork: failure to record a notice of commencement may result in paying twice.

The notice also expires, and section 713.13 treats payments made after its expiry as improper payments. On a house that runs long, renewing it is the owner’s task and it is routinely missed.

The second protection concerns deposits. Under section 489.126, a contractor who takes more than ten per cent of the contract price as an initial payment on residential work must apply for the necessary permits within thirty days of that payment and start work within ninety days of the permits issuing, unless there is just cause or you have agreed a longer period in writing. A request for a large deposit against a vague start date is worth reading against that section.

Holding the money properly

  • Record the notice of commencement before work starts
  • Note its expiry date, and renew before it lapses
  • Keep every notice to owner that arrives by post
  • Match a release to each notice before each payment
  • Read the deposit against section 489.126
  • Ask who holds the contingency, and who keeps what is unspent
  • Get the exclusions list, and price what is on it
  • Budget land, soft costs and landscape as separate lines

The calendar

What the calendar looks like once both clocks are applied

There are two calendars on a house here. One is in the statutes and runs in business days. The other is in the town’s code and runs in seasons. Programmes fail when only the first is read.

The state clock is in section 553.792. A local government has thirty business days to decide a residential permit for a structure under 7,500 square feet, and sixty for one at or above that size, which is where most significant custom houses fall. It has five business days to say what is missing from an application, failing which the application is deemed complete. Where it finds deficiencies, the applicant has ten business days to correct them and the jurisdiction ten more to decide. A missed deadline reduces the permit fee by ten per cent for each business day, rising to twenty after a resubmittal. It does not produce a deemed approval. The permit calendar has the full schedule.

A private provider under section 553.791 can take plan review, inspections or both out of the building department’s queue, and the statute requires the fee to be reduced accordingly. It is worth understanding what it does not reach. Zoning, landscape and engineering review stay with the municipality, as do floodplain review and the fire prevention codes, and a provider may not review a building it designed or built.

None of that covers the approvals that precede the permit. A review board meets when it meets. A variance has a notice period and a hearing. An association’s committee runs on no statutory clock at all. On the water, the seawall and dock are separate permits from separate authorities. These are routinely the longest items in the programme and the least discussed at the outset.

Then the season. In several barrier-island towns, construction inside a defined season is limited to weekdays, and the loudest categories of work are prohibited outright: pile driving, dredging, jackhammering, and in at least one town the mixing and pumping of concrete. A concrete frame cannot be poured where pumping is prohibited, so the structure goes up before the season opens or waits until it closes. A permit that issues in the wrong month can cost most of a year without any party being late. The ordinance for the specific town is read before a programme is written, not after.

Two dates sit inside any project starting now. The 9th Edition (2026) Florida Building Code takes effect on 31 December 2026, and an application filed before then remains under the 8th Edition. And a single-family permit now expires one year after issue or at the next code edition, whichever is later, which matters on a long build with sub-permits.

What sets the real length

  • Review board and association calendars, before the permit
  • Whether the house is under or over 7,500 square feet
  • How complete the application is on the day it is filed
  • Rounds of comments, at ten business days a side
  • Zoning, floodplain and fire review, outside the private provider route
  • The town’s season, and what it prohibits
  • Seawall and dock permits on a waterfront lot
  • Lead times for glazing, switchgear and lifts
  • Hurricane season, on any site with an open structure

Who may do it

Who is allowed to build the house, and the permit to be wary of

Florida is strict about who may contract for construction, and the rules are short enough for an owner to check personally.

Chapter 489 creates three classes relevant to a house. A general contractor’s scope is unlimited. A building contractor is limited to buildings not exceeding three stories. A residential contractor is limited to one-, two- and three-family residences “not exceeding two habitable stories above no more than one uninhabitable story.” That last definition matters on this coast more than anywhere, because flood elevation and narrow lots push houses upward. A three-storey house on the water is outside a residential contractor’s licence, and the class should be read against the drawings rather than assumed.

Above a certain scale the state adds a further layer. Section 553.71(12) defines a threshold building as one greater than three stories or fifty feet in height, or with a large assembly occupancy. A threshold building requires a special inspector chosen and paid by the owner, and its permit may issue only to a licensed general contractor, or a building contractor within scope. It does not touch an ordinary house. It can touch a very tall one, or a substantial guest building, and the line is worth knowing before the massing is fixed.

People who work on the house need not all hold a licence themselves. Section 489.117(4)(d) allows an unlicensed person to perform construction and improvement work on single-family homes and townhouses under the supervision of a certified or registered general, building or residential contractor, without a direct contract between them. The licensed contractor is the one answerable, which is the arrangement the statute intends.

The owner-builder permit is the other route, and it is frequently misrepresented. Section 489.103(7) lets an owner act as their own contractor, on conditions. The owner must appear in person and sign the application, must provide direct, onsite supervision of all the work personally, and may not delegate that to anyone who is not licensed. Any unlicensed worker must be the owner’s own employee, with payroll withholding and workers’ compensation. Selling or leasing within a year raises a presumption that the exemption was abused.

The statutory disclosure an owner signs says in terms that unlicensed people use these permits to conceal their involvement. An owner who is invited to pull an owner-builder permit so that someone else can run the job is being asked to carry that person’s liability. We do not work under a client’s owner-builder permit, for exactly that reason.

Checks that take a minute each

  • Look up the licence on the state register, by number
  • Read the class against the storey count of the house
  • Confirm the licence is current and active
  • Confirm the name on the contract is the name on the licence
  • Ask who the permit applicant will be
  • Decline any request to pull an owner-builder permit for others
  • Ask whether the building could meet the threshold definition
  • Ask what the one-year statutory warranty will look like in writing

Before you appoint anyone

Send the parcel first

The most valuable hour in a custom house is spent before there is a design team to spend it with.

Look back over this page and count how many of the decisions were made before the design team existed. The parcel, the flood position, the wind regime, the town and its calendar, the envelope, the seawall. By the time an architect is appointed these are facts, and the architect’s first task is to find out what they are, at your expense and on your programme. The cheaper course is to arrive knowing.

So we read parcels, at no charge, before you are committed to them. An address is enough. You get back the zoning district and what its code section allows, the flood zone and the elevation that is likely to govern, the wind regime and which approval standard applies, any overlay or historic designation, whether a review board sits, the town’s construction season and what it prohibits, the permit history of whatever stands there, and on the water the seawall’s position against the current standard. You also get the list of documents worth pulling and who holds each of them.

It is advice about land and paper, given before any construction exists, and it is useful at exactly the moment this page is written for: after the idea, before the architect. If the answer is that the house you have in mind does not fit the lot, you hear that while it is still someone else’s lot. If it does fit, your designer starts with the constraints in hand rather than discovering them.

Separately, we perform directly the trades that section 489.117(4)(a) of the Florida Statutes places outside state contractor licensure, among them painting, flooring, cabinetry, decorative stone and tile, plastering, stuccoing, caulking and ornamental iron. The statute also provides that a local government may not require a state or local licence to obtain a permit for those scopes. Each trade has its own page, and each says where it stops.

Questions

What owners ask

How do I build a custom home in South Florida?

In an order set by information rather than by habit. Read the parcel first: zoning envelope, flood elevation, wind regime, overlays and the town’s construction season. Then commission the surveys and a geotechnical report, appoint the designer with those constraints in hand, bring a licensed builder in while the drawings can still change, complete the engineering and product approvals, and apply for the permit. Before work starts the owner records a notice of commencement. Construction then runs through foundation, shell, dry-in, rough-ins and finishes, each inspected, to a certificate of occupancy. The decisions with the largest consequences, including the parcel and the floor elevation, are made earliest and with the least information, which is why the reading comes first.

Do I need an architect to build a house in Florida?

Not by law. Section 481.229(1)(b) of the Florida Statutes exempts one-family and two-family residence buildings, townhouses and appurtenant domestic outbuildings from the architect’s seal, regardless of cost. What a building official still requires is a complete document set, sealed structural engineering, product approvals for every opening, energy calculations and, in a flood zone, flood design documentation. The exemption removes a legal requirement and none of the technical work. On a significant house an architect is usually still the right appointment, for design and coordination rather than for permission, and the one to choose is the one who has taken houses through permit on this coast.

How long does a building permit take for a custom home in Florida?

Section 553.792 gives a local government thirty business days to decide a residential permit for a structure under 7,500 square feet and sixty business days at or above that size. It has five business days to identify what is missing, or the application is deemed complete. If the deadline is missed the permit fee is reduced by ten per cent per business day, rising to twenty after a resubmittal; the permit is not deemed approved. Those clocks do not cover architectural review boards, variances, association approval or seawall and dock permits, which commonly take longer than the building review itself.

What does it cost to build a custom home in South Florida?

Published 2026 figures from builders active in the market put high-end production custom work on the mainland at roughly $450 to $650 per square foot, bespoke custom at $650 to $900, direct waterfront at $900 to $1,300 and oceanfront or overlay-constrained sites from $1,300 upward. Palm Beach County commonly runs ten to twenty-five per cent above comparable Miami-Dade waterfront, and building inside the High Velocity Hurricane Zone adds roughly eight to twelve per cent. These are market bands for hard construction cost, not quotations from us, and they exclude land, demolition, design fees, permits, landscape, pool, dock, seawall and furniture.

Is building in Naples the same as building in Miami or Fort Lauderdale?

No. Miami-Dade and Broward are the High Velocity Hurricane Zone, where openings are approved under the TAS 201, 202 and 203 protocols. Naples is in Collier County on the Gulf, outside that zone, as is Palm Beach County, and openings there qualify under ASTM E1886 and E1996. A Miami-Dade approved product satisfies the statewide standard, but a product approved only to the statewide standard may not be used in the zone. Flood exposure, design wind speeds and municipal rules also differ, and each is read from the local sources for the parcel rather than carried over from another county.

Can I act as my own contractor and build my own house in Florida?

Section 489.103(7) allows it, on conditions. The owner must appear in person and sign the permit application, must personally provide direct, onsite supervision of all the work, and may not delegate that responsibility except to a licensed person. Unlicensed workers must be the owner’s own employees with withholding and workers’ compensation, and selling or leasing within one year raises a presumption of violation. The statutory disclosure itself warns that unlicensed people use owner-builder permits to conceal their involvement.

What kind of contractor licence is needed to build a custom home?

A certified or registered general, building or residential contractor, with the class matched to the house. A general contractor’s scope is unlimited. A building contractor is limited to buildings not exceeding three stories. A residential contractor is limited to one-, two- and three-family residences not exceeding two habitable stories above no more than one uninhabitable story, which excludes some taller waterfront houses. Every licence can be checked on the Department of Business and Professional Regulation’s public register by number.

Does Amalfi build custom homes?

Not today. A new house here is built by a certified or registered general, building or residential contractor engaged by the owner. What Amalfi does today is read a parcel and its documents before you buy or before you appoint a design team, at no charge, and perform directly the trades that section 489.117(4)(a) places outside state contractor licensure, including painting, flooring, cabinetry, decorative stone and tile, plastering, stuccoing and caulking.
