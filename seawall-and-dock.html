Building here · The water's edge

What you inherit at the water's edge

Buyers inspect the house and look at the dock. Very few have the seawall assessed, and of everything on a waterfront parcel it is the item most capable of producing a seven-figure surprise. It is also the one that municipalities have begun legislating about, which means the wall you inherit may not be the wall you are allowed to keep.

The wall

What a seawall is, and how it fails

Most are reinforced concrete panels with a cap, tied back into the land. They have a finite life, they fail from the inside, and the symptoms arrive late.

The structure

Vertical panels driven or set into the bed, a concrete cap along the top, and tie-rods running back to anchors or a deadman buried in the soil behind. The visible wall is perhaps a third of the system. The part that fails first is usually the part nobody can see.

Chloride and the steel

The same mechanism that destroys balconies. Salt reaches the reinforcement inside the concrete, the steel corrodes, rust occupies several times the volume of the metal, and the concrete cracks and spalls from within. Rust staining and spalling along a cap is not cosmetic; it is the structure reporting on itself.

The tie-backs

Steel rods in wet, salty soil. When they go, the wall begins to lean outward and there is very little warning. A wall that is visibly out of plumb is in an advanced condition, not an early one.

Soil loss behind

Look at the ground on the land side. Depressions, sinking pavers, a terrace that has settled in a line parallel to the water or a pool deck cracking along the same line all suggest material is washing out through the wall or under it. That is a failing wall announcing itself from the garden.

Weep holes and drainage

A wall holds back water as well as soil, and pressure behind it has to relieve somewhere. Blocked or absent weeps raise hydrostatic pressure and accelerate everything else. It is a cheap thing to check and a cheap thing to fix.

Service life

Commonly quoted at forty to fifty years for a well-built wall in these conditions, and considerably less where construction or maintenance was poor. The useful question about any wall is not whether it will need replacing but in which decade, because that answer belongs in the purchase price.

The new part

Minimum elevations, and redevelopment triggers

This is recent, it is spreading, and it changes the arithmetic on an older wall substantially.

Municipalities along this coast have begun adopting minimum seawall elevations, driven by tidal flooding that now arrives on calendar dates rather than during storms. The pattern is consistent: a required elevation, a deadline for walls that fall below it, and a trigger that brings the requirement forward if the property is substantially redeveloped.

That last part is the one that matters to anyone buying a teardown. A wall that is merely non-compliant may have years to run under a long deadline. The same wall on a property about to be redeveloped can become an immediate obligation, and it lands at the beginning of the project rather than the end — because you cannot sensibly build a house behind a wall you are about to replace.

The numbers vary by municipality and they change, so the only reliable source is the city that will issue the permit. The three questions are always the same: what is the required minimum elevation here, what is the deadline for existing walls, and does redevelopment accelerate it.

The cost of raising or replacing a wall is driven by length, access and whether the existing wall can be capped and faced or has to come out. On a long waterfront with poor access it is readily a seven-figure item, and it is almost never in a buyer's model.

Broward County publishes guidance for property owners and developers on seawall requirements, and the county's resilience planning sits behind much of the municipal activity. Read it alongside whatever the city tells you, because the county framing explains why the municipal rules are moving.

Before you offer

  • What is the required minimum seawall elevation in this municipality
  • What is the current elevation of this wall
  • What is the compliance deadline, and does redevelopment accelerate it
  • Has the wall been assessed, and by whom, and when
  • Is there any permit history for repair or replacement
  • What is the condition of the cap, the panels and the tie-backs
  • Is there evidence of soil loss behind it

The dock

Not automatically replaceable

An existing dock proves a dock was once permitted. It does not prove the same dock can be rebuilt today, and the gap between those two is where projects stall.

Dock work sits under several authorities at once, and they do not run on the same timetable. The municipality has a say. The county has a say. The state, through the Department of Environmental Protection and in many cases a submerged lands authorisation, has a say. And where the work affects navigable waters, the Army Corps of Engineers has a say.

Each has its own criteria. Between them they look at the extent of the structure, its position relative to riparian lines, the effect on navigation and on the neighbours' access, and the resources on the bottom. Seagrass is the one that most often changes a design: where it is present, a survey is usually required and the structure may have to be reconfigured, elevated or reduced.

Riparian rights are the other common surprise. Your right to the water extends along lines projected from your property, and on a curved or irregular shoreline those lines are not simply perpendicular to the bank. A dock that has sat comfortably for forty years may not sit within the lines as they would be drawn today, and the neighbour who has never minded may be replaced by one who does.

Grandfathering is inconsistent and should never be assumed. Repairs within a certain scope are generally straightforward; replacement is a new permit against current rules. Where an existing structure exceeds what would be approved now, the practical answer is often to maintain it carefully for as long as possible, because the replacement will be smaller.

Boat lifts, pilings, davits and mooring hardware each have their own requirements, and in several towns the dock has to be designed for the vessel rather than the vessel chosen to fit the dock. If a particular boat is part of the plan, say so at the start.

What to establish

  • The permit history for the existing dock, from every authority
  • Whether the structure as built matches what was permitted
  • Where the riparian lines actually fall
  • Whether a seagrass survey will be required
  • Water depth at mean low water, for the vessel you intend
  • Whether a submerged lands lease exists and is current
  • What the municipality's current dock standards would allow

Keeping it

What maintenance actually does here

Neither of these is a maintenance-free object, and both respond well to attention that almost nobody gives them.

On the wall, the work is unglamorous and cheap relative to replacement. Keep the cap joints sealed so water is not running down behind the panels. Keep the weeps clear so pressure relieves. Watch the land side for settlement, which is the earliest honest symptom. Address cracking and spalling while it is at the surface, because once chloride has reached the reinforcement in quantity the remedy stops being a repair.

Rust staining on a cap is the signal to take seriously. It means steel inside the concrete is corroding, and the same mechanism that splits a wall splits a balcony and a lintel — we have written about how that works and how to read it in the context of a building, and the physics is identical at the water.

On the dock, the enemies are chloride and marine growth. Stainless hardware of the wrong grade will bleed rust within a season here: grade 316 is the marine specification and grade 304, which looks identical on the shelf and costs less, will not survive. Through-bolts and fasteners are the first failures. Timber decking and piling want inspection below the waterline as well as above it, because the zone that fails is the one nobody looks at.

Both items belong on a schedule rather than in a crisis. On properties we keep, the wall and the dock are walked and photographed with everything else, so that a change is noticed as a change rather than discovered as a problem — and so that when a municipality does set a deadline, the owner already knows where they stand.

On a cycle

  • Cap joints sealed, weeps kept clear
  • The land side watched for settlement and soil loss
  • Cracking and spalling addressed at the surface
  • Rust staining treated as a structural signal, not a stain
  • Dock hardware in grade 316, never 304
  • Fasteners and through-bolts checked and replaced in kind
  • Piling and substructure inspected below the waterline
  • Photographic record, so change is visible over years

Questions

What owners ask

How long does a seawall last?

Commonly quoted at forty to fifty years for a well-built wall in these conditions, and materially less where construction or maintenance was poor. The useful question is not whether it will need replacing but in which decade, because that belongs in the purchase price. A wall built in the 1970s on a property you intend to hold for twenty years is a budget line, not a maybe.

How do I know if a seawall is failing?

Look at the land side first. Depressions, sinking pavers, a terrace or pool deck settling in a line parallel to the water all suggest soil is escaping through or under the wall. On the wall itself, rust staining and spalling along the cap mean the reinforcement inside is corroding, and any visible lean means the tie-backs are compromised, which is an advanced condition rather than an early one. Blocked weep holes accelerate everything. Have it assessed by someone qualified before you offer, not after.

Will I be required to raise the seawall?

Possibly, and increasingly. Municipalities along this coast have begun adopting minimum seawall elevations with deadlines for existing walls, and many of them accelerate the requirement where a property is substantially redeveloped. That means a teardown can convert a distant obligation into an immediate one, landing at the start of the project rather than the end. The requirement, the deadline and the redevelopment trigger all vary by municipality, so ask the city that will issue your permit.

Can I rebuild the existing dock the same size?

Not automatically. An existing dock is evidence that a dock was once permitted, not that the same structure meets current standards. Replacement is a new permit assessed against today's rules by the municipality, the county, the state and in some cases the Army Corps of Engineers. Where seagrass is present a survey is usually required and the structure may have to be reconfigured or elevated. Where the existing dock exceeds what would be approved now, careful maintenance is often worth more than replacement.

What are riparian rights lines?

The projected lines that define how far your water access extends either side of your property. On a straight shoreline they are intuitive; on a curved or irregular one they are not, and they are not simply perpendicular to the bank. A dock that has sat in place for decades may not fall within the lines as they would be drawn today. It rarely matters until a neighbour changes, and then it matters a great deal.

Does the seawall affect my insurance or financing?

It can affect both. Lenders on waterfront property sometimes require evidence of seawall condition, and a wall in poor condition can complicate or delay financing. On the insurance side the wall itself is often excluded from standard property cover, which surprises owners who assume otherwise. Establish what is covered and what is not before closing rather than after a storm.

What stainless should dock hardware be?

Grade 316, which contains molybdenum and resists chloride. Grade 304 looks identical, costs less and will bleed rust within a season in this environment. It is one of the most common and most avoidable specification failures on the water, and it applies to every fastener, bracket, cleat and bolt. We have written separately on what chloride does to stainless and how it is remediated.

Can you assess a seawall for me?

We will look at it and tell you honestly what we can see — cap condition, spalling and rust staining, evidence of settlement on the land side, weeps, visible lean, and whether the dock structure matches what appears in the permit record. For a wall that matters we would then want a marine or structural engineer, and we will say so rather than offering an opinion beyond what a condition walk can support. The parcel review is free and includes this.
