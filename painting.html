Painting and coatings

A paint failure on the water is almost never a paint failure. It is a substrate that was never prepared, a coating chosen for a climate that does not exist here, or a film laid over a wall that needed to breathe. We specify the system before we quote the work, prepare to a written level of finish, and put the next repaint on the calendar the day this one is signed off.

The climate

Four hundred feet from the ocean, paint is a consumable

Inland, a good exterior holds eight to ten years. On an oceanfront or Intracoastal elevation the same product on the same substrate is tired in three to five, and the south and west faces go first. Nothing is wrong with the paint. It is simply being asked to do a different job.

Salt

Airborne chloride settles on every elevation within a few hundred metres of open water and is carried further on an onshore blow. On the surface it draws moisture out of the air and holds it against the film. Behind the film it reaches embedded steel and begins the corrosion that eventually pushes the coating off from inside. Rinsing elevations with fresh water is not housekeeping; it is the single cheapest thing that extends a coating's life.

Ultraviolet

We are at twenty-six degrees north with very little cloud cover for much of the year. UV breaks down the resin binder that holds pigment to the wall, which is why a south elevation chalks, fades and loses sheen years before a north elevation that was painted the same week. Any colour scheme that has to match across four faces has to account for it, and any warranty that does not mention exposure is not describing your house.

Moisture drive

Masonry here is wet more often than it is dry, and the vapour moves outward through the wall looking for somewhere to go. A coating that will not let it pass does not stop the water; it collects it at the back of the film. Blisters, sheet peeling and the pale efflorescence that pushes through fresh paint are all the same mechanism — a wall trying to dry through something that will not let it.

Thermal movement

Stucco, cast stone, timber and metal all expand at different rates, and they are joined to each other at exactly the places a coating has to span. Hairline cracking at those junctions is normal and a good elastomeric or high-build acrylic will bridge it. Rigid coatings and hard patches will not, and they fail along the joint in a line you can read from the drive.

Biological growth

Warmth, humidity and shade give algae and mildew everything they need. The dark streaking on a north elevation or under a soffit is growth, not dirt, and painting over it buys a season. It is killed and removed before coating, and the coating is specified with a mildewcide package, or you are repainting the same wall next year.

Wind-driven rain

A squall drives water horizontally into elevations that would never get wet in a vertical rain, and it finds every unsealed joint, head flashing and window perimeter. Most of what gets blamed on paint on a coastal house is water entering somewhere above the failure and coming out through the weakest part of the film. The sealant schedule matters more than the paint schedule.

The substrate

What the wall is, before what goes on it

Almost every argument about a finished paint job is actually an argument about the surface underneath it. On interiors that surface has a published standard with six levels, and which one you were given was decided long before the painter arrived.

Levels of gypsum board finish, as defined in Gypsum Association GA-214.
LevelWhat it isWhere it belongs
Level 0Board hung. No tape, no compound.Temporary construction.
Level 1Tape set in compound at joints. Tool marks acceptable.Concealed plenum space above ceilings.
Level 2Tape plus one coat over joints and fasteners.Substrate for tile. Garages, back of house.
Level 3Tape plus two coats. No tool marks or ridges.Under heavy texture. Rarely appropriate in a house.
Level 4Tape plus three coats over joints, fasteners and beads, sanded. The field of the board is untouched paper.Flat paint, light colours, light texture. The default a house receives.
Level 5All of Level 4, plus a skim coat of compound or a high-build primer-surfacer across the entire surface, sanded and primed.Gloss, semi-gloss, eggshell and satin. Dark and saturated colours. Any wall under critical or raking light.

The distinction between Level 4 and Level 5 is not quality of workmanship. It is coverage. At Level 4 the joint compound exists only in strips over the joints, the fastener heads and the corner beads; everything between them is the paper facing of the board. Compound and paper absorb differently and reflect differently, so under directional light the joints read through the finished paint as faint bands. The trade calls it photographing, and it is the single most common complaint on a newly finished high-end interior.

A Level 5 skim coat removes the difference by making the whole wall one material. It is applied by trowel, or sprayed and immediately flattened, or achieved with a high-build primer-surfacer rolled behind the spray. It is then sanded fine, cleaned of dust properly rather than blown around the room, primed with a sealer that equalises suction, and inspected under a work light held nearly parallel to the wall with the room lights off. That last step is the one that separates a specified Level 5 from a claimed one, and it is the one most often skipped.

What decides whether you need it is almost always the sheen and the light. Flat paint in a soft colour on a wall with no raking light will hide a great deal. Eggshell begins to tell. Satin and gloss are merciless, and so is a dark saturated colour, and so is a long uninterrupted wall washed by a cove or a line of sconces. Those are the conditions under which a Level 5 is worth what it costs, and the conditions under which a Level 4 will disappoint you no matter whose paint goes on it.

Two things worth knowing before anyone quotes. Level 5 is specified, not assumed — if it was not written into the drywall scope, the house received Level 4, and the painter who arrives last inherits the blame for a finish that was decided months earlier. And Level 5 is a gypsum standard; it has nothing to say about plaster. On a lime-plaster wall in a 1920s house the equivalent conversation is a veneer skim or a lime finish, a different material with a different cure and different failure modes. We establish which of the two we are looking at before a figure is put on anything.

What we establish first

  • What the substrate actually is — board, veneer plaster, lime plaster, stucco over tile, cast stone, timber
  • Which level of finish the interior holds today, room by room
  • What is already on the wall, and whether the new system is compatible with it
  • Where the light falls, and at what angle, in the rooms that matter
  • Moisture readings on any elevation or wall with a history
  • Whether the building predates 1978, which changes how the work is run
  • The sheen and colour you want, because that decides the preparation, not the other way round

The specification

You should be given a system, not a colour

A painting quote on one page with a price and a brand name is not a specification. It gives you nothing to hold anyone to, and it is the reason most repaint disputes cannot be settled. What follows is what we write before we price, and what you keep afterwards.

What a specification states

  • Each substrate by elevation and by room, and its condition at survey
  • The level of finish being delivered, named, with any remedial work to reach it
  • Preparation by surface: what is washed, what is scraped, what is sanded, what is cut out and patched, and with what
  • The primer, by product, and why that primer on that substrate
  • The topcoat, by product, sheen and number of coats, with the manufacturer's spread rate
  • Wet and dry film thickness where the product specifies it
  • The sealant schedule — every joint, perimeter and penetration, and the sealant type for each
  • What is masked, what is removed, what is protected and by whom
  • Colour, by code, with a drawdown approved on site in daylight
  • Conditions: the temperature, humidity and surface-moisture limits work stops at

What you receive at completion

  • Photographs of each elevation and each room before, during preparation and after
  • The repair record — every crack cut out, every section of timber replaced, photographed open before it was closed
  • Batch numbers and the product data sheets for everything applied
  • A colour schedule by room and elevation you can hand to anyone, including the next contractor
  • Any leftover material, labelled by room, stored where it will not freeze or cook
  • The manufacturer's warranty documentation, and what it does and does not cover
  • The date the next inspection falls due, and the expected repaint window by elevation

The last line is the one that matters most and the one nobody provides. A coating has a service life that varies by exposure, and on a waterfront house the south and west elevations will reach the end of theirs years before the north. A schedule that says so turns an unpleasant surprise into a line in next year's budget, and it lets a single elevation be recoated on its own while the rest of the house is still sound.

The standard

How the work runs

The order below is deliberate. Most of the ways a paint job fails are decided in the first three steps, before a brush is opened.

  1. SurveyEvery elevation and every room walked, photographed and recorded. Substrates identified. Moisture read where there is any history of water. Existing coatings tested for adhesion and, where it matters, for what they are — an alkyd under an acrylic, or a film-forming sealer on masonry, changes everything that follows. Cracks mapped and classified: movement, shrinkage, or something structural that belongs to an engineer rather than a painter.
  2. Specification and colourThe system is written and priced against the survey. Colours are drawn down on the actual substrate, in place, and looked at in daylight and after dark, because a colour chosen from a fan deck indoors is a different colour on a sunlit stucco wall. Nothing is ordered until the drawdowns are approved.
  3. ProtectionBefore anything is disturbed. Floors, stone, joinery, hardware, light fittings, planting and water features. Art and objects are the owner's call and the owner's hands or their art handler's — we will work around a covered wall rather than take anything off it. Paths through the house are agreed and kept.
  4. CleaningExteriors are soft washed at pressures appropriate to the substrate, with a biocide given its dwell time to kill growth rather than simply knock it off. On historic fabric this is done at preservation pressures, which means lower pressure, more water and more labour. The wall is then given time to dry to the coating's moisture limit, which in a humid week is longer than anyone wants it to be.
  5. Preparation and repairFailed coating removed back to a sound edge. Cracks cut out and filled with a material compatible with the wall rather than whatever is in the van. Rotten timber cut back to sound wood and replaced in kind. Ferrous metal taken back, treated and primed. Chalked surfaces washed and bound. Every repair photographed open, because once it is painted nobody can see what was done.
  6. SealantsOld sealant cut out rather than painted over. Joints sized, backer rod set to depth, and the joint tooled. Window and door perimeters, control joints, copings, penetrations and dissimilar-material junctions each get the sealant appropriate to them. This is the step that keeps the water out, and it is the step most commonly skipped on a competitive price.
  7. PrimingThe primer is chosen for the substrate and for what is already on it — bare stucco, chalked acrylic, bare timber, ferrous metal and a Level 5 skim each want a different one. Spot priming of repairs, then full prime where the specification calls for it. Suction is equalised before the first topcoat, or the finish will flash.
  8. TopcoatsApplied by brush, roller or spray as the surface and the product dictate, to the film thickness the manufacturer specifies and not merely until it looks covered. Recoat intervals observed. Work stops when the surface temperature, the humidity or the dew point moves outside the product's limits, which on an August afternoon is a real constraint and not an excuse.
  9. InspectionInteriors under raking light with the room lights off. Exteriors in the morning and again in the late afternoon, because the sun finds things at four o'clock that were invisible at ten. Defects marked, corrected and re-inspected rather than noted and left.
  10. HandoverThe house returned to the state it was in, the record handed over, the leftover material labelled and stored, and the next inspection and expected repaint window entered on the calendar before anyone leaves.

The house you live in

Most of this work happens around a household

An empty jobsite and an occupied house are different disciplines. The painting is the same; everything around it is not, and it is the part that decides whether you would have us back.

Containment

Work areas are separated from living areas, not merely swept at the end of the day. Dust from sanding travels further than anyone expects and settles on things that are difficult to clean. Where a room has to be isolated it is isolated properly, with the air moving in the right direction, and the barrier goes up before the first scraper comes out.

Odour

Product selection is part of the brief when people are sleeping in the house. Low- and zero-VOC systems have become good enough that there is rarely a reason to put a household through solvent, and where an alkyd is the right answer for a particular surface we say so in advance and we schedule around it rather than announcing it on the morning.

Sequence

Rooms are taken in an order you agree, so that the house always has the parts of it you need. Primary suites, kitchens and the rooms a household actually uses are planned around rather than through, and nothing is opened up that cannot be closed again the same day.

Access and hours

Several of the towns we work in regulate construction hours tightly and some restrict them further in season. Arrival and departure times, vehicle parking and staging are agreed with you and with the municipality before the first day, not negotiated with a code officer afterwards. Those rules are on the location pages for the towns that publish them.

The same people

The crew that starts your house finishes it. You are told who will be there and when, vehicles are identified, and nobody arrives unannounced. On a house with staff, the house manager has a point of contact who answers rather than a dispatch number.

Contents

Art, objects and anything of value stay in your hands or your handler's. We protect in place, work around a covered wall, and ask rather than assume. It is a short conversation at the start and it removes the only question that ever matters afterwards.

On a cycle

The repaint that never has to be a project

A whole-house exterior repaint is disruptive, expensive and avoidable at that scale. Broken into elevations and touch-ups against a written schedule, the same work becomes maintenance, and the house is never in the state that forces it.

A coating fails progressively and unevenly, and it does it in a sequence that is predictable enough to plan around. The south and west elevations chalk and fade first. The seaward face loses adhesion where salt has been sitting on it. Sealant joints go brittle and open before anything visible happens to the paint. Shaded elevations grow before they fade. By the time a house reads as needing painting, three of those things have been running for several years and the fourth is the reason a section of stucco now needs repair before anything can be coated.

Houses on the estate calendar are walked and photographed monthly, which means the sequence is caught at the start of it rather than at the end. Sealant is renewed on its own cycle before it fails. Elevations are rinsed of salt after an onshore blow. A single face is recoated in its own year, matched to a colour schedule we already hold, while the other three are still sound. Touch-ups are done as part of a visit rather than mobilised for.

The result is that the twelve-year event never arrives. What arrives instead is a line in a budget, a few days of work on one elevation, and a house that looks the same in year ten as it did in year two. It is a less dramatic way to own a building and a considerably cheaper one.

Where you already hold a colour schedule and a product record from us, that record travels. It is the difference between matching a colour and guessing at one, and it is the reason a touch-up on a four-year-old wall can be invisible rather than a patch that announces itself at the wrong time of day.

What the programme covers

  • Monthly condition walk of every elevation, photographed from fixed positions
  • Fresh-water rinsing of elevations and metalwork after an onshore blow
  • Sealant and expansion joints inspected and renewed on their own cycle
  • Interior and exterior touch-ups carried out within a scheduled visit
  • Soft washing of elevations before growth takes hold
  • Timber, ironwork and exterior joinery kept rather than restored
  • Colour schedule, product record and batch numbers held and kept current
  • Elevation-by-elevation repaint windows forecast and budgeted in advance

Before 1978

The build date changes how the work is run

It is the first thing we establish at survey, and on a great many of the houses in the towns we serve the answer puts the job under a federal rule most people have never heard of.

Lead was in household paint in the United States until it was banned for residential use in 1978. Any house built before then is presumed to contain it somewhere under the layers unless it has been tested, and in the enclaves along this coast the housing stock is overwhelmingly older than that — the Mediterranean Revival estates of the twenties and thirties, the post-war houses, almost everything a preservation board has an opinion about.

Painting is the activity the federal Renovation, Repair and Painting rule was written for. Scraping, sanding, burning off and window work all release lead-bearing dust, and the rule governs how any compensated work that disturbs painted surfaces on a pre-1978 dwelling has to be carried out: a certified renovator assigned to the job, the work area contained, prohibited practices avoided, cleaning verified rather than assumed, and the Renovate Right pamphlet given to the owner with a signed acknowledgement before anything starts. Florida is not an authorised state, so the Environmental Protection Agency administers this directly.

The exemption is narrower than people hope. Minor repair and maintenance is capped at six square feet of interior surface or twenty square feet of exterior, and window replacement is excluded from it entirely. An exterior repaint passes twenty square feet before the first wall is finished. In practice, if the house is older than 1978 and paint is being disturbed, the rule applies.

We set this out here because it is the question to put to anyone quoting work on an older house, and most owners do not know to ask it. A contractor who has not raised the build date before pricing has either not looked or is not planning to comply, and the dust ends up in the soil, the terrace and the house. We have written the longer version of this for owners, including how to ask and what to ask for.

What the rule requires

  • The firm carrying out the work certified by the EPA
  • A certified renovator assigned to each job and on site as required
  • Renovate Right delivered and acknowledged in writing before work starts
  • Work areas contained and signed; the ground and planting covered
  • Open-flame burning, torching and high-heat guns prohibited
  • Machine sanding and grinding only with HEPA extraction attached
  • HEPA vacuuming and wet cleaning, with cleaning verified at the end
  • Records of all of it kept for three years

The line

Where painting ends

Painting and stuccoing are both named in Fla. Stat. 489.117(4)(a) as job scopes exempt from contractor licensing, and neither has been locally licensed since the state preempted those categories. Several of the things that routinely get bundled into a painting quote are still licensed work, and we draw the line in writing rather than discovering it halfway up a scaffold.

Ours

  • Interior and exterior painting, all substrates
  • Surface preparation, washing, scraping, sanding and chalk removal
  • Crack cutting-out, patching and filling to match the surrounding wall
  • Caulking, sealants, backer rod and joint renewal
  • Waterproof and elastomeric coatings
  • Priming systems and specialist primers
  • Ferrous metal preparation and coating — railings, gates, brackets, shutters
  • Exterior joinery and trim finishing
  • Stucco repair, patching and re-rendering, matched to the existing wall
  • Mineral, silicate and lime-based coatings on masonry
  • Specification, colour schedules, drawdowns and the record

Not ours, and why

  • Structural timber replacement beyond trim — that is carpentry under the contracting statute
  • Window and door replacement, and sash rebuilding
  • Any work requiring a building permit
  • Roofing, including anything coated that keeps water out of the building
  • Structural crack repair, which wants an engineer before it wants a trade
  • Mould remediation above ten square feet, a separately licensed trade in Florida

Where a job needs any of the second list, the licensed trade contracts directly with you, as on every project we run. Florida's contracting statute makes no distinction between performing licensed work yourself and having it performed by others under your contract, which is precisely why we do not take it into ours. You see each trade's licence and certificate of insurance before they arrive, and you are never paying one firm for another's work.

Questions

What owners ask

How often does a waterfront house actually need repainting?

It depends far more on the elevation than on the house. Inland, a quality acrylic system on sound stucco holds eight to ten years. On an oceanfront or Intracoastal exposure, the south and west faces are commonly tired at three to five while the north face is still sound at eight. That is why we forecast repaint windows by elevation rather than for the building, and why a house kept on a rinsing and sealant cycle sits at the long end of every one of those ranges rather than the short end.

Do you need a licence to paint in Florida?

No. Painting has never been a state-licensed trade under Florida's construction contracting statute, and local certificates of competency for it were eliminated by state preemption — Miami-Dade stopped issuing them in 2023 and Palm Beach County's painting certificate ended on 1 July 2025, with existing holders reclassified. Fla. Stat. 489.117(4)(a) now names painting, stuccoing, plastering, caulking and pressure washing among the exempt job scopes, and states that a local government may not require a licence to obtain a permit for them. What does still apply is a business tax receipt in each jurisdiction and current insurance, both of which the permitting portals verify, and some municipalities may still licence rooftop painting, coating and cleaning above three storeys. The licensing questions that matter on a painting job attach to the work around it, which is why there is a section above setting out exactly where our scope ends.

What is a Level 5 finish and do I need one?

It is the highest of the six gypsum board finish levels, and it means a skim coat of compound or high-build primer-surfacer over the entire wall rather than only over the joints and fasteners, so the surface is one continuous material. You need it if you are using eggshell, satin or gloss, if the colour is dark or saturated, or if any significant wall is lit at a grazing angle by a window, a cove or sconces. Under those conditions a Level 4 wall shows its joints through the paint as faint bands. If your rooms are flat paint in soft colours without directional light, Level 5 is money you will not see.

My paint is blistering and peeling in sheets. Is it bad paint?

Almost never. Sheet peeling on masonry is usually moisture leaving the wall and meeting a film that will not let it pass — commonly a film-forming sealer or an impermeable coating applied over stucco that needs to breathe. The water collects behind the film and pushes it off. The other common cause is painting over a surface that was not dry, or that was chalked and never bound. Both are preparation and specification failures rather than product failures, and repainting with the same system over the same wall will reproduce them. We test adhesion and identify what is on the wall before writing anything.

Can you match a colour nobody has a record of?

Usually, and better than a scanner will. A spectrophotometer reads a weathered and chalked surface and returns the colour of the weathering, not the colour the wall was painted. The reliable method is to find an unweathered sample — inside a shutter rebate, behind a downpipe, under a fitting — lightly expose it, read that, then draw down candidates on the actual wall and look at them in daylight and at dusk. Once we hold your colour schedule the question does not arise again.

Will you work while we are in the house?

Most of our work is done that way. It changes the product selection toward low- and zero-VOC systems, the sequencing so that the rooms you use are planned around, and the containment so that sanding dust does not travel. All of that is agreed before the first day rather than improvised. In several of the towns we serve the municipality also restricts working hours, and in season some of them restrict them considerably further, which is factored into the programme rather than discovered.

Our house was built in the 1920s. Does that change anything?

A great deal. Any house built before 1978 is presumed to contain lead paint, which puts the work under the federal Renovation, Repair and Painting rule and changes how containment, preparation and cleaning have to be carried out. Separately, a wall of lime stucco over hollow clay tile behaves nothing like modern stucco over block: it has to stay vapour-open, which rules out most film-forming products and points toward mineral, silicate or lime-based systems. Both questions are settled at survey, before a product is named.

Why does your quote take longer than everyone else's?

Because the survey comes first. Identifying substrates, testing existing coatings for adhesion and compatibility, taking moisture readings, classifying cracks and establishing the build date all happen before a figure exists, and the specification that results is the document you hold us to. A same-day price on a waterfront exterior is a guess with a number attached to it, and the difference between the guess and the real scope surfaces later, as a change order.
