Estates · Pool and water features

What a pool service may and may not legally do

Two things are true in Florida and almost nobody selling pool service says either of them out loud. There is no state licence, certification or registration to service a residential pool commercially. And the moment the work moves from the water to the equipment, a contractor’s licence is required, and the statute says so in terms. The line between those two positions is written into Chapter 489, and it is published here in full.

Ask about a pool programme The grounds standard

First, two facts

There is no Florida licence to service a residential pool

Both of these are verifiable in an afternoon, and both of them cost somebody a sale, which is presumably why they are not on anybody else’s website.

The first fact is an absence. There is no licence, certification or registration issued by the State of Florida that a person must hold in order to clean a private residential pool for money, test its water or add chemicals to it. Not in Chapter 489, which governs construction contracting. Not in the Construction Industry Licensing Board’s specialty rules at 61G4-15. Not at the Department of Health. The absence is deliberate, because the statute draws its line somewhere else entirely — at the equipment, not at the water — and the sections below set out exactly where.

The second fact is a misunderstanding that the trade has allowed to persist because it is commercially useful. Chapter 514 of the Florida Statutes and Rule 64E-9 of the Florida Administrative Code set out a substantial regulatory regime for swimming pools: operating permits, water quality standards, inspection, record-keeping, trained operators. That regime applies to public pools. A private residential pool at a single-family estate is not a public pool, so none of it reaches your pool, and a service company that implies its Chapter 514 compliance is a qualification for work at your house is describing a rule that does not apply to the job.

Which brings in the acronym. “CPO” stands for Certified Pool Operator, and it is a private industry certification issued by a trade body. It is a genuine course and a genuine examination, and holding it is a reasonable sign that somebody has studied water chemistry properly. It is not a Florida licence. It is not issued, examined, renewed or policed by the Department of Business and Professional Regulation or by any other state agency, and the state does not require it for residential work. When a company presents CPO as though it were state authorisation — “state certified”, “licensed and certified”, a seal next to a licence number that belongs to something else — that is marketing, and an owner is entitled to ask which agency issued it.

The part of pool work that genuinely does require a licence is the part almost every owner assumes is included: the pump, the heater, the filter vessel, the pipework, the equipment pad. That is where Chapter 489 bites, and it bites hard. The next section is the whole division, task by task.

What to ask any pool company

  • Which state agency issued the credential you are showing me?
  • What is the licence number, and under whose name is it held?
  • Is that licence a residential pool and spa servicing licence?
  • Who performs the equipment work, and under whose licence?
  • Do you hold the trade contract, or do I?
  • What is recorded at each visit, and where does the record live?
  • What are the readings you take, and against what target?
  • A Florida contractor’s licence can be checked against the Department of Business and Professional Regulation registry in about a minute

The division

Task by task, as Chapter 489 draws it

Sections 489.105(3)(j), (k) and (l) each carry the same two provisos, and those provisos do most of the work. The first: “Water treatment that does not require such equipment does not require a license.” The second: a licence is not required for the cleaning of the pool or spa in a way that does not affect the structural integrity of the pool, the spa or its associated equipment.

Two rows in that table are worth a second reading. The filter change is the one owners are told requires a licensed visit, and it does not: the statute names the partial-disassembly trigger and then expressly excepts filter changes from it. And the last row is the one that catches companies out in the other direction — a direct connection to sanitary sewer or to potable water is not pool work at all. It is plumbing under section 489.105(3)(m), and a pool licence does not reach it. A backwash line tied into the sewer, or a make-up water line tied into the house supply, belongs to a plumber.

The mechanism

Why the statute puts the line where it does

The drafting is better than it is usually given credit for. Read closely, the provisos track a real distinction between operating a system and altering one.

The word that governs the first proviso is equipment. The licensed categories are described by reference to installing, modifying, substantially disassembling or replacing permanently attached water-treatment and cleaning equipment — and then the statute adds that water treatment which does not require such equipment does not require a licence. Adding acid, chlorine, calcium chloride, sodium bicarbonate or salt to a body of water is water treatment that requires no equipment to be installed, modified or replaced. It therefore falls outside the licensed scope by the statute’s own test, not by anybody’s interpretation of it.

The word that governs the second proviso is structural. Cleaning is exempt where it does not affect the structural integrity of the pool, the spa or its associated equipment. Brushing plaster, netting leaves, vacuuming the floor, emptying a skimmer basket and wiping a tile line are cleaning operations that leave the shell, the bond beam and the equipment exactly as they were. The same is not true of acid-washing a plaster interior, chipping scale off a tile line with a tool that bites into the grout, or draining a shell to get at something — and the statute reaches each of those in turn.

Between the two provisos sits the partial-disassembly trigger, which is the most practically useful sentence in the section. Any cleaning or sanitising of equipment that requires at least partial disassembly is licensed work — opening a pump pot to its volute, breaking a union to clear an impeller, pulling a salt cell to acid-bath it, dismantling a heater to descale the exchanger. The statute then excepts filter changes. The exception is narrow and it is specific, and it means that replacing a cartridge element or changing filter media is unlicensed while almost everything else done with a spanner on that pad is not.

So the honest summary is this. Everything that happens in the water, and everything done with a brush, a net, a vacuum head or a test kit, requires no Florida licence of any kind. Everything that happens on the equipment pad, with the single exception of a filter change, requires one. And the connections at either end of the system — to the sewer and to the potable supply — are not pool work at all.

Where the line actually falls

  • In the water: no licence
  • On the brush, net, vacuum and basket: no licence
  • On the test kit and the dosing: no licence
  • On the filter element or media: no licence, by express exception
  • On the pump, heater, valve or cell: licensed
  • Inside any equipment that has to come apart: licensed
  • On the pipework, perimeter or filter: licensed
  • On the interior finish: licensed
  • Draining for repair or renovation: licensed
  • At the sewer or potable connection: plumbing, not pool

The bridge

Section 489.117(4)(e), named plainly

There is a lawful structure for equipment work that does not involve pretending to hold a licence, and it is set out in statute. It is worth understanding before anybody offers it to you.

Section 489.117(4)(e) of the Florida Statutes provides that an uncertified, unregistered person may perform pool, spa, hot tub and water-feature work without a local licence or specialty certification where a qualifying contractor supervises the work, the work falls within that contractor’s own licensed scope, and that contractor is responsible for it. The paragraph does not require a direct contract between the supervising contractor and the person performing the work, and it does not require employment either. Supervision and responsibility are the conditions; paperwork between the two parties is not.

That is a real provision doing real work, and it is the difference between a company that has to say “nobody may touch your pump” and one that can explain who is responsible when the pump is touched. Where equipment work is performed under this paragraph, the supervising pool contractor is a named party, their licence number is given to the owner in writing, and the responsibility for the work sits with them as a matter of statute rather than as a matter of goodwill. An owner should insist on the name and the number, and should check the number.

Two limits on it matter. The first is scope: the supervision can only reach work inside the supervising contractor’s own licensed scope, so a residential pool and spa servicing licence does not become a licence for something else because a second person is doing the work. The second is explicit in the paragraph — it does not extend to plumbing or electrical work. A sewer connection, a potable water connection, a bonding grid, a breaker, a conductor or a load-side connection to the equipment pad is not reached by it. Those belong to a licensed plumber or a licensed electrician and to nobody else.

In practice this is how equipment work on an estate pool gets done properly, and it is worth understanding as an arrangement rather than as a technicality. The qualifying pool contractor is identified before the work, their scope is stated, their responsibility is a matter of statute, and the hands on the pad are the same hands that read the water every week and know what the pump has been doing all season. The owner gets one record of the pool rather than two, and a licensed party standing behind the part of it that requires one.

How the structure is disclosed

  • The supervising contractor is named in writing before work starts
  • The licence number is given, and it can be verified with the Department of Business and Professional Regulation
  • The scope of the supervised work is stated, and it does not exceed that licence
  • Plumbing and electrical work are excluded from it, by the terms of the paragraph
  • The trade contract for licensed work stays in the owner’s name
  • Licensed work is passed through at the licensed trade’s own price, unmarked
  • The readings and the record behind the work are the owner’s, and travel with the property
  • Where a job needs a licence nobody on site holds, the right licensee is named and engaged

Waterfront chemistry

Saturation index management on a salt pool near the ocean

A salt pool on a waterfront lot is the hardest water chemistry on the property, and it is almost always managed with the wrong instrument — a chlorine reading and a pH strip, when the number that decides whether the pool eats itself is neither.

The index, not the readings

One calculated number saying whether the water is in balance with the calcium carbonate in the plaster, the grout and the stone. It is computed from temperature, pH, calcium hardness, total alkalinity, dissolved solids and the stabiliser in the water. Alone, none of those readings tells you anything about the finish.

Negative water dissolves

Below the balance point the water is undersaturated and goes looking for calcium — from the plaster first, etching and then roughening it, then the grout, then the coping. A pool can be perfectly chlorinated, perfectly clear and quietly consuming its own interior for a season.

Positive water deposits

Above the balance point the water gives calcium up where temperature and turbulence are highest: heater exchanger, cell plates, tile line, step edges, spa spillway. Scale on a cell cuts output, which reads as low chlorine, which is usually answered by raising output rather than by reading the index.

Why salt changes the arithmetic

A salt pool carries three to four thousand parts per million of dissolved solids where a chlorinated pool carries a few hundred, and dissolved solids are a term in the index. The cell also raises pH at the plate, which is why pH on a salt pool climbs continually rather than drifting.

Why the coast changes it again

Make-up water here is hard, and a well near the Intracoastal carries iron and can turn brackish as the salt front moves. Every inch of evaporation replaced raises hardness and dissolved solids and leaves the metals behind. Onshore wind adds chloride to water that already has salt in it by design.

Which is why it is recorded

The index is calculated and written down at every visit with its six inputs beside it, so the trend is visible before the consequence is. One reading says where the water is today. A season of readings says whether the plaster is dissolving and whether the make-up water has changed.

The targets belong to the pool, not to a chart: a polished-aggregate interior, a plaster interior, a glass tile line and a natural stone coping each tolerate a different band, and the finish manufacturer’s own data is the authority. What does not vary is the discipline — six readings, one calculated index, written down, with the make-up water tested at source each season. On a waterfront estate the pool and the irrigation supply are often drawing from the same well, so a change in the water is a change to two systems at once.

The visit

What happens, and in what order

The order matters, because several of these readings are worthless if taken after the pool has been dosed, and because the unlicensed column is only defensible if it is actually observed.

  1. Read before anything is addedFree and total chlorine, pH, total alkalinity, calcium hardness, cyanuric acid where present, salinity on a salt pool, water temperature. Taken at a consistent depth away from the returns, before any chemical goes in — a dose and a reading taken in the wrong order produce a record that cannot be trended.
  2. Calculate the index, then decideThe six inputs are combined and the dosing decision is made against the index rather than the chlorine number alone. A pool low on chlorine and scaling is a different problem from one low on chlorine and dissolving, and the two are corrected in opposite directions.
  3. Clean, then read the equipment without opening itBrushing, netting, vacuuming, baskets, lids, seals and water level. Then filter pressure against the clean baseline, suction and discharge at the gauges already fitted, heater status and fault history, cell output and reversal cycle, flow at the returns, and the automation schedule against the season — observation and photographs, no spanner on anything needing a licence.
  4. Dose, record the dose, state the boundaryChemicals added by quantity and product, written beside the readings that justified them. The record then names anything found that needs a licensed trade, which licence it needs and who holds it. Nothing licensed is quietly performed and nothing licensed is quietly priced, because the record is the deliverable and one that blurs the line is worth less than none.

Who does what

The division of work on your pool

Set out plainly, because this is the subject on which vagueness costs owners money. The weekly programme is our own work. The licensed column is your pool contractor’s, your plumber’s or your electrician’s, contracted in your name and passed through at their price.

Our own people, every visit

  • Water chemistry: six readings and the calculated saturation index
  • Dosing, by quantity and product, written down beside the readings
  • Brushing, netting and vacuuming
  • Skimmer and pump baskets, lids, seals and water level
  • Filter element and media changes, by the statute’s express exception
  • Tile line cleaning that does not affect structural integrity
  • Operation of treatment and cleaning equipment already installed
  • Equipment readings, photographs and fault logging
  • Make-up water tested at the source each season
  • The written visit record, with the next decision named in it

Held by the licensed trade, in your name

  • Repair or replacement of existing equipment — licensed pool contractor
  • Anything requiring partial disassembly, filter changes excepted
  • New equipment, interior refinishing, heater work
  • Perimeter and filter piping, equipment room repair
  • Substantial or complete draining for repair or renovation
  • Sewer and potable water connections — licensed plumber, 489.105(3)(m)
  • Bonding, breakers, conductors and pad electrics — licensed electrician
  • Equipment work under 489.117(4)(e) — the supervising contractor named in writing
  • Each licence number given to you, and verifiable with the Department of Business and Professional Regulation
  • Each licensed trade’s own price, passed through without a mark-up

There is a reason worth knowing for keeping the licensed trade contract in your own name, and it is in section 489.105(3): a contractor is defined as a person who for compensation undertakes the work “himself or herself or by others”. Subcontracting licensed work under somebody else’s contract is contracting licensed work, whoever holds the tools — which means a firm that signs your pool contractor’s agreement and bills you for it has taken on the statutory position of the contractor, and the licence had better be theirs. Contracted in your name, the licence, the insurance and the liability all sit with the party that actually holds them, and you can check every one of them in a minute against the state registry.

Questions

What owners ask

Does Florida require a licence to service a residential pool?

No. There is no state licence, certification or registration a person must hold to clean a private residential pool commercially, test its water or add chemicals to it. Chapter 489 licenses the equipment work, not the water. The regime most people have in mind — Chapter 514 and Rule 64E-9, with its permits, operator requirements and inspections — applies to public pools, and a private residential pool at a single-family estate is not a public pool.

Is CPO a Florida licence?

No. Certified Pool Operator is a private industry certification issued by a trade body. It is a real course and a real examination, and reasonable evidence that somebody has studied water chemistry. It is not issued, examined, renewed or enforced by any Florida agency, and the state does not require it for residential work. Where a company presents it as state authorisation, ask which agency issued it.

What pool work actually requires a licence?

Under 489.105(3)(l) and Rule 61G4-15.040: repair or replacement of existing equipment; cleaning or sanitising requiring at least partial disassembly, filter changes excepted; installing new equipment; interior refinishing; reinstalling or adding a heater; repairing or replacing perimeter and filter piping; equipment room repair; and substantial or complete draining for repair or renovation. Connections to sanitary sewer or potable water are not pool work at all — they are plumbing under 489.105(3)(m).

Can an unlicensed person change my pool filter?

Yes, and this one surprises owners. The statute makes cleaning or sanitising that requires at least partial disassembly into licensed work, then expressly excepts filter changes from that trigger. Replacing a cartridge element or changing media is unlicensed. Opening a pump pot, breaking a union, pulling a cell to acid-bath it or dismantling a heater is not.

Is draining my pool licensed work?

Substantial or complete draining for the purpose of repair or renovation is licensed under 489.105(3)(l). Lowering the level to the skimmer throat before a storm, or a partial drain-and-dilute on a pool whose dissolved solids have climbed, is not draining for repair or renovation. The distinction matters on a high water table: a shell relies on the water in it to resist uplift, and an empty shell on a waterfront lot in the wet season can move.

What is section 489.117(4)(e)?

It provides that an uncertified, unregistered person may perform pool, spa, hot tub and water-feature work without a local licence or specialty certification where a qualifying contractor supervises the work, it falls within that contractor’s licensed scope, and that contractor is responsible for it. No direct contract or employment between them is required. It does not extend to plumbing or electrical work, and the supervising contractor should be named and their licence number given in writing.

Why does the saturation index matter more than the chlorine reading?

Because chlorine tells you whether the water is sanitary and the index tells you whether the water is eating the pool. Below the balance point the water takes calcium out of the plaster, the grout and the coping. Above it, the water deposits calcium on the heater exchanger, the cell plates and the tile line. A pool can be perfectly chlorinated and perfectly clear in either condition.
