Historic · Roofs

The tile is not the part that wears out

A good clay barrel tile can last a century and frequently does. The felt underneath it cannot, and that is the part actually keeping water out of the house. On most historic Florida roofs the correct answer is to lift the original tile, renew what is beneath it, and put the same tile back — which is rarely the quote an owner is handed.

Two materials

A tile roof is a system with two very different service lives

One component is close to permanent. The other is a consumable, and it is hidden.

Clay barrel tile sheds the bulk of the water and takes the ultraviolet, the heat and the impact. Fired clay does that almost indefinitely; tile from the 1920s is routinely sound ninety or a hundred years later, and the colour variation that makes an old Palm Beach or Coral Gables roof look the way it does is a function of exactly that age.

Underneath it is the underlayment — historically felt, now a range of synthetic and self-adhered membranes — and that is the layer doing the waterproofing. Water gets under tile. It always has; the system is designed on the assumption that it will. The membrane is what carries it back out. Its practical life is in the range of twenty to thirty years in this climate, against a tile that will outlast several of them.

So a historic tile roof does not fail. Its underlayment does, repeatedly, across the life of the same tile. Understanding that one fact changes what an owner should be buying.

The default quote

Why you will be quoted a tear-off

Not usually because anybody is trying to mislead you. A tear-off and re-cover with new tile is faster, simpler to price per square, easier to schedule, and carries no breakage risk for the contractor. A relay is slow, awkward, needs somewhere to stack and protect several tons of fragile material, and exposes the roofer to a variable he cannot control — how much of your tile survives being handled.

It is also easier to warrant. A manufacturer will warrant new tile on new underlayment. Nobody warrants hundred-year-old clay. From a roofing contractor’s point of view the tear-off is the lower-risk sale, and the fact that it destroys something irreplaceable is not a line on the estimate.

The owner is then presented with a single number and no alternative, usually at the point when water has already appeared somewhere inside. Which is precisely the wrong moment to be learning the difference.

What is lost

What you do not get back

The tile itself

Old barrel tile was made from local clays in small batches and frequently by hand, and much of what sits on Palm Beach and Coral Gables roofs was imported or salvaged generations ago. The colour range, the surface variation and the slight irregularity of line are not available as a product. Modern replacement tile is dimensionally excellent and visibly uniform, and on a 1920s house it reads as new from the street.

The detailing

Mortar-bedded ridges and hips, mitred hip starters, bird stops, the way the courses were set out around a dormer or a chimney. These were site-made by people doing it daily and they are a large part of why the roof looks right. A modern mechanical system does the same job and does not look the same.

The approval

On a designated property, relaying salvaged original tile is frequently the only treatment a board will approve. New tile installation is generally reviewed at a higher tier than in-kind relay. A tear-off quote on a landmarked house may simply not be permittable, which owners discover after they have accepted it.

The method

How a relay is actually done

The sequence matters, and so does who is on the roof.

  • Catalogue first. Photograph the roof plane by plane, record the profile, the courses, the ridge and hip detailing and any pattern in the colour, before a single tile is moved. This is what the reinstatement is set out from.
  • Lift by hand, in order. Tiles are removed by hand rather than shovelled, kept in course order where the roof has a colour pattern, and stacked on edge on battens rather than flat in piles.
  • Stack and protect on site. Several tons of fragile material needs a place to live for the duration, out of traffic and not loaded on a terrace or a driveway that will not take it.
  • Allow for breakage honestly. A relay always loses tiles — to handling, to units that were already cracked under the overlap, to the mortar on the ridges. A credible contractor gives you a breakage allowance up front and a plan for making it up.
  • Make the loss up from salvage. Matching reclaimed tile exists and is traded, and the time to be acquiring it is before the roof comes off, not during. A house that has kept its own salvage stock from previous repairs is in a far better position.
  • Renew the underlayment properly. This is the one place to spend money without sentiment: the best membrane the budget allows, correctly lapped, with the valleys, flashings and penetrations detailed in metal that will survive the salt.
  • Relay to the record, rebed the ridges and hips, and reinstate the bird stops and the edge detail as they were.

The honest numbers

A relay is not the cheap option

It should be said plainly: relaying costs more in labour than tearing off, sometimes considerably more, and it takes longer. What it saves is the tile, and on a house where the tile is part of what makes the house significant — and part of what it is worth — that is not a sentimental calculation.

It is also not always the answer. A roof whose tile is genuinely spent, a profile that was a poor product when it was new, or a structure that cannot take the load any more are all real situations, and a good contractor will say so. The point is that the question should be asked and answered with evidence, rather than defaulted past.

What upkeep contributes

Making a relay possible years before you need one

Whether an owner has the option of relaying is largely decided long before the decision arrives. Three things make the difference, and all three are maintenance.

A counted inventory: slipped, cracked and missing tiles recorded and photographed each visit, so the rate of loss is known rather than guessed. A salvage stock held on the property: every tile taken off in a repair kept, labelled and stored dry, so there is matching material when it is needed. And a record of the detailing, photographed before anybody has ever worked on it, so the reinstatement has something to be set out from.

None of that is expensive. All of it is the difference between a relay and a tear-off when the membrane finally goes, and the membrane is going to go.

Questions

Quick answers

How long does a clay barrel tile roof last?

The tile itself can last a century or more, and frequently does. The underlayment beneath it, which is the layer actually keeping water out, has a service life closer to twenty to thirty years in the Florida climate. A historic tile roof therefore does not reach the end of its life as a whole; its underlayment reaches the end of its life repeatedly, under the same tile.

Should I replace or relay my historic tile roof?

On most historic houses, relay. Lifting and cataloguing the original tile, renewing the underlayment and relaying the same tile preserves material that cannot be bought again, and on a designated property it is often the only treatment a board will approve. Replacement makes sense where the tile is genuinely spent or the structure can no longer carry the load, and a good contractor will tell you which situation you are in.

Why do roofers quote a tear-off instead of a relay?

Because it is faster, simpler to price, easier to schedule, carries no breakage risk for the contractor, and can be warranted by a manufacturer. Nobody warrants hundred-year-old clay. It is usually a commercial preference rather than a technical judgement, and it is rarely presented alongside the alternative.

How many tiles break during a relay?

Some always do — to handling, to units that were already cracked under the overlap, and to the mortar on ridges and hips. A credible contractor gives a breakage allowance before starting and has a plan for making the loss up from matching reclaimed tile. A house that has kept its own salvage from earlier repairs is in a much stronger position.

Does relaying a roof need board approval on a landmarked house?

It depends on the municipality, but in-kind relay is generally treated far more leniently than new tile installation. In the Town of Palm Beach, for example, in-kind replacement of architectural features including S tile sits in the administrative approval column, while new S tile installation is reviewed at a higher tier. Where a specific job falls is a question for the Town.

What should be done between now and then?

Keep a counted inventory of slipped, cracked and missing tiles with photographs, keep every tile removed in a repair as labelled salvage stored dry on site, and photograph the ridge, hip and edge detailing before anybody works on it. Those three habits are what make a relay possible when the membrane eventually fails.
