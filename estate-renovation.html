Estates · Renovating what is standing

Renovating a waterfront estate, and the threshold that governs the budget

A whole-house renovation of a substantial older property in Fort Lauderdale, Boca Raton or Palm Beach is routinely harder, slower and less predictable than building the same house from nothing. That is not a sales position — it is the consistent experience of this coast, and the reason sits in a single arithmetical test buried in a flood ordinance. Owners who understand that test before they commission drawings phase their project differently, and spend materially less for the same result.

The premise

Why the older house is the harder project

On a new build the unknowns are in front of you and priced. On a renovation they are behind a wall, and they arrive one at a time, after the contract is signed.

A new house on a cleared lot is a known quantity in the only sense that matters to a budget: everything in it is being drawn, engineered, specified and priced before anybody commits. The site has been surveyed, the soil has been bored, the flood elevation is fixed, the structure is designed to current code and every opening carries a product approval. The programme is long and the number is large, but the number is largely real.

An existing substantial house is the opposite. Its structure was designed to a code edition that no longer exists, its openings almost certainly do not carry current product approval, its electrical and mechanical systems were sized for a different way of living, and its finished floor may sit below the elevation the current flood map requires. None of that is visible from the drive. It emerges in sequence, as finishes come off, and each discovery arrives at the moment it is most expensive to deal with, because the trades behind it are already scheduled.

Then there is the regulatory asymmetry, which is the part owners find genuinely counter-intuitive. A new house is permitted as a new house and is expected to meet current code throughout. A renovation is permitted as an alteration, and the code treats alterations generously — right up to a threshold, at which point the entire building is treated as new. That threshold is a percentage of the building’s market value, it is measured cumulatively over a rolling period, and crossing it can add a structural elevation exercise to what began as a kitchen and two bathrooms.

Where the money actually goes on a project of this kind is not where owners expect. It goes into openings, because every window and door in a high-velocity hurricane zone retrofit carries a design pressure, an approval and two inspections. It goes into systems, because an older house rarely has the electrical capacity or the mechanical distribution a current specification assumes. It goes into the substrate behind the finish, which on a coastal house of forty years is frequently the real condition of the project. And it goes into the time cost of discovery — the weeks in which nothing proceeds because a decision has to be taken about something nobody knew was there.

None of which means renovation is the wrong decision. On a good lot with a good house it is frequently the right one, and it is often the only route to a position the zoning would no longer permit a new building to occupy — if the comparison is live for you, building a custom home and waterfront estate construction set out the other side of it. What it does mean is that the first money spent should go on investigation and on the threshold arithmetic, before a drawing is commissioned.

Where a whole-house renovation budget actually goes

  • Openings — design pressures, product approvals, bucks, two inspections
  • Electrical capacity and distribution, sized for a different decade
  • Mechanical distribution, condensate and equipment location
  • The substrate behind the finish, found when the finish comes off
  • Flood-resistant construction, if the threshold is crossed
  • Sealed architect or engineer drawings above the county’s cost trigger
  • Asbestos and lead procedures on a house of a certain age
  • The time cost of each discovery, which compounds

The threshold

Substantial improvement: the 50 per cent test

This is the single most consequential fact in renovating an older low-lying waterfront house, and it is the reason a sensible renovation budget is phased.

Flood ordinances in this market apply a substantial improvement test: where the cost of improvement reaches 50 per cent of the market value of the structure, the whole building must be brought into compliance with flood-resistant construction requirements. Not the altered part. The building. On a house whose finished floor sits below the current base flood elevation, that can mean elevating the structure or abandoning the lower level as habitable space, and it converts a renovation into a different category of project altogether.

Fort Lauderdale’s substantial improvement worksheet, in the revision dated 25 February 2024, is the clearest statement of the mechanics we have found and is worth reading in full before a scope is fixed. Several features of it decide budgets. Costs are cumulative over a rolling two-year period, so three separate permits inside two years are added together. The 50 per cent test is measured against the market value of the building and specifically not the land — which on this coast is the whole point, because the land is frequently worth several multiples of the house standing on it, and an owner who assumes the test runs against the purchase price will conclude they have far more headroom than they do.

The building value is taken from the property appraiser’s improvement value, adjusted, or from an appraisal the city will accept. The cost side includes labour, materials, overhead and profit — so it is the full cost of the work and not a materials figure. Minimum corrections required to cure code violations are excluded from the calculation, and historic structures that retain their designation are excluded as well. A worksheet is triggered at a 40 per cent screening ratio, which means a project that looks comfortably clear of the threshold on the owner’s arithmetic will still be examined before it is let through.

Two warnings. First, look-back periods vary by city, so Fort Lauderdale’s rolling two years is not a universal figure and the number that applies is your own jurisdiction’s. Ask, in writing, early. Second, phasing a project to sit under the threshold is an ordinary and legitimate planning decision; splitting a single project into artificial parts to evade the threshold is not, and it is a risk rather than a strategy. The cumulative rule exists precisely because the pattern is well known, and the consequence of being found on the wrong side of it years later is not a fine, it is a building that cannot be certified.

The approach that works is dull. Establish the building’s market value for the purpose of the test before anything is designed, work out the cost headroom at 50 per cent and at the 40 per cent screening ratio, then design to that headroom — or decide deliberately to cross it, with the elevation work priced in from the start. The expensive version is to design first, discover the ratio at permit, and dismantle a resolved scheme to get back under a number.

How the 50 per cent test is actually computed

  • The denominator is the market value of the building, not the land
  • Building value from the property appraiser’s improvement value, adjusted
  • Or from an appraisal the city approves

Openings

Windows and doors: the retrofit rules in detail

Replacing the openings on an older estate is the largest single technical exercise in most whole-house renovations here, and the procedure is well documented.

  1. Establish the design pressure for every opening separatelyThe Broward County Board of Rules and Appeals Formal Interpretation #24 governs how this is done in the county. For buildings up to 30 feet in mean roof height, the submission is a per-opening design pressure schedule — each window and door listed with the positive and negative pressure it must resist, not one blanket figure for the house. There are three permitted ways to establish those pressures, and your engineer or the design professional preparing the schedule will choose among them.
  2. Above 30 feet, the route changesFor buildings above 30 feet in mean roof height, a sealed site-specific design is required rather than a schedule. On a three-storey estate this is the ordinary case rather than the exception, and it is the item most often missing from an early budget, because an owner replacing windows does not expect to be commissioning engineering.
  3. Specify products that carry statewide approvalEvery unit needs documented approval for the pressures it is being asked to resist. Section 553.842(4) of the Florida Statutes is useful here and not widely known among owners: where a product carries statewide approval, a local official may not demand further testing of it. That is a real protection against a plan reviewer asking for work that has already been done, and it is worth having the citation to hand.
  4. Plan for two inspections, not oneBroward’s section 110.3 provides for inspection at the bucks — that is, the framing and attachment substrate, before the unit goes in — and again before concealment, before anything is covered over. Both are hold points. A programme that assumes one inspection at the end will lose a fortnight to the first one it misses, and on a house with forty openings the inspection sequence is a scheduling problem in its own right.
  5. Keep the work with a licensed contractorWindow and door installation is one of the voluntary state specialty categories under section 489.113(6), which means the state does not require that particular certification — and a voluntary licence is not a required one. That is a narrow point about certification, and it does not make opening replacement a job to hand to anybody. The permit, the engineering and the attachment to structure put window and door replacement with a licensed contractor.
  6. Expect the structure around the opening to be the real costOn an older house the existing opening rarely has the substrate a current-pressure unit needs to attach to. The unit price is the visible number; the structural remediation around forty openings is frequently the larger one, and it is almost never in an owner’s first estimate because it cannot be seen until the old frame is out.

Permits

The permit triggers that catch estate renovations

Four local provisions do most of the damage to estate renovation budgets and schedules in this market, and none of them is obscure.

ProvisionWhat it does to the project
Broward 107.3.4.0.2Sealed architect or engineer drawings are required for single-family work valued at $30,000 or more. On an estate renovation this is met immediately, which means the design professional is not optional and the fee belongs in the first version of the budget rather than the third.
Broward 109.4Where work starts before the permit is issued, a penalty of up to 100 per cent of the permit fee may be imposed. On a large project the permit fee is a significant figure, so doubling it is a significant penalty, and it is entirely avoidable by not starting.
Notice of commencementFort Lauderdale’s checklist requires a notice of commencement above $5,000. It is recorded before work begins, not filed afterwards, and it interacts with lien rights, so it is worth getting right rather than treating as a formality.
Broward 105.2The local administrative provisions exempt only maintenance or repairs valued at $1,500 or less. There is no published exemption list here for painting, flooring or cabinet work, so on a project of estate scale the working assumption is that a permit is required and the question is which one.
Substantial improvement worksheetTriggered at a 40 per cent screening ratio against building market value. It is not a permit in itself, but it is a gate the permit passes through, and a project that has not prepared for it will discover it at the counter.

The statutory review clocks, the five-day completeness rule, the fee reduction when a jurisdiction runs late and the changes House Bill 803 made this year are all set out on our permit calendar page. On a renovation it is worth noting that the new single-family permit exemption in that bill reaches almost nothing on this coast: it excludes electrical, plumbing, structural, mechanical and gas work, and it excludes any property partly or wholly within a flood hazard area, which covers most of what is waterfront here.

Hazards

The two things an older house contains that nobody budgeted for

Asbestos and lead are procedural rather than dramatic, and both of them are schedule items before they are cost items.

Asbestos first. Demolition and renovation of regulated facilities fall under the asbestos NESHAP at 40 CFR Part 61 Subpart M, and the thresholds for the formal process are 160 square feet of regulated asbestos-containing material on surfaces or 260 linear feet on pipes. Where they are met, the route is a survey by a Florida-licensed asbestos consultant before anything is disturbed, and written notice to the regulator ten working days before work begins. Ten working days is a fortnight of real time, and it is a fortnight that was not in anybody’s programme.

For single-family houses the picture is federally more relaxed — residential buildings of four or fewer dwelling units are exempt from the federal rule — but the local paperwork position in Broward is genuinely unresolved and we are not going to pretend otherwise. Broward County appears to require an online statement of responsibilities for single-family work at no fee, while a Fort Lauderdale interior demolition checklist states that single-family work is exempt from the statement. Those two positions conflict. Confirm it with the county for your address before you file, rather than taking either answer from a website, including this one. For a condominium the analysis is different again, because a condominium building is a regulated facility and the four-or-fewer-unit exemption does not reach it — that is set out on our condominium renovation page.

Lead next. Any renovation disturbing painted surfaces in a house built before 1978 brings a federal layer with it, and the obligations attach to the firm doing the work rather than to the owner. It is a real constraint on how a job is set up, how surfaces are prepared and how waste is handled, and on a 1950s or 1960s house on this coast it applies to a great deal of what a renovation touches. We have written that up separately on our lead paint page rather than compress it here, because it deserves the detail.

The sequencing point ties both together. A survey commissioned before the scope is fixed tells you what the project contains. The same survey commissioned after the contract is signed tells you what the project is going to cost extra, at the point where every trade is already booked.

One final observation that sits outside any regulation. The condition most likely to change an estate renovation budget on this coast is not asbestos or lead. It is the state of the structure at the wall head, at the openings and anywhere salt-laden air has reached embedded steel over four decades. That is found by opening the building up, and the honest advice to an owner is to open a few places deliberately and early rather than find out everywhere at once in week nine.

Investigation worth commissioning before the scope is fixed

  • Asbestos survey by a Florida-licensed consultant
  • Lead assessment on anything built before 1978
  • Substantial improvement arithmetic against building market value
  • Elevation certificate and current base flood elevation
  • Mean roof height, which decides the window retrofit route
  • Electrical service capacity against the intended specification
  • Exploratory openings at wall heads and around existing openings
  • An as-built survey, to establish what the current setbacks permit

Owner-builder

The owner-builder permit, and what the form actually says

On a project of this size the suggestion will be made to you at some point. It is worth knowing exactly what the document you would be signing commits you to.

Section 489.103(7) of the Florida Statutes exempts an owner who improves their own property, and the exemption is real. Its conditions are heavier than owners are generally told. The owner must personally appear and sign the application — not an assistant, not a family member, not the firm doing the work. The owner must supervise the work directly on site, and the statute states that the owner may not delegate the responsibility to directly supervise all work to anyone other than a licensed person. For an owner who travels, works elsewhere or lives in another state for part of the year, that condition cannot honestly be met.

Unlicensed workers on the job must be the owner’s own employees, with tax withholding and workers’ compensation. That is an employment obligation and an insurance obligation, and it is the part owners are least often told about, because it is the part that converts a permitting convenience into a payroll. If the property is sold or leased within one year of completion, the law raises a presumption that the improvement was undertaken for sale or lease, which is outside the exemption — a serious issue for anyone improving a property with a view to moving on.

And the statutory disclosure the owner signs says, in its own words, that unlicensed people sometimes use these permits to conceal their involvement in a project. The legislature put that sentence on the form because the pattern is well established, and an owner is entitled to know that the document in front of them describes the arrangement they are being offered.

The practical reading, on an estate renovation specifically. Where a scope requires a permit containing licensed work — and on a whole-house renovation here it almost always does, because the openings, the electrical capacity and the mechanical distribution are the work — the arrangement that holds is a licensed prime contractor with the permit in their own name. An owner who takes the permit personally has not saved a fee; they have taken on supervision, employment and insurance obligations in exchange for one, and they carry the presumption on resale for a year afterwards.

What the owner-builder exemption actually requires

  • The owner personally appears and signs
  • The owner supervises all work directly, on site
  • Supervision may not be delegated except to a licensed person
  • Unlicensed workers must be the owner’s own employees
  • With tax withholding and workers’ compensation
  • Sale or lease within one year raises a presumption of violation
  • The disclosure warns that the permits are used to conceal involvement

Our work

Reading the house first, and the finishes afterwards

Two things, both of which belong at the start of an estate renovation and are usually bought at the end.

The first is the reading. Before a scope is fixed we will walk the house and the parcel and tell you what we find: the substantial improvement arithmetic against the building’s market value rather than against what was paid for the property, the elevation position and the current base flood elevation, the mean roof height that decides which window retrofit route applies, the asbestos and lead questions the age of the house raises, and the town’s own seasonal working restrictions, which on this stretch of coast can move a start date by months and are almost never in an owner’s model. That review is free, deliberately, because it sits at the point in a project where information is worth the most and costs the least.

The second is the finish work: paint, flooring, cabinetry, tile and stone, plaster and stucco, caulking and sealant renewal, performed by our own people. On a house of this kind those are not trailing items. They are the surfaces an owner will look at every day for twenty years, they are where a renovation is ultimately judged, and on this coast they are also where the building meets salt air and ultraviolet. A specification written for a Midwestern house will not survive here, and the difference shows within three years rather than thirty.

Section 489.117(4)(a) of the Florida Statutes places those job scopes outside state contractor licensure — painting, flooring, cabinetry, interior remodeling where the project includes no task requiring a state licence, decorative stone, tile, marble, granite and terrazzo, plastering, stuccoing, caulking, pressure washing, driveway installation, handyman services, and canvas awning and ornamental iron installation — and provides that a local government may not require a state or local licence to obtain a permit for them. That provision removes a licensure barrier to applying; it does not remove the permit requirement, and in Broward the administrative provisions still frame applicant qualification around Chapter 489 at section 105.3.0.1.1, so the route is confirmed with the individual building department rather than assumed.

Everything reserved by section 489.113(3) — electrical, mechanical, plumbing, roofing, sheet metal, pool and air-conditioning work — goes to a licensee in that trade. On an estate renovation that is a large share of the project, and the single most useful thing an owner can do about it is to establish early who holds the prime contract and therefore who carries those subcontracts. Our page on who to hire first sets out the order of engagement and why the order itself costs money, and what it costs to build gives the published 2026 bands to sanity-check any number you are given.

And the reason the reading comes before the finishes. On almost every estate renovation we are asked to look at, the decisive number is not a price per square foot. It is the ratio between the cost of the scope and the market value of the building, and it is knowable on day one for the cost of an appraisal and an afternoon.

What we will do before a scope is fixed

  • Walk the house and report plainly on what we find
  • Establish the substantial improvement arithmetic against building value
  • Pull the elevation position and the current base flood elevation
  • Identify the mean roof height and the window retrofit route it triggers
  • Flag the asbestos and lead questions the age of the house raises
  • Map the town’s seasonal working restrictions onto the programme
  • Price the finish scope — paint, flooring, cabinetry, tile and stone, plaster, caulking

Questions

What owners ask

Is it cheaper to renovate an older waterfront house or to build new?

On a whole-house scope the renovation is frequently the more expensive and far less predictable of the two. A new house is drawn, engineered and priced before anybody commits. An existing house conceals its real condition behind finishes, carries openings that almost certainly do not meet current design pressures, has systems sized for a different decade, and may sit below the elevation the current flood map requires — and each of those emerges in sequence, at the moment it is most expensive to address. Renovation is still often the right decision, sometimes the only way to keep a position the zoning would no longer permit a new building to occupy. It should simply be entered with investigation money spent first.

What is the 50 per cent rule for renovating a house in a flood zone?

Where the cost of improvement reaches 50 per cent of the market value of the structure, the whole building must be brought into compliance with flood-resistant construction requirements, not just the part being altered. On a house whose finished floor sits below the current base flood elevation that can mean elevating the structure or giving up the lower level as habitable space. The critical detail is the denominator: the test runs against the market value of the building and specifically not the land, which on this coast is frequently worth several multiples of the house standing on it. An owner who measures against the purchase price will believe they have far more headroom than they have.

How is substantial improvement actually calculated?

Fort Lauderdale’s substantial improvement worksheet, in the revision dated 25 February 2024, sets out the mechanics. Costs are cumulative over a rolling two-year period, so separate permits inside that window are added together. Building value comes from the property appraiser’s improvement value, adjusted, or from an appraisal the city approves. Costs include labour, materials, overhead and profit. Minimum corrections to cure code violations are excluded, as are historic structures that retain their designation. A worksheet is triggered at a 40 per cent screening ratio, so a project that looks clear on the owner’s own arithmetic will still be examined. Look-back periods vary by city, so confirm yours in writing early.

Can I phase a renovation to stay under the substantial improvement threshold?

Phasing deliberately, with a long enough gap and genuinely separate scopes, is an ordinary planning decision and the reason most well-run estate renovation budgets here are phased at all. Splitting a single project into artificial parts to evade the threshold is a different thing: a risk rather than a strategy. The cumulative look-back rule exists precisely because the pattern is well known, and the consequence of being found on the wrong side of it some years later is not a fine but a building that cannot be certified.

What is required to replace windows and doors on an older house here?

In Broward County the Board of Rules and Appeals Formal Interpretation #24 governs the submission. For buildings up to 30 feet in mean roof height, a per-opening design pressure schedule is required — each window and door listed with the pressures it must resist, not one figure for the house — and there are three permitted methods for establishing those pressures. Above 30 feet a sealed site-specific design is required instead, which on a three-storey estate is the ordinary case. Section 110.3 provides for two inspections, at the bucks and again before concealment. And section 553.842(4) of the Florida Statutes is worth knowing: where a product carries statewide approval, a local official may not demand further testing of it.

Does asbestos or lead testing apply to a single-family estate renovation?

Lead, yes, on anything built before 1978 where the work disturbs painted surfaces — there is a federal layer and the obligations attach to the firm doing the work. Asbestos is less clear-cut for a house. Residential buildings of four or fewer dwelling units are exempt from the federal rule at 40 CFR Part 61 Subpart M, but the local paperwork position in Broward is unresolved: the county appears to require an online statement of responsibilities for single-family work at no fee, while a Fort Lauderdale interior demolition checklist states single-family work is exempt from it. Those conflict, so confirm it with the county for your address rather than relying on either answer. For a condominium the analysis differs, because a condominium building is a regulated facility and the four-or-fewer-unit exemption does not reach it.

What does an owner-builder permit require of the owner?

More than owners are generally told, which is why it is worth reading the form before signing it. Under section 489.103(7) of the Florida Statutes the owner must personally appear and sign, and must supervise all work directly on site — the statute says that responsibility may not be delegated to anyone other than a licensed person. Unlicensed workers must be the owner’s own employees, with tax withholding and workers’ compensation, which is an employment and insurance obligation rather than a construction one. Sale or lease within one year of completion raises a presumption that the permit was obtained in violation. And the statutory disclosure warns, in its own words, that unlicensed people use these permits to conceal their involvement in a project. On a whole-house renovation the arrangement that holds is a licensed prime contractor with the permit in their own name.
