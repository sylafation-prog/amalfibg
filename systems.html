Systems & controls

Lighting control, audio, network, cameras, gates and pool automation are the parts of a modern estate that fail without a sound. No water on the floor, no smell, no alarm — just an app that has quietly gone grey while the house sat empty. We install and service these systems, and we test every one of them on every visit, so that the failure is found in March rather than in November.

The coast

Why this equipment fails here and not in Connecticut

The same Lutron panel, the same Ubiquiti gear and the same camera heads will run for a decade in a dry northern climate. On the Intracoastal they are fighting salt, heat, humidity, lightning and a hurricane grid, and they are doing it in an empty building for five months of the year.

Salt in everything outdoors

Camera housings, gate operators, dock lighting, exterior access points and antennas all carry fasteners, gaskets and circuit boards. Salt aerosol works into every one of them. The failure is rarely dramatic — a camera that fogs behind the lens, a gate that loses one leaf, a dock fixture that corrodes at the gland and fills with water.

Heat in the rack

Most equipment rooms in South Florida are closets that were never conditioned. A rack running a gateway, a switch under load, an amplifier and a video head end will sit at a temperature that halves the life of everything inside it. Power supplies and hard drives go first, and they go in August.

Lightning and the grid

This is the most lightning-prone region in the United States, and the coast adds routine brownouts and utility switching. Surge takes gear through the power side, the coax, the network and the long runs out to gates, docks and cameras. One strike nearby removes a PoE switch port by port.

Humidity and standing air

A house closed up at seventy-eight degrees with nobody opening a door holds humidity that condenses inside enclosures and speaker grilles. In-ceiling drivers corrode. Touch panels delaminate. The same moisture that threatens the building threatens everything mounted in it.

Firmware, clouds and accounts

Half of a modern system depends on a service somewhere else. Firmware updates break integrations. Apps force re-authentication. Subscriptions lapse on a card that expired. A camera plan quietly stops recording and the app keeps showing a live picture, which is the most misleading failure of them all.

Nobody owns it

The AV company wired the house, the alarm company fitted the cameras, the pool builder set the automation and the electrician hung the gate operator. Each is excellent at their part and none of them is responsible for the whole. When something stops, the owner is the integrator, from twelve hundred miles away.

The failure nobody hears

Five months is long enough for all of it to go dark

A burst supply line announces itself. A network does not. This is the sequence we find most often on a first visit to a house that has been closed for a season.

Every one of those four events is trivial on its own, and every one of them is caught by somebody standing in the equipment room with a phone. That is the entire argument for putting these systems on the same calendar as the air conditioning and the glass: not because the technology is fragile, but because nothing in the house is checking on it and neither is anybody else.

The cameras are the part that matters most and fails most quietly. A recording plan that has lapsed, a drive that has filled, a channel that dropped off the recorder — in every case the app still shows a live picture when you open it, so there is nothing to notice. The absence is only discovered at the one moment it is needed, which is after something has happened.

So we do not take the app's word for it. On each visit a camera is covered and uncovered and the recording is played back from the timeline. A lighting scene is run. A zone of music is played. The gate is cycled from the intercom. The generator is exercised under load and the transfer witnessed. The access points are checked for client count rather than a green dot. All of it is photographed and dated, and it goes in the record with everything else about the house.

The equipment room

One cupboard decides whether the house works

Almost every system on this page runs through a single rack in a single closet. It is usually unlabelled, usually too hot, and usually the last place anyone looks. We photograph it, label it, cool it and leave a drawing on the inside of the door.

A rack is an honest document. Open one and you can read the history of a house — the original structured wiring, the alarm panel from the second owner, the AV head end from the renovation, the mesh kit somebody added when the Wi-Fi reached the dock badly. Four generations of equipment, three of them still powered, two of them doing nothing at all.

The first thing we do on a new property is empty it of what is dead, label every port and every cable at both ends, photograph the front and the back, and record the make, model, serial, firmware, licence and account for everything still in service. That record is the difference between a one-hour repair and a day of somebody tracing cable with a toner.

The second thing is heat. A closet at ninety-five degrees will take a switch or a power supply out in its third summer. A vent, a fan panel, a louvred door — the fix is usually trivial and almost never done. The third is the battery: an uninterruptible supply with a dead cell is worse than none, because it fails the first time it is asked, and nobody finds out for months.

Where a house is on the estate calendar, every one of those records sits in your portal beside the air handlers, the pool equipment and the irrigation drawing. One file for the building, kept current, and yours to take with you if you ever leave us.

Working with who you have

We are not asking you to replace your integrator

Most estates we walk into already have an audio-visual company, an alarm company and an electrician, and most of them are good at what they do. The gap is not competence. It is that nobody is at the house between the times they are called.

We hold the file

Every make, model, serial, firmware version, licence key, subscription and renewal date for every system in the house, in one place, kept current. Most owners cannot name the company that wired their network, let alone produce the password for the gateway.

We make the call

When something needs the specialist who installed it, we are the ones who notice, the ones who call, the ones who meet them at the gate and the ones who confirm the work was actually done. One number for you, and a photograph afterwards.

Common questions

Before you call

Do you install new systems or only maintain existing ones?

Both. We install and upgrade lighting control, audio, network, camera and access systems, and we hold the service and the seasonal programme afterwards. Work that requires a licensed trade is performed by licensed, insured contractors we direct, under our drawing and on our statement.

Our Wi-Fi is fine in the house but useless at the dock and the guest house.

Almost universally the same cause: a consumer router at one end of a long concrete-and-impact-glass building, with a mesh node added later that is talking back over the same congested band. The fix is a survey, a wired access point where the far coverage is needed, and a gateway that hands out addresses the devices keep. Coral-filled block and large-format glazing attenuate signal far more than most people expect, and no amount of mesh will push through them.

Can you take over a system somebody else installed?

Yes, and it is most of what we do. We start with an audit: everything in the rack identified, every port labelled, accounts and licences located, coverage measured and the whole thing photographed. You receive the record whether or not you go further with us.

Nobody has the password for our system. Is that recoverable?

Usually. Lighting panels, gateways and recorders can generally be recovered or re-adopted with physical access to the hardware, which is one of the reasons we insist on documenting the equipment room before anything else. Cloud accounts tied to an email nobody can reach are harder, and occasionally the honest answer is a fresh account and a day of re-provisioning.

How do we know the cameras are actually recording?

Because we play it back. A live picture in the app proves only that the camera has power and a connection — it says nothing about whether anything is being written to storage, how many days are retained, or whether the plan lapsed in July. On every visit a camera is covered and uncovered, the clip is found on the timeline, and the remaining storage and plan status are recorded with the date.

Is there anything that protects this equipment from lightning?

Nothing makes it immune, but the difference between a protected house and an unprotected one after a nearby strike is substantial. Whole-house surge protection at the panel, point-of-use protection at the rack, surge devices on the long runs out to gates, docks and cameras, and correct bonding and grounding of coax and network cabling. We check those on every visit, because a surge device that has already taken a hit looks identical to one that has not, unless you read the indicator.

Will you work with our existing AV or alarm company?

Yes, and we prefer to. They know the system they built. We keep the record, watch it between their visits, do the ordinary work and call them when the house needs what only they can do. Nobody is replaced and nobody is cut out.

Which areas do you cover?

The same nine counties as the rest of the firm: Palm Beach, Broward, Miami-Dade and Monroe on the Atlantic, and Manatee, Sarasota, Charlotte, Lee and Collier on the Gulf. The heaviest work is on the waterfront in Fort Lauderdale, Boca Raton, West Palm Beach and Naples. Response targets by county are on the coverage page.
