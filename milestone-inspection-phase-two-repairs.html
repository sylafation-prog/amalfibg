Condominiums · After the report

The report says substantial structural deterioration. Now what?

Phase one is a reading of condition. Phase two is what happens when that reading is unfavourable, and it carries an obligation that phase one does not: repairs must be scheduled or commenced within a fixed period, and where they are not, the enforcement agency must determine whether the building is unsafe for human occupancy. This page is about the twelve months after the report arrives.

Request a facade survey The two obligations explained

The threshold

What “substantial structural deterioration” actually means

The phrase is defined in the statute, and the definition is narrower than most boards fear and narrower than some reports imply.

The statute, as amended by HB 913 (ch. 2025-175, effective 1 July 2025 and the last substantive change to this regime), defines substantial structural deterioration as substantial structural distress or substantial structural weakness that negatively affects a building’s general structural condition and integrity. That is the test. It is a question about the structure, not about appearance.

The statute then adds a sentence that matters a great deal and is routinely overlooked. The term does not include surface imperfections such as cracks, distortion, sagging, deflection, misalignment, signs of leakage, or peeling of finishes unless the licensed engineer or architect performing the phase one inspection determines that such surface imperfections are a sign of substantial structural deterioration.

So a cracked balcony soffit is not automatically a phase two trigger. A spalled column edge is not automatically a phase two trigger. What makes either one a trigger is the inspecting professional’s determination that it signals substantial structural distress or weakness. That determination is a professional judgement and it must be made and recorded.

The practical consequence for a board reading a phase one report is a specific question worth asking the engineer in writing: for each noted condition, does the report find that it signals substantial structural deterioration within the meaning of the statute, or is it recorded as a maintenance item? The answer determines whether a 365-day repair obligation attaches, and the two things are often listed in the same table without the distinction being drawn.

This is not an argument for minimising findings. A board that talks its engineer out of a finding has bought itself a much worse problem. It is an argument for a report whose categories are legible, because the obligations that follow are different and the budget consequences are very different.

Where the engineer does find substantial structural deterioration, phase two begins, and the statute directs that it may involve destructive or non-destructive testing at the inspector’s direction — conducted, where practicable, in locations that are both representative of the structure and the least disruptive and most easily repaired. That last clause is worth quoting to anyone proposing to open up a building comprehensively when a representative sample would do.

The clocks

Every date that follows, in order

There are more deadlines than most boards realise, and several of them run from events the board does not control.

The 365-day figure is a ceiling, not a standard. The statute requires each county commission or municipal governing body to adopt an ordinance providing a timeframe within which repairs must be scheduled or commenced, and that timeframe must be no later than 365 days from the agency’s receipt of the phase two report. A local ordinance may require sooner, and some do. The first call a board should make is to the local enforcement agency to establish which period applies to this building.

Note also what the obligation is: repairs scheduled or commenced. Not completed. A board that has signed a contract, has a start date and has a sequence is in a different position from a board that has three bids and a committee. The statute asks for commitment, evidenced.

The consequence of not producing that evidence is the sentence boards should read twice. If the owner of the building fails to submit proof that repairs have been scheduled or have begun, the local enforcement agency must review the building and determine whether it is unsafe for human occupancy. That is the mechanism by which this becomes a question of whether people can live in their homes, and it is why the first month after a phase two report is not a month to spend forming a subcommittee.

One more clock, easy to miss because it runs from the association’s own receipt rather than from anything the agency does: within forty-five days of receiving the inspection report, the inspector-prepared summary must go to every unit owner regardless of what it says, be posted conspicuously on the property, and — where the association is required to maintain a website or application — be published there in full alongside the summary. A clean phase one does not switch this obligation off.

Boards sometimes ask whether the forty-five day distribution can wait until there is a plan to distribute alongside it. It cannot, and the better answer is to prepare the plain explanation in parallel so that both arrive together: what was found, what the statute requires, what the sequence is and when the next decision point falls. The summary going out without context is what generates three months of owner correspondence.

One date is close enough to act on this month. An association whose milestone inspection is due by 31 December 2026 may have the structural integrity reserve study performed alongside it — but in no event later than 31 December 2026. That is a hard outside date, and it is about five weeks away. Where both are outstanding, commissioning them together is cheaper than commissioning them separately, and a milestone inspection completed within the past five years can substitute for the visual portion of the study. Enforcement agencies may also prescribe timelines and penalties for compliance with the inspection requirement itself, and local governments set those locally. Where a building has missed a deadline already, the posture is different and counsel should be involved early rather than late.

The first month

Eight things to do before the next board meeting

This is the sequence we see work. It is ordinary project management, done early, in a situation where almost everything that goes wrong goes wrong because it was done late.

  1. Establish the actual deadlineCall the local enforcement agency and ask two things: the date they received the phase two report, and the period their ordinance allows for repairs to be scheduled or commenced. Write both down. Everything else is scheduled backwards from that date, and the board should not be working from an assumption of 365 days if the ordinance says 180.
  2. Separate the structural from the restAsk the engineer to confirm, in writing and item by item, which findings constitute substantial structural deterioration within the statutory meaning and which are maintenance. The first group carries the clock. The second group is budget, and it belongs in the reserve study. Mixing them inflates the urgent scope and the urgent cost.
  3. Send the disclosure question to every bidderOne email, in writing, to each firm you will consider: do you have any direct or indirect interest in the firm that performed our inspection, and does any relative of yours? Keep the answers. Both the milestone statute and the reserve study statute require this disclosure, and an undisclosed relationship makes the resulting contract voidable — which protects you but only if you asked.
  4. Get the scope quantified before you get pricesA price against “repair spalling as noted” is not a price. Before tendering, have the work quantified: square feet of concrete repair by elevation and location, linear feet of sealant, square feet of coating, counts of balcony edges and railing posts, each item photographed and referenced to the engineer’s drawing. Three bids against a quantified scope can be compared. Three bids against a narrative cannot.
  5. Decide the engineering relationship deliberatelyThe engineer who found the deterioration may design the repair, administer the contract, or both — or a different firm may. Each arrangement has a case for it. What a board should not do is drift into one of them by default and discover at the end that nobody independent ever reviewed the work that was done. Decide, minute the decision, and tell the owners.
  6. Settle the funding route in parallelDo not wait for final prices to start on the money. Statutory reserves may be funded by regular assessments, special assessments, lines of credit or loans, and special assessments, credit lines and loans each require a majority of the total voting interests. A vote takes notice, a meeting and time. Begin that process while the scope is being quantified, not after.
  7. Write the owner communication before you need itWithin forty-five days of receiving the report, the summary goes to every owner regardless of findings. Owners will then ask what it means, what it costs and whether they can sell. A board that has prepared a plain two-page explanation — what was found, what the law requires, what the sequence is, when the next decision is — spends the following months on the work. A board that has not spends them on the owners.
  8. Phase deliberately, if you must phaseWhere the structural items and the envelope items can be sequenced across budget years, say so explicitly, in a document, with which elevations are in which year and why. Phasing driven by a plan is defensible to owners, to lenders and to the agency. Phasing driven by running out of money is not, and it is visibly different.

Scope boundary

Who does what, and which licence pulls the permit

Structural repair and envelope restoration arrive in the same report and are routinely bid as one package. They are not the same work and they are not performed under the same authority.

The distinction is not cosmetic and it is not ours. Florida’s contracting statute defines a contractor as the person responsible for a project, whether the work is performed by that person or by others under their contract. A firm that holds no structural licence cannot lawfully take the structural scope and subcontract it out, and a board that permits that arrangement has a contract that may be unenforceable under §489.128 and an insurance position that may not survive examination.

So the question to ask every bidder on a milestone-driven repair is narrow and answerable: which licence will each permitted portion be pulled under, and may we see it? A bidder who answers clearly and produces the licence has told you something useful. A bidder who explains that it is all handled has told you something more useful still.

On projects where the structure and the envelope both need work, the arrangement that avoids this entirely is the association contracting the licensed structural party directly, with the envelope work contracted separately and sequenced behind it. It is marginally more administration for the manager. It removes a category of risk from the board.

There is one further licensing wrinkle specific to tall buildings. Among the few carve-outs the Legislature preserved from its preemption of local licensing is rooftop painting, coating and cleaning above three storeys, which a municipality may still require a local licence for if it did so before January 2021. On a mid-rise or high-rise condominium that is a question to settle before mobilising, not after.

Structural and permitted scope

  • Engineer-directed, performed by a contractor licensed in the trade and permitted in that contractor’s name
  • On a condominium above three storeys this requires a certified general contractor — a building contractor licence is limited to structures of three storeys or fewer and does not reach it

Envelope and finish scope

We do not perform milestone inspections, we do not perform structural repair, and we do not hold ourselves out as general contractors. What we do is the envelope: the survey, the specification, and the waterproofing, sealant, coating and finish work that follows. Where a project needs more than that, the licensed party contracts with the association directly and we sequence behind them.

What the state’s own data shows

1,575 phase two inspections. 903 repair permits.

In July 2026 OPPAGA published the first statewide milestone inspection data. One pair of numbers in it should concentrate the mind of every board holding a phase two report.

Read the highlighted row against the one above it. Across two years, 1,575 phase two inspections were completed and 903 repair permit applications were filed. Phase two is only reached where phase one found substantial structural deterioration, and substantial structural deterioration is what the repair obligation attaches to. On the face of it, a substantial number of buildings that reached phase two have not yet pulled a permit.

There are innocent explanations for part of the gap. Some repairs need no permit. Some phase two reports land late in a year and the permit follows in the next. Reporting is incomplete, which we come to below. But the gap is large, it runs in the same direction in both years, and the honest reading is that a meaningful number of associations have a finding and no permitted repair.

The figure boards ask about next is cost, and the data gives two averages for reported repair permit values: $337,229 and $496,236, within a range running from under $1,000 to $30 million. Treat those as orientation and nothing more — they mix balcony sealant against full structural reconstruction, and the distribution behind a range that wide tells you very little about any particular building.

The extensions row is the one with a practical lesson. 1,587 extensions were granted across the two years against 8,736 completed phase one inspections, and the report notes they were concentrated in coastal areas. Extensions exist and are obtainable. They are not obtained by a board that approaches the agency a fortnight before the deadline with nothing to show, and they are obtained by a board that approaches early with an engineer engaged, a scope being quantified and a funding vote scheduled.

Limits, stated plainly, because they matter to how much weight the figures carry. Only 71 per cent of jurisdictions reported for 2024 and 64 per cent for 2025 — 277 and 250 of 389. Several municipalities in Broward, Miami-Dade and Palm Beach did not report for 2025. The reporting form does not restrict entries to condominium and cooperative buildings, so some other building types are in the figures; it does not clearly handle unincorporated against municipal jurisdictions; and some officials filed against the wrong year. Neither “unsafe” nor “uninhabitable” is defined in the statute, and officials applied their own definitions — which is why the 54 figure should be read as an indication rather than a count. Source: OPPAGA, Milestone Inspection Reporting Data 2024 and 2025, Report 26-04, July 2026.

Marketability

Why the file matters as much as the repair

Lenders, insurers and buyers now ask for this documentation, and a building that cannot produce it has a problem before any enforcement officer is involved.

Since the inspection and reserve requirements took effect, the documentation has become part of how a condominium unit is valued and financed. Lenders ask whether the milestone inspection has been completed and what it found. Insurers ask the same question at renewal. Buyers’ attorneys ask for the report, the summary, the reserve study and the minutes.

That changes what a board is producing. The repair is the obvious deliverable. The file is the one that gets read for the next decade: the engineer’s report, the summary as distributed, the quantified scope, the bids and the disclosure replies, the contract, the submittals and product approvals, the progress records, the completion documentation and the warranty.

Our part of that file is specific and we produce it as a matter of course: the survey as quantified, the specification as issued, the products as approved, the work as installed by elevation and date, and photographs before, during and after. It goes to the manager in a form that can be handed to a lender, an insurer or the next reserve study without being reassembled.

It is unglamorous and it is the thing boards thank us for two years later, usually at a refinancing.

Questions

What owners ask

What triggers a phase two milestone inspection?

Phase two is required only where the phase one inspection identifies substantial structural deterioration. Fla. Stat. 553.899 defines that as substantial structural distress or substantial structural weakness negatively affecting the building's general structural condition and integrity, and expressly excludes surface imperfections — cracks, distortion, sagging, deflection, misalignment, signs of leakage, peeling finishes — unless the inspecting engineer or architect determines that those imperfections are a sign of substantial structural deterioration. Where phase one finds no substantial structural deterioration, phase two is not required.

How long do we have to start repairs after a phase two report?

Each county commission or municipal governing body must adopt an ordinance setting the period within which repairs must be scheduled or commenced after the local enforcement agency receives the phase two report, and that period must be no later than 365 days. A local ordinance may require sooner, so the operative deadline is the local one. The obligation is to have repairs scheduled or commenced, not completed. Where proof of scheduled or commenced repairs is not submitted, the enforcement agency must review the building and determine whether it is unsafe for human occupancy.

Is phase two always destructive testing?

No. The statute permits destructive or non-destructive testing at the inspector's direction, and provides that where practicable, testing should be conducted in locations that are both representative of the structure and the least disruptive and most easily repaired. A comprehensive opening-up of a building is sometimes necessary and is often not. It is a reasonable question to put to the engineer where a scope of testing looks broader than the findings require.

Do we have to tell the owners if phase one is clean?

Yes. Within forty-five days of receiving the inspection report, the association must distribute a copy of the inspector-prepared summary to each unit owner regardless of the findings, post the summary conspicuously on the condominium or cooperative property, and — where the association is required to maintain a website or application — publish both the full report and the summary there. A clean report does not remove the distribution obligation.

Can the engineer who did the inspection also do the repair work?

Not without disclosure, and the disclosure obligations are statutory. An architect or engineer bidding to perform the inspection must disclose in writing any intent to bid on the repair work recommended by their own report. Separately, a design professional or contractor bidding on the recommended work may not have a direct or indirect interest in the firm that performed the inspection, nor be a relative of someone with such an interest, unless disclosed in writing. Where disclosure has not been made, the contract may be voided and terminated and the professional may face disciplinary action.

Does the structural repair contractor need a licence?

Yes, and this is the point boards most often get wrong. Structural concrete repair, roofing, window and door replacement, electrical, plumbing and mechanical work all require a licensed contractor pulling the permit in that contractor's name. Florida's contracting statute defines a contractor as the party responsible for the project whether the work is done by that party or by others under their contract, so an unlicensed firm cannot take the scope and subcontract it. Contracts by unlicensed contractors are unenforceable under Fla. Stat. 489.128. The envelope job scopes — painting, caulking, waterproofing, stone and decorative work — are placed outside state licensing by Fla. Stat. 489.117(4)(a).

Can we phase the repairs across budget years?

Often yes, for envelope and non-structural work, where the engineer agrees the sequencing does not worsen the condition and the association documents the plan. Structural items identified as substantial structural deterioration carry the repair clock and are not generally candidates for deferral. Where phasing is used, document which elevations fall in which year and why, so the plan is legible to owners, lenders and the enforcement agency. Phasing by plan and phasing by cash shortage look different in the file.

What should we have in the file when this is over?

The engineer's phase one and phase two reports; the inspector-prepared summary as distributed, with evidence of distribution and posting; the quantified repair scope; the bids and the written conflict-of-interest disclosures; the contracts; product approvals and submittals; progress records; completion documentation by elevation and date with photographs; and the warranty. Lenders, insurers, buyers' attorneys and the next reserve study will each ask for part of it, and assembling it afterwards from memory and invoices is considerably more expensive than keeping it as you go.
