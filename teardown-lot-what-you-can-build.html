Building here · The land

What you can actually build on the lot

People buy the land and then find out what fits on it. That order is expensive and it is avoidable, because almost everything that constrains the house is published and free to look up before you close. The lot does not tell you how big a house you can build. Six separate documents do, and they rarely agree with the estate agent.

The controls

Six things decide the house, and none of them is the architect

Each of these is set by somebody other than you, each is published, and together they describe the envelope your house has to fit inside before anyone draws a line.

Setbacks

How far the building must sit from each property line — front, rear and both sides, frequently different numbers, and on a waterfront lot a separate and usually larger setback from the water or the seawall. Setbacks are the control that most often makes a plan impossible, because they define the buildable rectangle rather than merely trimming it.

Lot coverage

The proportion of the lot the building footprint may occupy, and sometimes a second limit on total impervious area that sweeps in the drive, the terrace and the pool deck. A generous coverage allowance with a tight impervious limit is a common and unwelcome combination on a waterfront parcel.

Floor area ratio

Where it applies, a ratio of total floor area to lot area. It is the control that caps overall size regardless of how cleverly the footprint is arranged, and it is the one most often missed by people reasoning from coverage alone. Not every town uses it; the ones that do use it decisively.

Height

Usually measured in feet and sometimes in storeys, and the measuring point matters as much as the limit. Height is commonly measured from a datum tied to flood elevation or crown of road rather than from your finished floor, which means a house that has to sit higher for flood reasons may lose a storey at the top.

Flood elevation

Not a zoning control but it behaves like one. The required lowest habitable floor pushes the whole house up, and because height is capped from a fixed datum, every foot gained below is a foot lost above. This is the interaction that quietly removes the third floor from a design.

Review boards

Where a town has an architectural or historic review board, it has a say in massing, materials, roof form and sometimes colour. That is not a constraint you can read off a zoning table, and in the Town of Palm Beach it is the single largest influence on what the finished house looks like.

The trap

Non-conforming is not the same as permitted

The most expensive misunderstanding in teardown buying, and estate agents repeat it constantly in good faith.

The house standing on the lot may not comply with the current code. It was probably built decades ago under different rules, and it is allowed to remain as a legal non-conforming structure. That status attaches to the existing building, not to the land.

Knock it down and in most jurisdictions the protection goes with it. The replacement has to meet today’s setbacks, today’s coverage, today’s height and today’s flood elevation. A 1958 house sitting fifteen feet from the water on a lot where the current setback is twenty-five feet is not a precedent for your new house; it is a reason your new house will be smaller than the one you are looking at.

“But the house next door is bigger” is the sentence that follows, and the answer is usually that the house next door was permitted under a previous code, or obtained a variance, or was built before the overlay existed. None of those transfer to you.

There are nuances — some codes allow reconstruction within an existing footprint after a casualty, some allow a percentage of improvement before conformity is triggered, and a variance is sometimes achievable. All of them are jurisdiction-specific and all of them are questions for the town’s planning staff before you close, not after.

The practical move is simple and almost nobody does it: telephone the planning department, give them the parcel number, and ask what the replacement envelope would be. They will tell you. It is a public service, it takes one call, and it is free.

The second question for that same call is whether the lot is affected by any overlay — a historic district, a coastal construction control line, a design district, a conservation or scenic corridor. Overlays sit on top of the base zoning and they do not appear on a parcel listing.

Waterfront

What the water adds

A waterfront lot carries a second set of controls that an inland lot does not, administered by different agencies on different timetables.

The waterward setback

Measured from the seawall, the bulkhead line or the mean high water line depending on the jurisdiction, and typically larger than the side setbacks. On a shallow lot it can be the control that determines the house depth entirely.

The seawall

You inherit its condition, its height and its remaining life. Several municipalities now set minimum seawall elevations and require existing walls to be raised at the point of substantial redevelopment. That can be a seven-figure item and it is frequently discovered after closing.

The dock

A separate permit from a separate authority, often several — the municipality, the county, the state and in some cases the Army Corps. Existing docks are not automatically replaceable like for like, and submerged land leases, riparian lines and the neighbour’s sightlines all get involved.

The coastal construction control line

On the ocean side, building seaward of the CCCL requires state approval in addition to everything else, with its own engineering standards for foundations and its own timetable. It is not a formality and it is not quick.

Sea turtle lighting

Along the ocean, lighting ordinances restrict exterior and interior light visible from the beach during nesting season — in Highland Beach, all beachfront lighting off from dusk to dawn, 1 March to 31 October. It shapes the lighting design and the glazing treatment rather than being added at the end.

Mangroves and shoreline vegetation

Protected, and trimming or removal is regulated with real penalties. A shoreline that looks like an overgrown nuisance to a buyer may be a protected feature that cannot be cleared for the view.

The paperwork

Six documents, and where to get them

Everything above is knowable before you close. This is the list, in the order we pull it.

  1. The zoning designation and the code section behind itFrom the municipality’s online map or planning counter. The designation alone is not enough — you want the actual code section, which sets setbacks, coverage, any floor area ratio, height and the measuring datum. Read it; it is usually two pages and it decides the project.
  2. The surveyA current boundary and topographic survey with elevations, not the one from the last sale. It shows the real dimensions, the easements, the existing elevations and where the seawall actually sits relative to the property line, which is not always where everyone assumes.
  3. The FEMA flood map and the elevation certificateThe flood zone and base flood elevation for the parcel, plus any local freeboard requirement above it. Broward County adopted a Future Conditions 100-Year Flood Elevation map in 2021 that is used to set minimum habitable floor elevations, so the governing number may be higher than the FEMA figure.
  4. The overlaysHistoric district, coastal construction control line, design or scenic overlay, conservation district. Ask the planning department directly rather than relying on a map layer, because overlays are the thing most often missed.
  5. The association documentsWhere there is a homeowners’ association or a club, its architectural committee may impose limits tighter than the town’s, and it runs on no statutory clock. Get the declaration and the architectural guidelines before closing, not during design.
  6. The permit historyPull the permit record for the existing structure. It tells you what was approved, what was not, whether there are open permits — which become your problem at closing — and whether previous owners obtained variances that may or may not survive demolition.

The order

How to use all of this before you close

The sequence matters more than the diligence, because the answers are cheap and the land is not.

Pull the zoning section and the survey first, and sketch the buildable rectangle: the lot minus every setback. Most of the time this single step tells you whether the house in your head is possible, and it takes twenty minutes.

Then check the flood requirement and work out how high the finished floor has to sit. Subtract that from the height limit, measured from whatever datum the town uses rather than from your floor. What remains is your vertical envelope, and it is where third storeys disappear.

Then establish whether a review board is involved. If it is, the question is not only what you may build but how long approval takes and how much the board will shape it. In some towns that process adds six months and materially changes the design.

Then telephone the planning department with the parcel number and ask what the replacement envelope would be if the existing structure came down. Planning staff answer this question daily and they are generally straightforward about it.

Only then does an architect become useful, because now they are designing inside a known envelope rather than discovering it at the first zoning review — which is the single most common way owners pay for a design twice.

Twenty minutes, before you offer

  • Lot dimensions minus every setback equals the buildable rectangle
  • Required finished floor elevation, from the governing flood number
  • Height limit, and the datum it is measured from
  • Coverage and impervious limits, and whether a floor area ratio applies
  • Whether a review board or an overlay is involved
  • Whether the town restricts construction seasonally
  • The condition and elevation of the seawall
  • Any open permits on the existing structure

Questions

What owners ask

Can I rebuild the same size house that is there now?

Frequently not. The existing house may be a legal non-conforming structure, built under an older code and permitted to remain as it is. That status generally attaches to the building, so once it is demolished the replacement must meet current setbacks, coverage, height and flood elevation. A 1950s house close to the water is not a precedent for your new one. There are exceptions in some codes — reconstruction within an existing footprint, thresholds before conformity is triggered — and they are jurisdiction-specific. One call to the planning department with the parcel number settles it.

How do I find out the setbacks for a specific lot?

Find the zoning designation on the municipality's online map, then read the actual code section for that designation rather than relying on a summary. It will give front, rear and side setbacks, any waterward setback, lot coverage, height and the datum height is measured from. It is usually a page or two. If anything is ambiguous, the planning counter will interpret it for you over the telephone, and their interpretation is the one that matters.

Why would flood elevation cost me a storey?

Because height is usually capped from a fixed datum such as crown of road, not from your finished floor. If the required lowest habitable floor is several feet above grade, every foot the house gains at the bottom comes off the top. On a lot with a tight height limit and a high flood requirement, that interaction is what removes a third floor from a design, and it is better discovered before you buy than at the first zoning review.

What is an overlay and why does it matter?

A set of additional rules sitting on top of the base zoning for a defined area — a historic district, a coastal construction control line, a design or scenic corridor, a conservation district. Overlays can control massing, materials, roof form, lighting and demolition, and they rarely appear on a parcel listing or a real estate site. Ask the planning department directly whether the lot carries one, because it is the item most often missed in diligence.

Do I inherit problems with the seawall?

Yes, and it can be a very large number. You take the wall in its condition, with its remaining life and its current elevation. Several municipalities now set minimum seawall elevations and require existing walls to be brought up at substantial redevelopment, which can turn into a seven-figure item on a long waterfront. Have the wall inspected before closing by someone qualified to assess it, and ask the town what elevation it would require if you redevelop.

Can I replace the existing dock?

Not automatically, and not always like for like. Dock work typically needs approval from the municipality and the county, often the state, and in some cases the Army Corps of Engineers, each on its own timetable. Riparian rights lines, submerged land leases, neighbours' sightlines and seagrass surveys can all be involved. An existing dock is evidence that a dock was once permitted, not that the same dock can be rebuilt today.

Should I hire the architect before or after I buy?

Buy the information before you buy the land, and hire the architect after. Zoning, survey, flood and overlay answers cost almost nothing and take a few days. An architect designing without them is designing in the dark, and the result is a scheme that has to be redrawn after the first zoning review — which is how owners pay for the same design twice. Give an architect a known envelope and they will do better work faster.

Will you look at a lot for me?

Yes, without charge. Send the parcel number or the address and we will come back with the zoning envelope, the flood requirement, whether a review board or overlay is involved, what the town's construction restrictions do to a programme, and what we can see of the seawall. It takes about an hour and it is the most useful thing anyone can hand someone buying land in a county they have not lived in. We are not bidding on the construction, which is precisely why the answer is straight.
